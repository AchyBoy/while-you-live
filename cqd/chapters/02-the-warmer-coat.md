# CQD

## Chapter 2: The Warmer Coat

The snow had gone to rain by Thursday, and the gravel road up to his house had gone to mud.

I took it slowly, in second gear, with the wipers going and the coat on the passenger seat beside me, folded, like a passenger who had not decided yet whether to come in. The field beyond the house was brown again, with white still lying in the furrows where the sun did not reach. The old blue pickup was where it always was. Smoke came out of the chimney and lay down flat across the roof in the wet.

He was at the window. He was always at the window. By the time I had parked he was on the porch, with the big umbrella open, coming down the steps one at a time with his free hand on the rail.

I put the coat on in the car, which is harder than it sounds, and got out.

He stopped at the bottom of the steps and looked at me. It was a short look, and a thorough one. He took in the hood folded into the collar, and the button hanging by its thread, and the moth hole in the sleeve that I had tried to hide by holding my bag on that arm. Then he smiled, the smile that took ten years off him.

"Good," he said. "That's a working coat."

"It's an old coat."

"It's both." He held the umbrella over me, though I was already wet. "You bought it for standing about outside in, and you stood about in it a great deal, and then one day you stopped. The pockets have gone out of shape from the notebooks, and the hem is worn on the right side where your bag hangs. You haven't worn it in a long time. It smelled of the closet when you got out of the car. It still does, a little." He turned toward the house. "I'm glad you brought it. Come in. The kettle's on."

* * *

The house was warm, and smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and something baking. There was a loaf on the kitchen counter in a paper bag from the town bakery, with a hand-lettered sign taped to the bag: *LAST WEEK. THANK YOU FOR 61 YEARS.*

"They're closing," I said.

"Saturday." He put the tea things on the tray. "The grandchildren didn't want it. I bought two loaves. I'll put one in the freezer and feel sorry for myself in June." He carried the tray through to the living room. "You'll have to tell me how your week was. Before we start."

He asked it every Thursday, and every Thursday I had given him the short answer, because I was being paid to listen and not to talk. This Thursday I sat down in the chair that did not eat people and gave him the long one.

I told him about the transcript from the sixth week, and the line I had put my pencil on. The woman on the second floor. I told him that I had called Petrakis, and that Petrakis had called her a name I would not write down, and then given me her real one. Eileen Dunleavy. A widow, whose husband had driven a bus. Thirty years in that building. Gone to her son's, out near Frederick.

"Frederick," he said. "You drove past it this morning."

"I know."

"Are you going to find her?"

"I'm going to try." I put my cup down. "I don't know what I expect her to tell me. It was six years ago. She heard everything in that building, every day, for thirty years. Why would she remember one Wednesday?"

He was quiet for a moment. He had the notepad on his knee and the pencil in his hand, but he was not drawing.

"Ms. Barnett," he said. "Petrakis stopped answering her."

"He got a second phone. He told me."

"So for some years, every time Mrs. Dunleavy heard something wrong through her ceiling, she picked up the phone and called the man whose job it was to fix it, and he didn't pick up." He tilted his head. "What does a woman do, when she knows something and nobody will listen to her?"

I did not answer at once, because I knew the answer, and I did not much like where it came from.

"She writes it down," I said.

"With the date," he said. "And the time. So that one day, when somebody finally asks, she can prove it." He looked at me over his cup. "You did exactly that. You have a box at the top of a closet that says so. I would be very surprised if Mrs. Dunleavy hasn't got one too."

I sat back. My own box was on the top shelf of my closet with a bag of winter hats on it, and I had not wanted to be told that it was a habit somebody could predict.

"There's something else," I said. "I didn't tell you last week. There wasn't time, after the card."

I told him about the movers. The small outfit from across the river, still in business, with the woman on the phone who said they kept their job sheets for seven years. The job booked by phone eleven days before my source disappeared, with a cash deposit. And the line for the new address, left blank, with a note written beside it in somebody's hand: *Customer to call with address.*

"He never called," I said.

He did not say anything for a long moment. The pencil lay still on the pad.

"Eleven days," he said at last. "He booked a truck to take everything he owned out of that apartment, eleven days before a Saturday. And he didn't give them an address."

"He told me he was going to move after the story ran. Somewhere Carrow couldn't find him."

"Then he may have known exactly where, and not wanted it on a moving company's form. You told me he never wrote a real name down where someone could find it. That would be like him." He turned the pencil over in his fingers. "Or he hadn't decided, which doesn't sound like a man who kept copies of everything. Or somebody else was finding him the place, and he was waiting to be told."

"Which do you think?"

"I think the first is the likeliest, and the last is the one that would matter." He looked toward the window. "If somebody was finding him a place, it was somebody he trusted enough to let them choose where he would live, when he was more frightened than he had ever been. That's a great deal of trust, Ms. Barnett. It would narrow things."

I wrote it down. I wrote, *No address: secrecy? undecided? somebody finding him a place?* and, under the last one, *Same person at the door?* and then I looked at what I had written and did not like it at all.

* * *

At ten past eleven I set the recorders on the little table between us, my phone and the small digital one that had been with me for eleven years, and pressed the buttons and said the date into them, and the time, and *Session nineteen.*

Then I looked at him and waited. It was his turn.

"April 1912," he said. "I was twelve."

I laughed. I did not mean to. It came out of me before I could stop it, a short surprised sound, and I saw it go across the room and land on him, and I was sorry at once.

He didn't laugh.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I didn't..."

"No. It's all right." He had not taken offense. He looked, if anything, a little amused at himself. "It's a strange thing to hear. It was a strange thing to be."

"I assumed," I said, and stopped, and tried again. "I suppose I'd assumed you'd still be Kit. He'd have been, what. Sixty-seven?"

"He would." He looked down at the pad. "Kit didn't see the new century. He had a good deal more life than he expected, after 1865, and a good deal less than he wanted. That's another Thursday." He turned the pencil around in his fingers, once. "There were others in between, and they're other Thursdays too. In the spring of 1912 I was very small. I was a boy on a ship, in a uniform with too many buttons, and I had been one for about three weeks when the part I want to tell you about began."

"A boy."

"A bellboy. On the Carpathia." He said the name carefully, as if it were something he had kept wrapped up a long time and was not sure would still be whole. "A Cunard ship. Not a famous one, not a fast one. Steady. She went back and forth between New York and the Mediterranean with whoever wanted to go, and that spring it was mostly American ladies going to see Italy, and families going home to Hungary. I carried their bags. I ran their messages. I said *sir* and *madam* about four hundred times a day."

"At twelve."

"At twelve. Boys went to sea younger than that, not so long before." He looked at me. "You're wondering what it's like."

I was. I had not been going to ask. I had been going to keep to the things that could be checked, as I always did, and leave the rest for him.

"Yes," I said.

"It's like being a grown man locked inside a cupboard," he said, "who can see out through the keyhole and is not allowed to speak above a whisper. Everything you know is still in there with you. Nobody on the outside wants any of it. A boy who tells a ship's officer what he ought to do gets a clip round the ear and an extra watch, and he deserves both." He smiled a little. "The first few weeks of that life, I was very angry. Then I remembered something I had learned a long time before, and had to learn again every time it happened."

"What?"

"When you're small, you don't push. You can't. There's nothing to push with." He set the pencil down on the pad. "So you make the best of what is in front of you. You find out what a small boy can do that a grown man can't. And it turns out, Ms. Barnett, that it's a great deal more than you would think."

"Such as?"

"Such as nobody ever looks at him." He picked the pencil up again, and began, without looking down, to draw. "A grown man walks into a room, and every person in it decides what he's there for. A boy with a tray walks into the same room, and nobody decides anything. He's furniture. He can stand by the door for ten minutes and hear everything said in that room, and if anyone notices him at all, they ask him for more hot water." The pencil moved. "I heard more on that ship, in four days, than I heard in a year at the National Hotel."

* * *

I was going to ask him about the four days. I had the question ready. I had looked up the dates on Sunday night, and I knew the Carpathia had picked up the survivors on a Monday morning and come into New York on the Thursday night, and I was going to ask him where he had been on the Sunday, before any of it.

But I was looking at the notepad.

I had seen him draw on it every Thursday for nineteen weeks. The first day, I had even looked properly. I remembered thinking it was the kind of thing people do on the phone. One shape, over and over, gone over so many times that the lead shone. I had noticed it, and noted it, and then it had become part of him, like the cardigan buttoned wrong and the tea gone cold. I had stopped seeing it. You stop seeing anything you see every week.

A five-pointed star, drawn in one long line, without the pencil ever leaving the paper. And in each of the five points, a tiny letter, crowded in. Greek, I had thought, that first day. Or something like it.

I had seen that star somewhere else.

I had seen it ten days ago, in the reading room of the Madison Building, under a lamp, in the left margin of a letter a hundred and sixty years old. Small, beside the last line, drawn in the same brown ink as everything else, as if the pen had moved there by itself while the writer was thinking. A star, the kind you draw without lifting the pen, crowded with some foreign letters. I had looked at it and thought it was vaguely familiar, the way a lot of things are, and I had not given it a second thought. I had been too busy reading the letter.

*I am one of those you spoke of.*

The folder was in my bag, on the floor by my chair. Six feet from his knee. Again.

"Ms. Barnett?"

I looked up. He was watching me, the pencil still, his head a little on one side.

"Sorry," I said. "Go on. Where were you, the Sunday night?"

He looked at me a moment longer. Then he looked at the window, at the rain, and began.

I did not hear the first thing he said. I had to get it off the tape that night, at the kitchen table, after Freda had gone to bed, with the photocopy laid out flat in front of me under the light and the drawing I had made of his star from memory beside it. I had drawn it four times before I was happy with it. I had never drawn it before. My hand went around the five points in one line, the way his did, the way the letter's did, as if it had been waiting to.

It was the same star.

I sat and looked at the two of them for a long time, and then I put on the headphones and pressed play, and listened to him begin, and to myself not saying anything at all.
