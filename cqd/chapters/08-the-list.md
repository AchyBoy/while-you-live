# CQD

## Chapter 8: The List

I called Kevin Dunleavy on Friday morning, after the school run, and he did not want to talk to me.

He was polite about it. He drove a delivery truck, he said, for a beverage company, and he was in the cab between stops with the engine running, and he did not know who I was or what I wanted with his mother, and his mother was eighty-one years old with a bad hip and did not need people calling her about things that happened six years ago.

"I understand," I said. "I'm sorry to have bothered you."

"What was it about, anyway?"

I told him. A man who had lived upstairs from his mother on Calloway Street, who went out one Wednesday and didn't come back. That I had been the reporter on a story he had helped with. That I thought his mother might have heard something that morning, through the ceiling.

There was a silence on the line, with an engine in it.

"Upstairs," he said. "The quiet guy. With the cat."

"Yes."

"Hang on," he said. And then, away from the phone, but not far enough: "Ma. *Ma.* It's about the guy upstairs. On Calloway. The quiet one."

There was a pause. Then a different voice, older, sharper, coming from somewhere on the far side of a kitchen, louder than the engine.

"Give me that phone."

* * *

Freda wanted to come.

I had not expected it. I told her on Friday night, over dinner, that I was driving out to Frederick in the morning to see the woman from the second floor, and I expected her to say *okay* and go back to her phone. Instead she put the phone face down on the table and said, "Can I come?"

"Why?"

"I'm the transcriber." She said it as if it were a job title, which I suppose it was. "I should meet the sources."

"She's not a source. She's an old lady who used to complain about the noise."

"That's literally what a source is," said Freda. "You told me that. When I was like nine."

So she came. She sat in the passenger seat with her feet on the dashboard, which I had stopped telling her not to do, and her headphones around her neck, and a bag of pretzels in her lap, and when we got to the bottom of our street and found 16th Street closed for a fun run, she didn't even look up.

"Go up Georgia," she said. "Cut across at Missouri. Then you can get back on."

I went up Georgia. We cut across at Missouri, and got back on.

"Since when do you know Georgia Avenue?"

"Since always." She ate a pretzel. "Maddie's sister drives that way."

Maddie's sister, I thought, had become a very generous girl this spring. I didn't say it. I drove.

* * *

The house was the one from the county website, a split-level at the end of a cul-de-sac on the east side of Frederick, with the basketball hoop over the garage and the newer wooden ramp built up over the front steps. Kevin Dunleavy opened the door before we had knocked. He was a big, tired man in his fifties with a delivery company's polo shirt and his father's bus driver's face, I imagined, patient and a little worn.

"She's been up since six," he said, in a low voice. "She's been waiting for you since six. I want you to know that."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry." He stood back to let us in. "She hasn't been this happy since the Orioles fired the manager."

Mrs. Eileen Dunleavy was in the living room in a wheelchair by the window, with a crocheted blanket over her knees and her white hair set, and a cardigan buttoned correctly, all the way to the top. She was very small. She had a face like a walnut and eyes like a bird's, and they went over me from my shoes to my hair before I was halfway across the carpet.

"You're the reporter," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"You're shorter than your picture." She looked at Freda. "Who's this?"

"My daughter. Freda."

"Hm." The bird's eyes went over Freda the same way, shoes to hair. "You've got your mother's mouth. You look like you've got your own mind, too. Good. Sit there." She pointed at the couch. "Kevin, get the girl a soda. Get the girl a soda and get Darren out of his room and make him say hello to somebody for once in his life."

Kevin went. Mrs. Dunleavy turned back to me.

"I read it," she said. "Your story. In the paper. That Sunday."

I didn't say anything. I hadn't expected that.

"I read all of it," she said. "I knew it was true the minute I read it. I knew it was him, too, even though you didn't put his name in. Carrow House. The documents. Of course it was him." She folded her small hands on the blanket. "He was a careful man. I could hear him being careful. Every night, ten o'clock, chair back, desk lamp off, two steps, bedroom door. Never once slammed a door in four years. Never had a party. Never had a girl up, never had a boy up. Never had anybody up." She looked at me sharply. "Until that Wednesday."

* * *

I had brought my notebook. I had brought the recorder, too, and I asked her if she minded, and she said she had been waiting six years for somebody to record her, and she was not going to be shy about it now.

So I asked her what I had come to ask. And she told me what Tom had said she would.

"I wrote it down," she said. "Of course I wrote it down. I wrote everything down. That man Petrakis stopped answering his phone years before, and after that what was I supposed to do? I wrote it down. Every bang, every pipe, every dog. With the time." She nodded toward the hall. "Kevin's got them in the garage. Kevin! The tub. The blue one."

Kevin brought the tub. It was a plastic storage bin, the kind you buy for sweaters, and it was full to the top with composition notebooks. The black and white marbled kind, the kind Freda used in third grade. There must have been sixty of them. Every one had a date range on the cover in neat ballpoint capitals.

"Six years," said Mrs. Dunleavy, "before that. And two after. Then I broke my hip and came here, and Kevin's house is quiet as a church, God help me." She pointed. "That one. Third from the left."

I found it. The dates on the cover took in that spring, and that summer. I turned the pages slowly, very slowly. They were full, every line of every page, in the same small, tidy, furious hand. *7:10 AM. Dry cleaner machine started. 20 MIN EARLY.* *11:45 PM. 4th floor. Television. Loud. Game show.* *2:15 PM. Dog. Alley. Barking 9 min.* Day after day after day. A building's whole life, through one woman's ceiling.

And then I came to the Wednesday.

I knew it before I read the date. The handwriting was different. Not messier. Smaller. Pressed down harder into the paper, as if she had known, while she was writing it, that it was something she would need.

*WED.*

*9:38 AM. Knock, upstairs (3rd fl). 3 knocks. Then 2 more.*

*9:39. Cat crying. He's shut her in.*

*9:40. His door. Man's voice, NOT HIS. Laughing. Door shut.*

*9:40 to 10:10. Talking. Couldn't hear words. Other one laughing a lot.*

*10:12. His door. TWO on stairs going DOWN. Slow. Heavy. Something banging the wall all the way down. New paint!!*

*10:14. Back door. Car in alley. Engine running. Exhaust coming in my kitchen window AGAIN.*

*10:21. Two back UP. Quick.*

*10:33. Two DOWN again. Slow. Heavy. Banging.*

*10:41. Car door. Car door. Car gone.*

*Called P. No answer. As usual.*

And then, on the next line, in the same hard small writing, as if she had gone back to it at the end of the day:

*He did not come back up.*

* * *

I read it three times. Then I looked up at her, and she was watching me with her bird's eyes, and her small hands folded on the blanket.

"He didn't come back up," I said.

"No."

"Not that day."

"Not that day. Not ever." She said it very plainly. "I'd have heard him. I heard everybody come up those stairs for thirty years. He had a step like nobody else's. Very light. Like he was apologizing to the stairs." She looked at the notebook in my hands. "He went down those stairs at twenty to eleven on that Wednesday morning, with whoever that was, carrying whatever that was, and two car doors shut in the alley, and that was the last time."

"You said two people on the stairs," I said. "How do you know one of them was him?"

"Because one of them was light, and one of them was click, click, click." She said it as if I were slow. "And after, it was quiet up there. Two days quiet. Nothing but that poor cat."

"Were you home all of those two days?"

"I'm always home," said Mrs. Dunleavy.

I wrote that down, and put a question mark after it, because I had been hearing people tell me *always* for twenty years, and I kept my hand over the page so she wouldn't see it.

"Did you tell the police?"

She laughed. It was not a nice laugh.

"The police came on the Friday. Petrakis let them in. One young officer, with a face like a boiled ham. I was standing right in my doorway on the second floor when they went up. Standing right there." She jerked her chin at the notebook. "Nobody asked me. He asked Petrakis. Petrakis told him about the rent, and the carpet. He wrote it all down. Then he went upstairs and looked around, and came down, and went away, and nobody ever asked me a single thing."

"You could have told them."

"I called the number on his card," she said. "Twice. They said they'd pass it on." She looked out of the window at the cul-de-sac. "Nobody called me back. Nobody ever calls me back."

* * *

Freda had been sitting on the couch through all of this with a can of soda in her hand, very still. Darren, the grandson, had come out of his room at his father's insistence, a long boy of about sixteen with headphones on, and said *hi* to the carpet, and sat on the other end of the couch looking at his phone. At some point the two of them had started talking, very quietly, about something on his screen. Now I saw that Freda had stopped, and was looking at the notebook in my lap.

"Mrs. Dunleavy," she said. "Did you ever see him? The other man?"

Mrs. Dunleavy turned and looked at her, and something in her face changed, and softened, a very little.

"No," she said. "I wish I had, sweetheart. My kitchen window looked on the alley, but by the time I got to it with this hip, the car was going. Big. Dark. That's all I saw." She paused. "But I heard him. All that half an hour. Through the ceiling. And going down the stairs, every step." She shook her head slowly. "He had hard heels on. Leather. You don't hear leather heels much anymore. Click, click, click, like a lady. And he laughed."

"What kind of laugh?"

Mrs. Dunleavy thought about it. She took her time.

"A big one," she said. "Too big. The kind of laugh a man has when he's used to people laughing with him." She looked back at me. "Your man upstairs didn't laugh. Not once, in four years. That's why I wrote it down. That's what was wrong with it. Somebody was in that apartment having a wonderful time."

* * *

Kevin let me photograph every page of that notebook, and then, when his mother insisted, let me take the notebook itself, on a promise to bring it back, which I wrote down and signed for her on a page of my own. She watched me do it with great satisfaction.

At the door, she held my hand in both of hers for a moment. Her hands were very cold and very strong.

"I knew he didn't run," she said. "I knew. A man who apologizes to the stairs doesn't run off and leave his cat." She let go. "You find out where that car went, Miss Barnett. And then you come back and tell me. You come back. You're the first person who ever did."

* * *

Freda didn't say anything until we were on the highway.

Then she said, "She was great."

"She was."

"She was so mad. Like, for six years. And she kept every single page." She was looking out of her window at the hills going by. "You're really good at this. You know that? I didn't know you were that good at it. You just sat there and let her talk."

"That's most of it. Letting people talk."

"That's what he does," said Freda. "Mr. Hale. That's exactly what he does to you."

I drove for a while.

"Mom," said Freda. "When it happened. The story, and all of it. When you lost your job." She was still looking out of the window. "Did Dad ever, like. Help? Or call, or anything?"

I kept my eyes on the road. It was a long, straight stretch, with nothing on it.

"Your father was in Phoenix," I said. "He sent you a card for your birthday that year. It came in March."

Her birthday was in January. Freda knew that. She didn't say anything. She didn't put her headphones on, either. She sat with her feet on the dashboard and looked out of the window at Maryland until we were home, and when we got out of the car she said she had homework, and went to her room.

The door didn't slam.

I sat at the kitchen table with Mrs. Dunleavy's notebook open in front of me and copied out that one page by hand, every line, the way I used to copy out a source's quotes on the night before a story ran, so that they would be in my own hand and I would know I had them right. Then, under it, I wrote:

*Knock 9:38. Pearl crying 9:39: shut away. Half an hour of talk, and the other one laughed.*

*Two heavy trips down. The boxes, the first time? What was the second?*

*Two people on the stairs, light step and leather heels. She says the light one was him. Two car doors. Nobody came back up, that she heard.*

And then the question that I was going to be asking, I already knew, for a very long time.

*Where did the car go?*
