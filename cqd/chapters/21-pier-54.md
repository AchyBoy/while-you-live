## Chapter 21: Pier 54

*April 18, 1912, night*

It was raining in New York. A cold, hard, driving rain, coming in off the harbor sideways, the kind that finds the gap between your collar and your neck however you stand.

I wasn't supposed to be on deck. A boy who has been discharged is nobody's business, and nobody's bell boy, and Lyle had told me to stay in the cabin with my bag and keep out from under people's feet until I was sent for in the morning. I stayed there until we passed the lightship. Then I went up. Nobody stopped me. Everybody on that ship who could stand was at a rail.

We came in through the Narrows in the dark, very slowly. And the harbor was full of boats.

Tugs, mostly. Dozens of them. Little steam tugs and launches and ferries, crowded right up against our sides, with their lamps lit and their whistles going, and every one of them packed with men in coats and hats, standing on the decks and the cabin roofs in the rain, shouting. Newspapermen. Every paper in New York had hired something that floated. They had megaphones, and they were shouting up at our rails through them, at anybody, at the passengers, at the crew, at the faces looking down.

*Were you on the Titanic? Is Astor alive? Did you see her go down? Fifty dollars for your story! A hundred! Jump down to us! We'll take you off!*

And in among the shouting there were flashes. White, sudden, blinding. Photographers on the tugs with flash powder, firing up at us in the rain, so that every few seconds the whole side of the ship and every face along the rail jumped out of the dark, white and staring, and then fell back into it.

The captain wouldn't let one of them aboard. Not one. He had said so on the wireless, all day, and he meant it. The tugs came alongside, and the men on them shouted and waved money and tried to throw lines up, and our seamen stood at the rails and threw the lines back. We went on up the river through all of it, very slowly, with the tugs following us like gulls after a trawler.

I thought of Mr. Hatch. I couldn't help it. *In New York they'll all have a story.* There they were. Every newspaper in the world, in the rain, shouting for exactly what he'd had in his notebook. And he was lying under a sheet in a cabin on C deck, and his notebook was ash in the stokehold, and his five pages were at the bottom of the Atlantic, and nobody on any of those tugs would ever know his name.

* * *

We didn't go to our own pier first. We went past it, up the river, to the White Star pier, where the Titanic should have come in on Wednesday morning.

And there, in the rain, under the lights, with ten thousand people watching from the pier and the street behind it in silence, they lowered the Titanic's boats.

They'd been hanging on our davits and stowed on our decks since Monday morning, all the ones we had been able to take aboard. Now our seamen swung them out, one after another, and lowered them down into the black water, empty. And men from the pier rowed them away, one after another, into the dark under the White Star shed, and they were gone.

The survivors watched from the rails. Nobody said anything. Fourteen hundred people at the rails, and the only sounds were the rain, and the creak of the falls, and the oars.

I was standing by the after deckhouse. A little way along the rail from me, I saw the young woman in the dark coat. She had the little boy on her hip, asleep, under her coat, out of the rain. She was watching the boats go down.

She watched one boat more carefully than the others. I saw her. As it went down past us on its falls, and touched the water, and swung there empty, and the man from the pier took up the oars and began to pull it away into the dark, she watched it all the way, until it went under the shed, and was gone.

I knew which boat. So did she. I won't tell you the number, Ms. Barnett. I said I wouldn't.

She didn't look at me.

* * *

Then we went back down the river to Pier 54, the Cunard pier, our own, and the gangway went down, and they began to go ashore.

You have seen the pictures, perhaps. Everybody has. They don't show it. The pier was a great long shed, lit up inside like a railway station, and it was packed, wall to wall, with people. Thirty thousand, the papers said the next day, or forty, in the shed and outside it and all along the street, in the rain. Families. Friends. Reporters. Doctors, and nurses, and ambulances backed up to the street door, and priests, and the police holding the crowds back with ropes. And the silence. When the first of them came down the gangway, the noise in that shed just stopped, all of it, and thirty thousand people stood under the lights in the rain and didn't make a sound.

Then somebody saw somebody, and screamed a name, and it broke, and after that it was all noise.

First class came off first. That was how it was done.

And among the first of them, in his good gray suit and his good gray hat and his shined black shoes, with his wrapped hand in his pocket and the bruise on his jaw gone yellow at the edges, was Mr. Ainsley.

He came down the gangway into the light, and the flashes went off all around him, white, white, white, and from the crowd behind the ropes a woman cried out his name, and he turned, and lifted his good hand, and smiled. A woman in furs came through the ropes, with two girls behind her, young ladies, his daughters, and the police let them through, because of who he was, and they ran to him and he held them, all three, there at the bottom of the gangway, under the lights, with the rain coming in under the roof of the shed. He held them a long time. I watched him do it. He held them well. He was a man who knew how to do that sort of thing so that people saw it, and he did it, and they saw it.

Then the reporters had him. They pushed in through the police with their notebooks, a dozen of them, shouting his name, and he let them. He stood with one arm round his wife and talked to them, in the flashes, in his warm, easy, carrying voice. I couldn't hear the words from the rail. I didn't need to. I read them the next morning, in four different papers.

How calm it had been. How orderly. How the men had stood back. How in his boat they had done everything they could, and kept up the ladies' spirits, and nobody had been turned away.

And at the end, one of the reporters said something, and he laughed. The big laugh. Right there at the bottom of the gangway, with his arm around his wife. And the reporters laughed with him.

* * *

He lived another thirty-one years.

I know because I read his obituary. He died in his own bed, in a big house, in 1943, with his daughters beside him. The obituary was half a column long, with a photograph. It listed his boards, and his clubs, and his charities, and his grandchildren. Near the end, it said that he had survived the sinking of the Titanic, and that in his lifeboat on that terrible night he had taken his turn at the tiller and kept up the courage of the women and children in his care.

I've read it a great many times, Ms. Barnett. Bertie cut it out of the paper, and lost it, the way you lose everything. I found it again in this life, in a library, and printed it. It's in a box upstairs.

Half a column for him, with a photograph. Three lines for Mr. Hatch. I never went looking for those. You found them for me.

* * *

They carried Mr. Hatch off at about eleven, after most of the living had gone. Two stewards, with a stretcher, under a sheet, down the crew gangway at the far end where the crowd had thinned. Nobody was waiting for him. There was a hearse from the company at the street door, and a man from the coroner's office with a clipboard who signed something, and that was all. His sister was in Ohio. She would hear by telegram, I supposed, in the morning, and not know who to ask about anything.

I stood at the rail and watched him go all the way to the street door. I don't think anybody else on that ship was watching.

* * *

She came down the gangway late. Most of second class had gone. She had the little boy on her hip still, asleep, and the girl by the hand, in her blanket coat, and the mother on the other side of her on the arm of a stewardess, walking very slowly, like an old woman, though she couldn't have been thirty-five. There was somebody waiting for them behind the ropes. An older couple, gray, the grandparents perhaps, who came forward with their arms out and their faces breaking. I saw the mother see them and stop on the gangway and put her hands over her face.

The young woman stopped too, a step behind, to let them have it.

And while she stood there, she turned her head and looked up. Along the side of the ship, along the rail, at all the faces still looking down. Quick and complete, as she always did. Until she found mine.

She looked at me for a long moment, up through the rain, from the gangway. I looked back.

Then she said something. I couldn't hear it. The shed was full of noise, and the rain was loud on the roof. I saw her lips move. I've thought about it since, for a very long time, and I've never been sure. I think it was a name. I think she was telling me her name.

Later, on the pier, on the Friday morning, when they put me ashore, a stewardess I didn't know came and found me where I was sitting on my bag by the street door, and gave me an envelope, and said a lady had left it with the purser for the bell boy Pike. There was nothing in it but two shillings, wrapped in a half sheet of paper. On the paper, in a clear, quick, educated hand, it said, *For Stan, with apologies. E. P.*

Eleanor Penhallow. That's what I've called her ever since. That's what I said I'd tell you, when it mattered.

I looked for that name, afterward, for years. On every list. The Titanic's, and the Carpathia's, and the purser's, and the inquiry's, and the newspapers'. There was no Penhallow on any of them. She had given the purser a different name at the gangway on the Monday morning, and that one is on the lists, and I'm not going to tell you what it was.

I gave Stan his two shillings. He cried, a little. He gave me back one.

* * *

The last I saw of her, she was going away through the crowd toward the street door, with the little boy asleep on her shoulder and the girl holding her coat, behind the mother and the grandparents. The crowd closed round them. Just before it did, she turned her head, once, and looked back up at the ship. Not at me. At all of it.

I could see her lips moving. She wasn't speaking.

She was humming.

I couldn't hear it. I didn't need to. I knew what it was.

Neither of us waved.

He stopped.

He had not drawn once, all morning. The notepad was on his knee, and the pencil on the notepad, and his hands were folded on top of them both, quite still.

"That's the end of the Carpathia," he said.

I didn't say anything. I left the recorders running.

"What happened to him?" I said. "To Bertie. After. A boy of twelve on a pier in New York, with no reference and a bag."

"He made the best of it," he said. "He was good at that. I'll tell you how, before we're done. Not today."

I stood up. I picked up the recorders and put them in my bag. I didn't stay for lunch. He didn't offer it. He got up when I did, and went ahead of me to the door, and held it open, and stood on the porch in his cardigan in the cold bright noon while I went down the steps.

At the bottom I heard him say, "Ms. Barnett."

I stopped. I didn't turn around.

"Tell her..." he said, and stopped.

"No," I said.

I got into the car. When I backed it round in the gravel to go, he was still on the porch, with one hand on the rail, looking at me. I didn't wave.

In the mirror, at the end of the gravel road, he was still there.
