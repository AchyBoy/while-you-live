## Chapter 16: The Governess

*April 18, 1912, morning*

I had read the pages twice, sitting on the floor in front of my locker. I put them back in the lid of the case, and shut it, and latched it, and put it at the back of the locker behind my spare jacket, and shut the locker door. Then I sat there a while longer.

I knew what I had to do with them. That was the easy part. They had to go to somebody who could do something. Not a bell boy. Somebody with gold on his sleeve.

The hard part was how.

I couldn't walk into the purser's office with a dead man's typewriter case and say, *Sir, I took this from the library, and inside it there's an account of a first-class gentleman beating a drowning steward off a lifeboat with an oar, and I believe he killed the man who wrote it.* I'd have been in irons before I got to the second sentence. A bell boy who takes a dead passenger's property is a thief. That's all he is. Whatever's in the property.

No. It had to be found. By the right man, in the right way, so that he found it himself and believed what he'd found. That's the trick, Ms. Barnett. It's always been the trick, when you're small. You don't hand a grown man the answer. You put it where he'll trip over it, and then you stand back and let him be clever.

I was still working out where to put it when Lyle put his head round the door.

"Pike. There you are. There's a lady in second class asking for you."

"For me, sir?"

"By name. God knows why." He looked at me as if I might have been stealing the spoons. "One of the survivors. Second class, up on the bridge deck. She says you were very kind to the children and she'd like a word, and some hot milk for the little one. Get the milk from Evans and go. And Pike." He narrowed his eyes. "Whatever she gives you, you give half to me."

* * *

It was an inside cabin, small, with two berths and a washstand and no porthole. Somebody from our own second class had given it up for them. The lower berth was empty, the blanket turned back, and on the washstand beside it stood the doctor's sleeping draught in its little bottle, the cork still in. I noticed that, the way you notice a picture hanging crooked, and then the girl looked up at me, and I forgot it. The little boy was asleep in the upper berth with his thumb in his mouth. The girl, the six-year-old, was sitting on the floor in the corner wrapped in a blanket coat that one of our ladies had sewn, drawing on the back of a menu card with a pencil.

The young woman in the dark coat was sitting on the only chair, by the door, with her hands in her lap. She was dressed now, properly, in a gray skirt and a white blouse that were not hers, too short in the sleeves, and her hair was up. She looked tired. She looked as if she had not slept at all. She looked, also, as if she had been waiting for me, and had known exactly how long I would take.

"Thank you," she said, and took the milk, and gave it to the little girl, and said something to her low, and the little girl went on drawing.

Then she turned the chair a little toward me, and looked at me, and didn't say anything.

I stood by the door with my cap in my hands, and didn't say anything either.

It went on for some time. The ship's whistle went, outside, muffled, through the steel. The little girl's pencil scratched.

"You were in the boot room this morning," she said at last. "At about half past four."

I didn't answer.

"You smelled of blacking at breakfast," she said. "I was at the second sitting. You poured the coffee at the first-class tables and then you came through ours with the cream. Your hands smelled of blacking, and you had it in the creases of your knuckles, and on your cuff. You carry shoes for the boots in the mornings, I know. I've seen you. But you don't black them. Nobody touches the blacking but the boots himself, an old man with a limp called Fennimore. Half the ship has heard him say so." She tilted her head. "You went to look at somebody's shoes."

"Yes, miss," I said.

There didn't seem to be any point saying anything else.

"Whose?"

"You know whose, miss."

"Yes," she said. "I do."

* * *

"You took the typewriter case from the library," she said. "At about seven. It was on the chair on Wednesday night, in his corner, when I came through with the children before bed. It wasn't there when I went past at eight this morning. Nobody had touched anything else of his. His pen was still on the table. Only the case." She looked at my hands again. "It's not with the purser. I asked the purser, at nine, very sadly, whether the poor man's things had been collected, because I'd like to send a note to his family. The purser said he hadn't had time. So you've got it."

"Yes, miss."

"Why?"

"Because he typed some of it out on Wednesday, miss," I said. "A fair copy. Five pages. They were in the lid. Mr. Ainsley took the notebook. He doesn't know about the pages."

She went very still.

I watched her take that in. I watched her see the five pages, folded, in the lid of a scuffed case with a mended handle, and see what was on them, and see that she had not known about them, and see what it meant that I had. It took her about as long as it takes to read this sentence. Then her face was calm again.

"You've read them," she said.

"Yes, miss."

"All of them."

"Yes, miss."

She looked at the lower berth, at the hollow in the pillow.

"Then you know what's in them," she said, "and who."

* * *

I want to tell you about the next twenty minutes very carefully, Ms. Barnett, because I've never had another twenty minutes like them, in any life, before or since. I've had longer conversations. I've had cleverer ones, perhaps, with famous men. I've never had one like that.

She didn't argue with me. That was the first thing. She didn't pretend. She didn't tell me I was a child who didn't understand, or that I'd imagined it, or that a bell boy had no business in it. She talked to me as if I were exactly what I was, which nobody had done for three weeks, and I think nobody had done for longer than that, in that life or the one before.

"Mr. Ainsley killed him," I said. "On the stairs. You saw the step."

"Yes."

"You saw the heel. The half moon. You wiped it out."

"Yes."

"Why?"

She folded her hands in her lap.

"Because there was only one place that step went," she said. "Think about it. You're a clever boy. Cleverer than any boy I've met. Think it all the way through." She said it quite gently. "An officer sees that mark. He asks whose heel. He finds the shoe. Mr. Ainsley is questioned. Mr. Ainsley says he never went near the man. The officer asks why anyone would want to harm a poor writer. Somebody says, he was writing about the boats. The captain is told. The owners are told. In New York there will be an inquiry about the Titanic in any case, a great public one, with senators, and every survivor will be asked what happened in their boat, under oath." She looked at the berth. "And somebody will ask her."

"She told Mr. Hatch," I said.

"She told him once. At two in the morning, on Tuesday, in a corner of a smoking room, after a doctor had given her brandy. She told him because he was kind, and asked, and she hadn't slept, and she couldn't hold it anymore." She was still looking at the berth. "She'll never say it again. Not if she lives to be a hundred. Not unless somebody makes her. In a room full of newspapermen. Under oath. With her name in every paper in America the next morning. *The woman who sat in a boat with twenty empty places and watched.* And her children reading it, one day." She turned back to me. "Her husband went down with that ship, Bertie. Did you know? She kissed him at the rail, and he put her into the boat, and he stood back. She has nothing now. Not a husband. Not a house. Two children, and a governess who isn't paid. Your Mr. Hatch's justice would take what's left."

"He wasn't my Mr. Hatch."

"No," she said. "But you've made him yours. I saw it on the landing. You'd made him yours long before."

I didn't answer that.

"And you," I said. "You're in the pages too."

"Yes." She didn't look away. "I'm the one who said not to go back. I know. I said it, and I'd say it again tomorrow, and I would say it at any inquiry they liked. I don't mind that being known. I mind her."

"A man's dead, miss," I said. "A man who did nothing but write it down."

"Yes. And a steward is dead who did nothing but swim," she said. "And fifteen hundred others who did nothing at all. And not one of them will come back, whatever is done to Mr. Ainsley." She leaned forward a little. "I'm not defending him. Don't think that. I watched him stand up in that boat. I watched him at the service, afterward, singing the hymn. I know exactly what he is. I knew what he'd done on those stairs before I'd got to the bottom of them. But there's no way to hang him for it that doesn't go through her."

"So he walks down the gangway."

"Yes."

"And he does it again. Men like that do. Somewhere. To somebody."

"Perhaps," she said. "And then someone will stop him then. Someone who can." She looked at me, a long, level look, with her gray eyes too far apart. "You and I are a bell boy and a governess, on a ship full of grief, with eleven hours to New York. Neither of us can stop anybody. We can only decide who gets hurt next."

* * *

I've thought about what she said, since. Every word of it. I've had a long time to.

She was right about the inquiry. There was one, in New York, the next day, in the Waldorf-Astoria, with senators, and they called the survivors and asked them about their boats, and the newspapers printed every word. She was right about that, and about the mother, and about what it would have cost.

And she was wrong. A man killed another man on a staircase for writing down the truth, and walked away. I've never been able to make that right, whatever the arithmetic.

Both of those are true. I told Freda that, this morning. Nobody likes it. I didn't like it then.

"What do you want from me, miss?" I said.

"The pages," she said simply. "Give them to me."

"No, miss."

She smiled. It was the first time I'd seen her smile. It was small, and quick, and a little sad, and it reached her eyes, and for a moment she looked about twenty.

"No," she said. "I didn't think you would." She stood up. "Thank you for the milk, Bertie. And for the blanket, on Monday. You can go."

I went.

* * *

I went down four decks to the bell boys' cabin, faster than I should have, because something in the way she had smiled had got under my collar and was sitting there.

Stan was there. He was sitting on his bunk, in the middle of the morning, when he should have been on B deck, and he had a shilling. He was turning it over and over in his fingers, and when he saw me in the door he put it in his pocket very quickly and stood up.

"Bertie," he said. "Where've you been?"

I didn't answer. I went to my locker. The door was shut. I opened it.

My spare jacket was where I had left it. The typewriter case was where I had left it, behind the jacket, latched.

I unlatched it and opened the lid.

The pages were gone.

I turned round. Stan was standing by his bunk with his hands in his pockets, and his face had gone red from the neck up.

"A lady came," he said. "Down the companionway. They're not supposed to, but she did. She was nice. She said you'd taken a toy of the little girl's to mend, a wooden horse, and the little girl was crying for it, and she couldn't wait, and would I mind." He swallowed. "She gave me a shilling, Bertie. A whole shilling. I opened your locker and she looked in the case and there wasn't any horse, and she said she must have been mistaken and you must have it somewhere else, and she was sorry to trouble me, and went." He looked at me. "I didn't see her take anything. Honest."

"No," I said. "You wouldn't have."

"Was it a horse? Did you have the kid's horse?"

"No, Stan."

"Oh," said Stan. And then, after a moment, unhappily: "D'you want half the shilling?"

He stopped. He put the pencil down on the notepad.

Freda, on the stairs, had her hand over her mouth.

"She kept you talking," she said, through her fingers. "On purpose. The whole time."

"Twenty minutes," he said. "About right and wrong, and what the two of us could and couldn't do. And I enjoyed every minute of it. God help me. It was the first real conversation I'd had in three weeks, and she'd known I would enjoy it, and she'd known that while I was enjoying it I wouldn't be thinking about my locker." He shook his head slowly. "She asked for the pages so I'd say no. So I'd feel I'd won. She never expected me to give them to her. She'd already sent somebody to look in that case."

"That's so..." Freda took her hand away from her mouth. "That's so good, though. That's evil. But it's so good."

"Yes," said Tom. He was smiling, a little, at the window. It was not a happy smile, quite, but it was not an unhappy one either. "That's exactly what I thought, at the time. Standing in that cabin, looking at an empty case. I thought, that's evil, and it's so good."

"Did you hate her?"

He considered that, seriously, as he considered everything Freda asked him.

"No," he said at last. "I think that was the trouble."
