# CQD

## Chapter 5: Did You Know?

He stopped at one, as he always did, and said that was enough for the morning, and went to heat the soup.

I sat with the recorders still running for a minute after he had gone into the kitchen. I did that sometimes. I used to say it was to catch anything said in the doorway, which people often did, the important thing saved for when they thought you had stopped listening. Really it was to give myself a minute before I had to talk.

On the arm of his chair, the notepad lay face up. The star was on it, gone over so many times the lead shone.

I turned the recorders off.

* * *

The soup was split pea, and the bread was from the bakery, the second of his two last loaves, which he had decided not to freeze after all.

"I'd only eat it in June and be sad," he said. "Better to eat it now and be sad in March, when it's appropriate."

We ate at the kitchen table, with the rain going on outside, and for a while neither of us said anything. He looked tired. He had looked tired the week before, too, and I had put it down to the card. Now I was not sure what to put it down to. He ate slowly, and broke his bread into small pieces, and did not finish them.

"Can I ask you something off the tape?" I said.

"You can ask me anything off the tape."

"Did you know?"

He did not pretend not to understand. He put his spoon down.

"That night? No."

"Not the ship. Not the iceberg. I understand you couldn't have known that." I turned my glass on the table. "I mean, did you know you'd done something. Afterward. When it was over, and they'd picked them all up. Did you ever think, that was me, I did that."

"No," he said.

"Never?"

"I thought, I don't know whether that was me." He looked at the window. "It's not the same thing. It's worse, in some ways. If I'd known I had done it, I could have been proud of it, or got tired of being proud of it, and put it away. If I'd known I hadn't, I could have forgotten it. Instead I've carried it around for a hundred years like a stone in my shoe."

"You could have told it differently. This morning." I said it carefully. "You could have said you kept him at the set. That you saved them. I'd have written it down."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because I don't know that I did," he said. "And you asked me to tell it the way it was." He picked up his spoon again. "Besides. Harold Cottam heard that call. Arthur Rostron turned the ship. Every man in that engine room shoveled coal until his hands bled, to get her there. They were the ones who did it. All I did was carry a cup."

"Evans made the cocoa," I said.

He laughed, the real laugh, the one that surprised him.

"So he did," he said. "I'd forgotten that. Put it in. Evans made the cocoa."

* * *

He did not go back to 1912 after lunch. He said he would rather tell the next part fresh, on a morning, from the beginning of it, and I did not argue, because he looked as if he needed the afternoon more than I needed the tape.

He asked about Freda before I left. He did, most weeks, at the door. I told him she was typing the transcripts now, for fifteen dollars an hour, and that she was faster than I was, and that she had told me he sounded on the last tape like a man whose house had been broken into.

He stood on the porch with the umbrella furled, and thought about that.

"She's right," he said. "That's a good ear." He looked at me. "She'll hear what you leave out, you know. When you type, you tidy. You take out the ums, and the places where I stop and start again. She won't, not at first, because she doesn't know which parts you'd want gone. Read what she types. You'll learn things about me you missed sitting in the chair."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's a compliment," he said. "To her."

* * *

I passed the Frederick exit at a quarter past three, in the rain, and did not take it.

I had found the son's address on Sunday night, after the receipt, after Petrakis. It had taken about twenty minutes. There was only one Dunleavy household in the county with an Eileen in it, a split-level on a cul-de-sac on the east side of town, owned by a Kevin Dunleavy and his wife. There was a picture of the house on the county site. A basketball hoop over the garage. A ramp, newer than the house, built over the front steps.

I had not called. I did not know what I would say. *Hello, Mrs. Dunleavy, you won't remember me, but six years ago a man who lived above you went out one Wednesday and never came back, and I wondered if you happened to write down who knocked on his door.* I had made calls like that a thousand times, once. I had been good at them. I was not sure, anymore, that I was the person who made them.

I drove past, and watched the exit go by in the mirror, and decided it was because I needed to be home for Freda.

* * *

That night, after Freda had gone to her room, I did what I always did. I checked.

It did not take long. The Senate had held hearings on the Titanic within days of the Carpathia getting into New York, and every word of them had been printed, and every word of the printed record was online, searchable, on a site run by people who plainly cared about it a great deal. Rostron had testified on the very first day, the nineteenth of April, 1912, the morning after he brought them in.

I read his testimony from the beginning. He had a plain way of talking, short sentences, no fuss, and you could hear in it the man Tom had described, turning his whole body toward whatever he looked at. He told them about the course he set, and the stokers, and the ice. And then, partway through, a senator asked him about the message, and how it was that he had happened to receive it, and he said this.

*I will tell you this, that the wireless operator was in his cabin, at the time, not on official business at all, but just simply listening as he was undressing. He was unlacing his boots at the time. He had this apparatus on his ear, and the message came. That was the whole thing. In 10 minutes, maybe he would have been in bed, and we would not have heard the messages.*

I read it twice. Then I sat back in the kitchen chair.

*I have thought about those ten minutes for nearly a hundred and fifteen years.*

There it was, the boots, the headphones, the ten minutes, in black and white, in a public record, under oath. Cottam had testified too, the same week, and so had others. Most of what he had told me that morning that could be checked at all, I could check right here, in one evening, at my own kitchen table.

Which was exactly the problem. I knew it was, and I made myself look at it squarely, as I would have made a junior reporter look at it. A man who had read this testimony would know about the boots. He would know about the ten minutes. He could build a beautiful story around them, with a bell boy and a mug of cocoa and a Welshman with a Bible, and every piece I could check would check, because he had taken it from the very place I would go to check it. That was how a clever liar worked. I had written about clever liars. I had been called one.

And it was also exactly what a man who had been there would say.

I had been going back and forth like that for twenty weeks. I wrote it down, as I wrote everything down: *Rostron, Senate, day 1. Boots, headphones, 10 min. All public. Proves nothing either way.* Then under it, because I was honest with myself on paper even when I was not anywhere else: *He didn't claim it. He could have. He said it was Evans's cocoa.*

* * *

Freda came out at eleven for a glass of water. She had her headphones around her neck, the new white ones, and her laptop under her arm, and she put it down on the table across from me and opened it.

"I finished this morning's," she said. "You can check it. I left in the ums."

"He said you would."

"He said what?"

"That you'd leave things in. That I should read what you type, because I'd learn things I missed." I took the laptop. "He said you had a good ear."

Freda looked at the table. She did a thing with her mouth, a small pressed thing, that she had done since she was very small whenever someone praised her and she did not want them to see it land.

"He doesn't know," she said. "If he did it. The cocoa thing."

"No."

"That's worse. Right?" She filled her glass at the sink and stood with her back to me, drinking it. "Not knowing if you made a difference. To someone. Like, you'd rather know either way."

"He said the same thing. Almost word for word."

She turned around. "Do you think people can tell? If they made a difference to someone. Or do you just never find out?"

I looked at her. She was standing very straight, with the glass in her hand, and the question was too careful, and I did not know what it was about.

"Sometimes you find out," I said. "Usually much later. Usually when you'd stopped expecting to."

Freda nodded, as if that were an answer to something, and put the glass in the sink, and said good night, and went. The door to her room closed. It didn't slam. I sat with her transcript open in front of me, all his ums and stops and starts left in, just as he had said, and read it through to the end, and learned that he had said *I don't know* nine times in a single morning, which I had not noticed sitting in the chair.

* * *

The money came on Saturday.

In the fall it had come on Thursday afternoons, three thousand dollars, a few hours after I left his house. Since January, since the client doubled it, it had come on Mondays, six thousand, as regular as the electric bill. On Saturday morning, at nine o'clock, my phone buzzed on the kitchen counter, and it was the bank, and it was the payment for Session 19, the same six thousand, with the same line in the note that had come with every payment since the rate went up.

*Our client asks you to take all the time the gentleman needs.*

I had sent the transcript on Friday at noon. Somebody had read it by Friday evening, or had it read to him, and had not waited for Monday. I didn't know what that meant. I didn't like any of the things it might mean.

I stood at the counter with the phone in my hand. Then I took my notebook out of my bag, and turned to a clean page, and drew the star from memory, in one line, the way his hand did it and the letter's did. Five points. A small letter crowded into each.

Under it I wrote: *Same star. On his pad. On the letter. Does he know?*

And then, after a long while: *Not yet.*

I meant that I would not show him yet. I am still not sure, now, which of us I meant it about.
