## Chapter 3: The Bellboy

*March 1912*

The first thing I knew was that I was holding a tray, and the floor was moving.

Not much. A long slow lean to one side, and a long slow lean back, the kind you stop noticing after a day at sea and never stop noticing in your knees. There was a cup and saucer on the tray, and a little silver pot, and a plate with two biscuits on it, and all of it was sliding very gently toward my chest. My hands were already tilting the tray to stop it. They knew how. I didn't.

I have told you my rule for the first hour of a life. Say nothing. Move as little as you can. Watch.

It's a hard rule to keep with a full pot of tea.

I was in a corridor. White painted walls, a long red carpet, brass rails, doors with numbers on them, electric lights in little glass shades that swung a quarter inch every time the floor leaned. The air smelled of coal smoke and polish and the sea, and very faintly of cabbage from somewhere below. A ship, then. A big one. The hum of her engines came up through the soles of my feet, through the whole of my body, like a cat purring against your chest.

And my body was very small.

You notice it at once. Everything is too high. The door handles were at the height of my ribs. The brass rail along the wall was at my shoulder. My wrists stuck out of a pair of navy sleeves with a row of brass buttons up the front of the jacket, more buttons than any jacket has needed in the history of the world, and there was a tight little cap on my head held on by a strap under the chin. I could feel that my knees were bony, and my feet hurt in a pair of boots a size too small, and somewhere in the front of my mouth there was a tooth that wobbled when I pressed my tongue to it.

A boy. A child. I had not been one in a very long time.

"Boy!"

A door down the corridor had opened, and a woman was leaning out of it. American, in a pink wrapper, with her hair in a net and a face that had been waiting.

"Is that my tea? I rang for it twenty minutes ago."

"Yes, madam," said my mouth, in a high clear voice I had never heard before, flat and quick, with every vowel squeezed. Liverpool. The body knows its own accent. "Sorry, madam. Busy in the pantry, madam."

I carried the tray down the corridor without spilling anything, which was the body's doing and not mine, and she took it from me at the door and looked at me, and I looked at the carpet, because the body seemed to think that was the right place to look.

"You're the one who's always whistling," she said.

"Yes, madam."

"Well, don't," she said, and shut the door.

* * *

It took me most of that first day to find out who I was. It always does. You don't ask. You listen, and you let other people tell you, and they always do, if you wait.

My name was Albert Pike. Nobody called me that. I was Bertie, to the other boys, and *boy* to the passengers, and *Pike* to the second steward, a heavy, tired man called Lyle who ran the bell boys like a farmer runs dogs. I was twelve years old. I had been at sea for a year. I came from Bootle, outside Liverpool, where my mother lived with my three younger sisters in two rooms off the Stanley Road, and every month most of my wages went home to her. I knew that because there was a letter from her in my locker, in pencil, thanking me for the last one and telling me Agnes had the croup again.

I knew the ship was the Carpathia, because it was written on everything, on the towels and the life rings and the menus and the little brass plate by the purser's office. She was a Cunarder. Not one of the great ones. She had one tall red funnel with a black top, and she rolled, and she was slow, and the passengers who had sailed on the Mauretania told you so. She went back and forth between New York and the Mediterranean, carrying whoever wanted to go: tourists going east to see Italy, and families going home, and families leaving home for good.

We were coming west, then, toward New York, and still a good way out. I worked that out from the menu cards.

I learned the rest from Stan Malley. Stan was fourteen, the oldest of the bell boys, with a broken front tooth and a talent for knowing which passengers tipped. He slept in the bunk over mine, in a cabin down on E deck with four other boys and no porthole, and he talked to the underside of the deck above him every night until he fell asleep in the middle of a word.

"You're quiet," he said to me, the second night. "You been quiet since yesterday."

"Got a headache."

"You never get headaches."

"Got one now."

"You want to watch Lyle," said Stan. "He's got his eye on you since the business with the soap." And he told me, before I had to ask, all about the business with the soap, which had happened before I arrived, and which I had apparently enjoyed very much.

* * *

That is the strangest part of it, Ms. Barnett, every time. Not the waking. The finding out what kind of person you've been.

The body keeps what it knows how to do. I've told you that. Bertie Pike's body knew every ladder and passage on that ship. It could go from the boat deck down to the third-class dining saloon and back without touching a rail, at a speed that made the stewards swear. It knew how to carry six cups on a tray in a swell. It knew how to take a coin from a passenger's hand without seeming to, and say *thank you, sir* in a way that made the next coin larger. It could whistle through the gap in its teeth, loud enough to make a lady in a pink wrapper complain, but only while I wasn't attending to it. The first time I tried on purpose, nothing came out but air. Twice that first week it started up by itself on the stairs, and both times I stopped it, because I didn't know what tunes Bertie whistled, and I couldn't afford to find out by getting one wrong.

What it did not keep was anything Bertie had thought. Whether he was happy. Whether he missed his mother. Whether he was the kind of boy who took the extra biscuit or the kind who gave his to Stan. I had to work all of that out from the outside, from how the others treated him, from the soap, from the letter in the locker. And then I had to go on being him, as near as I could, so that nobody noticed he had gone.

I have done that a great many times. It never gets easier when the body is a child. A grown man who changes overnight is thought to be ill, or in love, or in trouble. A boy who changes overnight is thought to be up to something. Lyle watched me for a week.

I gave him nothing to see. I carried the trays and ran the messages and took the coins and said *sir* and *madam*, and I did not whistle, which Stan noticed and Lyle noticed and neither of them understood. A quiet Bertie was odd. A Bertie whistling a tune he had never known would have been worse.

* * *

The one thing I could not do was sleep.

The body could. It was tired every night, the way a working boy is tired, and it would have gone down the moment it lay in the bunk, like a stone dropped down a well. But my mind would not go down with it, and so neither did the boy. I lay in the dark in the bunk under Stan's, with the engines going through the hull and the other boys breathing, wide awake, the way you are wide awake in a strange house.

An old man's sleep, in a boy's body. It happens. It passes, after a few weeks, when the mind settles into the body and they agree on things. Until then, you get up.

So I got up. The third night, and most nights after. I put on my trousers and my jacket and went up through the ship in my socks, carrying my boots, because a boy in socks can go anywhere on a ship at two in the morning and a boy in boots is heard.

I learned her that way. Every corridor, every ladder, every door that stuck. The night steward in the first-class pantry, an old Welshman called Evans who could not sleep either, and who would give a boy a mug of cocoa if the boy would sit and let him talk about his chapel. The quartermaster on the bridge wing who pretended not to see me. The engine room, where I was not allowed, and where the stokers let me stand at the top of the ladder and watch them feed the fires with the sweat running into their eyes.

And the wireless cabin.

It was a little room on the boat deck, not much bigger than a cupboard, with a bunk at one side and a desk at the other, and on the desk a great deal of brass and wire and black boxes, and a key, and a pair of headphones. The Marconi Company had put it there, and the Marconi Company owned it, and the young man who sat in it all day and half the night was a Marconi man, not a Cunard one. His name was Harold Cottam. He was twenty-one years old. He was the only wireless operator on the ship.

He had the light on, the first time I went past, at a quarter to one in the morning. The door was hooked open for the air, and he was sitting at the desk with the headphones on, writing on a pad, and his tea had gone cold beside him with a skin on top.

He looked up and saw a bell boy in socks, holding his boots, looking in at his door.

"You're up late," he said.

"Can't sleep, sir."

"Nor can I, it seems." He took one side of the headphones off. "Don't call me sir. I'm not an officer. They don't know what to do with me." He looked at the cold tea and made a face. "I don't suppose you know where a man could get a hot drink at this hour?"

I did. I went down to Evans in the pantry, and Evans made two mugs of cocoa and told me about his chapel, and I carried them up to the boat deck without spilling a drop.

That was the first night. After that, most nights, if the light was on in the wireless cabin, I brought him cocoa. He would take off one side of the headphones and drink it, and I would stand in the door, and he would tell me what he was hearing.

It was a whole world, Ms. Barnett. I had never thought about it. All across that ocean, in the dark, the ships were talking to each other. Not in voices. In sparks, in dots and dashes, each operator with his own hand that the others knew the way you know a friend's knock. Messages for passengers. Weather. Ice. Prices on the stock exchange in New York for gentlemen in first class who could not bear not to know. Gossip, between the operators, late at night, when the traffic was slow. Who had a cold. Who was in trouble with his chief. Which ship had the best food.

He let me listen, once. He put the headphones over my cap. It was a thin dry crackle, like frying, far away, and through it, now and then, a tiny high whine that came and went in a pattern.

"That's Cape Cod," he said. "Talking to a German, I think. You can tell by the hand."

I could not tell anything. But I was in the room, and the room was warm, and the sea was black and enormous on the other side of the wall, and in that little cupboard of brass and wire a young man was listening to the whole of it.

I didn't know anything, Ms. Barnett. I am not a prophet. I've told you so. I didn't know what ship had sailed from Southampton the day before we sailed from New York, or what was waiting for her, or that it would have anything to do with us. I was an old man who couldn't sleep, in the body of a boy, and I liked that room better than any other place on the ship. That's all it was.

That is what I keep coming back to. That's all it was.

* * *

We came into New York at the start of April. I couldn't tell you the day; one day in port was like the next, at the Cunard pier on the North River, taking on coal and stores and passengers for the run back east.

I saw the city from the boat deck. I had seen it before, in another life, and it had grown since, upward, as it always does. Bertie Pike had seen it a dozen times and found it ordinary, and his body didn't even turn to look. I stood at the rail with a broom I was supposed to be using and looked until Lyle shouted.

I can tell you the day we sailed. Everyone who was aboard her can. Thursday, the eleventh of April.

You have to picture that pier, that morning. Piles of trunks with labels on them for Gibraltar and Genoa and Naples and Trieste and Fiume. The first-class passengers coming up the gangway in good coats, Americans mostly, going to see the Old World, with guidebooks under their arms and their maids behind them. The families in third class, going home to Hungary and Croatia and the mountains behind Fiume, with everything they owned in bundles and baskets, and children asleep on top of the bundles. A band, somewhere, playing something cheerful and out of tune.

I carried bags for two hours without stopping.

There was a man in second class I remember from that morning, though there was no reason to then. A thin man, about forty, in a brown suit gone shiny at the elbows, carrying his own bag, which nobody in a brown suit ever does. A typewriter case, a small one, which he would not let me take from him.

"I'll manage it," he said, when I reached for it. "It's all I've got that's worth stealing."

He gave me a nickel anyway, at the door of his cabin, and asked my name. Passengers never ask your name. I told him.

"Bertie." He looked at me, properly, the way passengers never look at you either. "I'm Hatch. I write things, for the magazines, when they'll have me. Going to Naples to write about Vesuvius, if Vesuvius will cooperate." He smiled. "If you see anything worth writing about, Bertie, you come and tell me. I pay better than a nickel."

I said I would. I didn't think about him again for four days.

We cast off at noon, or near it. The tugs took us out into the river, and the city slid away behind us, and we turned south past the Statue and out through the Narrows into the open sea. I watched from the boat deck with a tray of beef tea for the ladies, until Lyle found me.

That night the light was on in the wireless cabin, and I brought up the cocoa.
