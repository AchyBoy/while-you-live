# CQD

## Chapter 24: The Letter

We ate lunch on the porch, too. Soup, from the dented pot, and bread from the supermarket in town that he said was improving, slowly, as a bad student improves when he is frightened of failing. He didn't eat much. He broke his bread into small pieces and moved them about the bowl, and I watched him do it and didn't say anything.

When we had finished, I went to the car and got the folder out of my bag.

I had carried it every Thursday since February. Ten Thursdays. It had sat on the floor by my chair, six feet from his knee, through the Carpathia and the boats and the fog and the pier, and every Thursday I had meant to take it out and every Thursday I had found a reason not to. The star, after I'd recognized it. Freda, after that. Being angry, after that.

On the back of a gas bill in my daughter's handwriting, at half past twelve on a Monday morning, it said *No more lies, in either direction.* I had signed it. I had not been thinking about Damon when I signed it. I was thinking about him now.

I came back up the porch steps and sat down and put the folder on the little table between us, on top of the soup bowls, and opened it.

"I found something," I said. "In February. Before the Carpathia card. I've been carrying it ever since. I meant to show you the day the card came, and I couldn't, and then I didn't." I turned the photocopy round so it faced him. "I'm sorry. I should have shown you weeks ago."

He looked at it. He didn't pick it up.

"What is it?"

"A letter," I said. "From the Lincoln papers. At the Library of Congress. In one of Nicolay's envelopes, the ones nobody's asked for in twenty-two years." I heard my voice go careful. "It's dated April the thirteenth, 1865."

He went very still.

* * *

He read it slowly. I watched him read it. I had read it so many times myself I knew where his eyes were by how long they stayed.

*Mr. President.* He stopped there, the first time, for a long moment, on the two words, on the hand. The long looping capitals. The most beautiful hand, he had told me in the winter, that he had ever seen on a bill of fare. I saw him know it. I saw his mouth move, very slightly, around a name he didn't say.

Then he read on. *There is a man in this city who means to take your life.* The National Hotel. The speech on the lawn. *The last speech you would ever make.* Seward. The Kirkwood. The large young man who called himself Wood and Paine. The German who drank. The druggist's clerk.

*A young man of Charles County, lately a soldier of the rebellion, lame in the left leg, who is much at his table and knows the roads of Southern Maryland.*

He stopped there for a long time.

"He named me," he said quietly. "He named all of us. To the President. The day before."

"Yes."

"And it went on a pile." He didn't look up. "*Anonymous. Warning. Filed.* Like mine."

He read the rest. *We cannot spare you, Sir. Not now.* And the last line, by itself.

*I have no name that would carry weight with you. I am one of those you spoke of.*

He sat back.

His eyes had filled. He didn't do anything about it. He sat on the bench on his porch in the April sun with the photocopy in front of him and let them, and looked out at the plowed field, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

"He tried," he said at last. "The day before. He tried with everything he had, and he signed it with nothing, so it would only be about the President, and not about him." He shook his head slowly. "I guessed, once. On Seventh Street, that summer, I guessed what he'd been writing. But I never knew. More than a hundred and sixty years, Ms. Barnett. I never knew what he'd said."

"I'm sorry I kept it from you."

"Don't be," he said. "You've just given it to me. That's the only part that matters." He wiped his face, finally, with the back of his hand, like a boy. "Thank you."

Then he looked down at the page again, to read the last line one more time, I think.

And his eyes went to the margin.

* * *

I had been waiting for it. I had been waiting for it since February, if I'm honest. I watched it happen.

His eyes went down the last line, *I am one of those you spoke of,* and came to the end of it, and moved, because eyes do, a little to the left, into the margin. To the small thing drawn there in the same ink, beside the last line. A star, drawn without lifting the pen, crowded with foreign letters.

He stopped.

Not the way he stopped for the Carpathia. That was stillness. This was something else. His whole body stopped, like a clock stopping, and his face emptied, and his hand, which had been resting on the edge of the photocopy, stayed there, flat, pressing down, as if the page might lift off the table.

I didn't count. I had counted, the first day, on the tape. I had counted at the Carpathia. I didn't count now. I sat across the little table from him and waited, and I could hear the wind in the field, and a tractor, very far off, and my own heart.

Then, without looking down, he reached for the notepad on the bench beside him. He put it on the table, next to the photocopy. The top page was covered in his star, gone over so many times the lead shone.

He looked from one to the other.

The same five points. The same single line, never lifting. The same small letters crowded into the points, in the same order. One in pencil, gone over a thousand times by an old man's hand on a porch. One in brown ink, more than a hundred and sixty years old, in the margin of a letter to Abraham Lincoln, in the most beautiful hand on any bill of fare in Washington.

"Thank you for bringing this," he said.

That was all. His voice was perfectly level.

* * *

"It's your star," I said.

"Yes."

"I saw it in February. In the reading room. I thought it looked familiar, and I didn't know why, and I didn't think about it again. And then in March, the day you told me you were twelve, I looked at your notepad, and I knew." I made myself say the next thing. "Did Kit draw it? Did you? On his letter, somehow? Before it went?"

"No," he said. "I never saw this letter. I didn't know it existed until two minutes ago. You know that. You've just watched me find out."

I had. I knew it the way I knew my own hand.

"Then how is it on there?"

He looked from one star to the other, and back.

"I don't know," he said.

He said it as he had said it once before, in the winter, looking at a shaking card in the client's hand. The way a man says it who has not had to say it very often in a very long life, and doesn't like the taste.

"Who taught it to you?" I said. "The star."

"Somebody a very long time ago." He was still looking at the page. "The same person who taught me the song."

I sat with that. I thought of a young woman in a deck chair, on a cold night, wrapped in two blankets, humming to a child who couldn't sleep. *It's a very old song. Older than anything.*

"The governess hummed your song," I said slowly. "And Damon drew your star."

"Yes."

"Two lives. Fifty years apart. Two different people."

"Yes."

"And they both had something of yours. Something nobody else has. Nobody alive."

He didn't answer.

"Ari," I said. Quietly. It was the porch, and it was just us. "What does that mean?"

He looked up from the page, then. At me. And I saw that he didn't know. That he was frightened, a little, that he didn't know. And that underneath the fright there was something else, something I had never seen in his face in all the Thursdays, that I didn't have a word for until I was halfway home.

Hope. It was hope.

"I don't know," he said again. "I don't know, Ms. Barnett. I've spent a very long time thinking I was the only one." He put one finger on the star in the margin, very lightly, as you'd touch something in a museum when the guard wasn't looking. "May I keep this? The copy."

"It's yours. I made it for you. I just didn't know I had."

He folded it, very carefully, along the creases, and held it in both hands in his lap. Neither of us said anything else. After a while he unfolded it again, and looked at the margin, and folded it back along the same creases, as if he needed to see it was still there.

* * *

On the drive home I pulled into the gas station at the bottom of the hill, the one with the sign for night crawlers, and sat in the car with the engine running and took out my notebook.

*Damon. 1865. The star.*

*E. P. 1912. The song.*

*Two people. Two lives. Fifty years. His marks.*

And then, under it, because I was honest on paper even when I was not anywhere else:

*He didn't know. He was frightened. He was glad.*

I looked at that for a while. Then I wrote one more line, and underlined it, and put the notebook away and drove home.

*Who else has he put away?*
