# CQD

## Chapter 23: Aristeas

I wore the coat.

It was too warm for it, by then. The last week of April, and the trees along the highway were full and green, and the farm stands past Frederick had opened again, with hand-painted signs for asparagus and eggs. I wore it anyway, the green wool with the moth hole and the button I had finally sewn back on, on Wednesday night, at the kitchen table, while Freda typed beside me with the headphones around her neck instead of on, so she could talk.

He wasn't at the window.

That was the first thing. On every earlier visit he had been at the window, and on the porch before I had the engine off. I sat in the car for a moment with my hand on the key, looking at the house, and the curtain didn't move.

Then the door opened, and he came out.

He came out slowly. He came down the porch steps with both hands on the rail, not one, and stopped at the bottom to get his breath, and I saw that he had lost weight since the winter. More than I had let myself notice. The cardigan hung on him. His face had that gray under the weathering that I had seen last week and decided not to care about, and it was worse, and I cared.

He saw the coat. I saw him see it.

He didn't say anything. He stood at the bottom of his steps, in the sun, with one hand still on the rail, and waited for me.

I got out of the car and walked across the gravel and stopped in front of him.

"You should have told me," I said.

"Yes," he said.

"I know why you didn't."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, slowly, and something in his shoulders came down that I hadn't known was up.

"Thank you," he said.

* * *

We sat on the porch. He suggested it. He said the house was stuffy and the sun was out and he had been indoors too much that week, and I thought that he didn't want to climb the steps again so soon, and I didn't say so. He sat on the bench and I sat in one of the two chairs. He had put the kettle on before he came out. Through the open door I could hear it beginning to tick on the stove.

"What I said," I said. "Last week. In the kitchen. About pretending to be other people for a living."

"It was true," he said.

"It was cruel."

"Yes. That's why it hurt. The cruel things that aren't true, you can put down." He was looking out at the field. It had been plowed since last week, long dark furrows all the way to the tree line. "I've been a great many people, Ms. Barnett. I've been them very carefully, so nobody would notice they'd gone. Bertie Pike's mother never knew. Kit's mother never knew. That's what I do. You weren't wrong."

"You let Freda know you."

"Freda's very hard to pretend in front of," he said. "So are you."

Inside, the kettle began to whistle. He started to get up, and I said, "I'll get it," and went in and took it off the stove and turned the gas off. I stood for a moment in his kitchen. The last time I had made tea in it, I had opened all the wrong cupboards. I knew where the cups were now. Then I came back out.

"How is she?" he said.

This time I answered. I told him. The end of the bed, and the pier, and the card from Phoenix in the box of baby teeth, and the hardware store on Georgia Avenue, and *it's Freda,* and ten seconds of nothing, and a man who hung up. I told him about the liquor store on Kennedy Street. I told him about the terms, on the back of a gas bill, signed by both of us, in the pocket of a sweater I would never see again.

I told him I was meeting Desmond on Saturday, for coffee, at ten. That Desmond had said he'd be there at nine.

He listened to all of it without interrupting. When I got to the gas bill, he laughed, the real one.

"She'll keep that," he said. "For years. You'll find it in a drawer when she's forty."

"I know."

"And you'll go on Saturday."

"I'll go." I looked at my hands. "I don't know what I'll say."

"You'll check," he said. "That's what you'll say. You'll ask him everything, very politely, and you'll write it all down afterward in the car, and then you'll go and find out whether it's true." He smiled at the field. "And some of it you won't be able to check. And you'll have to decide."

"Freda said that. About you."

"She's very like you," he said. "I don't think either of you know how much."

* * *

He gave me something, before we went in.

He reached into the pocket of the cardigan and took out a folded piece of paper, notebook paper, torn from a spiral, and held it out to me.

"This came on Tuesday," he said. "By post. It's addressed to me, but I think she'd want you to see it. Since there aren't any secrets now."

It was Freda's handwriting. The small neat handwriting from the back of the gas bill. Three lines.

*Mr. Hale. Thank you for not telling. And for telling me to. I told her. All of it. You were right, it got lighter. F.*

*P.S. I typed the pier. I'm sorry nobody came for Bertie.*

I read it twice. Then I folded it back up, along the creases she'd made, and gave it back to him. He put it in the pocket of his cardigan, and kept his hand over it for a moment, on the outside, the way Hatch used to keep his hand over the notebook.

* * *

We went in at eleven. He went up the porch steps slowly, with both hands, and I went behind him, not touching him, close enough. In the living room he sat down in his chair as if he had walked a long way, and picked up the notepad, and did not draw on it.

I set out the recorders. I didn't turn them on.

"Before you do," he said.

I looked up.

"Ms. Barnett," he said. "In all these Thursdays, you've never asked me my name."

"I know your name. You told me. The first day."

"I told you the name on the mailbox I keep meaning to paint." He was looking at the empty frame on the mantel. "That's the name this body came with. Tom Hale. It's a good name. He was a good man, I think, the one who had it first. I've tried to be careful with it." He was quiet a moment. "It isn't mine."

I waited.

"I had one, once," he said. "A long time ago. The first one. Before any of the others. I haven't said it aloud in..." He stopped, and seemed to count, and gave up. "In a very long time. I haven't told anybody what it was, in more lives than I could list for you. There didn't seem to be anybody to tell." He turned from the mantel and looked at me. "I'd like somebody to know it. I find I'd like that very much. While I..." He stopped again. "While there's time."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't." He smiled, a little. "That's why I'd like to."

He said it.

It was a Greek name. Old. He said it slowly, carefully, in a way that was not quite English, with the weight in a different place than I would have put it, as if his mouth were remembering how. Four syllables. It sounded like something carved on a stone.

"Aristeas," he said.

I didn't write it down. I don't know why. I had written down everything he had said to me for seven months. I sat with my pen in my hand and didn't write it, and I think he saw that, and I think it was the right thing to have done.

"Aristeas," I said. Badly. I put the weight in the wrong place.

He laughed, softly.

"Close," he said. "Nobody's said it to me in a very long time. Nobody alive." He looked down at the notepad, at the star, gone over so many times the lead shone. "The people who called me that called me Ari, mostly. When I was young. The first time I was young."

"Can I call you that?"

He thought about it, seriously, the way he thought about everything Freda asked.

"Not on the tape," he said. "Not in the transcript. Not to your client." He paused. "But here. On the porch. When it's just us." He looked at me. "Yes. I think I'd like that."

"Ari," I said.

He closed his eyes for a moment, as if he were listening for it to come again. Then he opened them, and picked up the pencil, and settled the notepad on his knee.

"Turn them on," he said. "We've got a great deal to do, and I don't know how many Thursdays."

I didn't ask him what he meant. I think I knew. I turned on the recorders, and said the date into them, and the time, and *Session twenty-seven,* and he looked out of the window at the plowed field, and began to draw.
