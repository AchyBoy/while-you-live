## Chapter 12: The Foot of the Stairs

*April 17, 1912*

On Wednesday evening the fog came down.

We were getting near the American coast by then, off the shoals south of Nantucket, and the fog came up out of the sea in the late afternoon and closed over us like a lid. In an hour you couldn't see the bow from the bridge. In two you couldn't see the rail from the deck chairs. The captain brought her down to half speed, and then to dead slow, and the ship's whistle began.

Every minute. A long, low, terrible sound, out into the white, and then nothing, and then a minute later the same sound again. It went on all night. It went through the hull and the decks and the walls and the bunks and the people in them. I have heard a great many sounds in a great many lives that I would rather not hear again. That one is near the top. Seven hundred people who had heard a ship's whistle at sea three nights before, and what came after it, lay in their bunks and on the floors of the saloons and listened to it, once a minute, all night long.

Nobody slept much, on the Wednesday. Not them, and not us.

* * *

Mr. Hatch spent the afternoon typing.

He had moved from the smoking room to a table in the corner of the second-class library, where it was quieter, and he had the typewriter out of its case for the first time since we'd left New York, and he was typing with two fingers, very fast, from the notebook propped open beside the machine. The notebook was twice the size it had been on Monday. He had been writing in it for three days.

I asked him once, when I brought him tea, what he was typing.

"A fair copy," he said, without stopping. "Some of it. The worst of my handwriting. In case a man in New York can't read it." He stopped then, and looked at the page in the machine, and at the notebook, and then at me. "I'll never finish. There's too much. I'll do the rest on the train."

"What train, sir?"

"Whichever one's going out of New York first on Friday morning," he said, "to wherever is furthest from Mr. Ainsley." He smiled. It was a tired smile, but a real one. "Don't look like that, Bertie. Men like Mr. Ainsley don't do anything. They hire lawyers. In a year I'll be in court, and I'll be broke, and I'll have the best story of my life with my name on it." He went back to his two fingers. "There are worse ways to be broke."

I looked at the typewriter case, open on the chair beside him. There were already pages in the lid, a little stack of them, typed and folded. Not many. Five or six.

I didn't know then that I would carry that case off the ship myself, and that it would cost me more than anything I had carried on it.

* * *

At half past ten that night I brought him his last cup of tea.

He was back in his corner in the smoking room by then. The library had been given over to survivors to sleep in. He was sitting by the black window with the fog pressed against the glass outside, with his coat on, and the notebook on his knee, and his pencil, and he wasn't writing. He was reading it, from the beginning, slowly, turning the pages, the way you read a letter you're about to send to somebody who matters.

The whistle went, outside. The glasses on the sideboard rattled.

"Bertie," he said.

"Sir."

"When this is printed." He closed the notebook, and slid it into the inside pocket of his coat, and buttoned the pocket, and patted it once, as I had seen him do every time. "When it's printed, I'm going to put you in it. *A bell boy named Bertie, who brought tea.* How's that?"

"I'd rather you didn't, sir."

He laughed. "Why on earth not?"

"Lyle would have my hide, sir."

He laughed again, properly this time, and took the tea, and drank it, and made a face at it, because it had been standing.

"I'm going to walk once round the deck," he said, "in this pea soup, and get the cobwebs out, and then I'm going to bed. If anybody asks you, Bertie." He looked at me over the cup, and for a moment he wasn't smiling. "If anybody asks you, you never saw this notebook."

"No, sir."

"Good boy."

That was all. That was the last thing he ever said to me. *Good boy.* I took the cup back to the pantry and Evans washed it and I went down to my bunk.

* * *

I didn't sleep. The whistle wouldn't let me, and nor would I.

At a quarter to one I got up and went up through the ship in my socks.

You couldn't see anything on deck. I came out through the door onto the boat deck and stepped into white. Not white like snow. White like the inside of a cloud, wet and cold and close against your face, so thick the electric lights along the deckhouse were only yellow smears in it, a few feet apart, with nothing between them. Everything dripped. The rail dripped. The boats over my head dripped. The deck was black and shining and slick under my socks.

The whistle went. I felt it in my chest.

And when it stopped, in the silence after it, I heard footsteps.

Somewhere ahead of me, along the deck, in the fog. Going away. Quick, and even, and hard. Click, click, click, click, on the wet boards, getting fainter. Then a door. Then nothing.

I stood still. I knew that step. Anyone on that ship would have known it, by then.

Leather heels.

* * *

The after companionway was a steep stair that went down from the boat deck, inside, to the promenade deck below. Teak steps with brass on the edges, a brass handrail on each side, and a light at the top and a light at the bottom. It was how the second-class passengers went up and down to the boat deck. It was where I'd seen Mr. Hatch go up and down, every day, with his notebook.

The door at the top was standing open, and the light at the top was on, and the fog was drifting in over the sill.

There was a hat on the top step.

A gray felt hat, a cheap one, lying upside down on the top step, with its crown on the tread and the inside of it facing up at the light. I stopped in the doorway and looked at it. The fog was blowing in all around it, and the tread was wet, and the brass edge was wet. The inside of the hat was dry.

Then I looked down the stairs.

He was lying at the bottom. On his back, on the landing, with one leg still up on the lowest step and his arms out, and his head at an angle a head should not be at, against the wall. His eyes were open. He was looking up the stairs, straight at me.

I have seen a great many dead men, Ms. Barnett. I knew before I was halfway down.

I went down anyway. I went down very carefully, on the edges of the steps, close to the rail, not touching it, and I knelt beside him on the landing and put two fingers under his jaw, as I had done in other lives, and there was nothing. He was still warm. He had not been there long. Minutes.

I didn't touch anything else. I looked.

I want to tell you what I saw, in the order I saw it, because I have gone over it so many times since that it's worn smooth, and I want you to have it exactly.

His coat. The inside pocket, on the left. The one he buttoned every time, and patted. It was unbuttoned. It was hanging open, and it was empty. I could see the lining. There was nothing in it.

His right hand, the one flung out toward the wall. The knuckles were skinned. All four of them, raw and fresh, with a little blood in the creases. You don't skin your knuckles falling down a flight of stairs on your back. You skin them hitting something.

His pencil. It was on the third step from the top, lying across the tread. Not at the bottom with him. At the top.

And on the fifth step, near the wall side, on the wet teak, there was a mark. Half a mark. The back of a shoe, where the heel had come down hard on the wet wood. A neat, sharp-edged, curved half-moon, the kind a hard leather heel makes, going up. Not down. Up.

I knelt there, beside him, with the whistle going, and I put it together, as you put together a thing you already know. A man at the top of the stairs, in the fog, at night, with his notebook buttoned in his pocket. Somebody with him. A hat knocked off on the top step before any of the fog had got into it. A fist thrown. A pencil dropped. A fall. And then somebody coming quickly down after him, kneeling where I was kneeling now, unbuttoning a dead man's pocket. And going back up, fast, on hard heels, one of them coming down on a wet step and leaving half a moon.

Click, click, click. A door.

* * *

There was a sound behind me. Below me. On the promenade deck, at the bottom of the stairs, a door opening.

I turned.

It was the young woman in the dark coat.

She had come up the corridor from the second-class cabins below, I learned later, because the little girl had woken with the whistle and cried, and she had come up to find somebody, anybody, to get the child some warm milk. She was in a nightdress with her coat over it, and her hair down her back, and a pair of borrowed felt slippers on her feet, the soft kind they had given out to the women who came off the boats with no shoes.

She stopped in the doorway at the bottom of the stairs and saw him. Then she saw me.

She didn't scream. She didn't make any sound at all. She stood in the doorway with one hand on the frame and looked.

And I watched her look, Ms. Barnett. I watched her eyes. I have never forgotten it, and I never will.

They went to his face first, as anyone's would. Then, without any pause at all, they went to the coat. The open pocket. Then to his right hand, against the wall, and the knuckles. Then up the stairs, quickly, step by step, to the pencil on the third step, to the hat on the top step, upside down in the light. Then down again. To the fifth step.

To the half moon.

Every one. In order. In about the time it takes to say it.

Then she looked at me.

We stayed like that for I don't know how long. A boy of twelve kneeling by a dead man on a landing, and a young woman in a nightdress and a coat, standing in a doorway three feet from him, with the fog drifting down the stairs between us and the whistle going, once, and then the silence after it. And neither of us said anything at all, because there was nothing to say. We had both just done the same thing. We both knew it. We were both, for the first time in that life, looking at somebody who could see.

I can't tell you what it was like. I've tried. It was like being alone in a dark house for a very long time, and hearing somebody in the next room put down a cup.

"You've seen it," she said. Very quietly.

"Seen what, miss?"

"Don't," she said. "Not with me."

I didn't say anything.

She came in off the doorway, then, and knelt on the other side of him, on the landing, and put her fingers to his throat, the way I had. Her face didn't change. She took her hand away.

"Go and get the doctor," she said. "And the officer on watch. I'll stay with him."

I looked at her. I looked at the felt slippers on her feet, soft, silent, the kind you couldn't have heard on those boards if you'd been standing beside them. I looked up the stairs at the half moon on the fifth step.

I didn't want to leave her there with it. I knew that. I knew it as clearly as I've ever known anything.

And I was twelve, and a dead man was lying on the landing, and a lady had told me to go for the doctor.

So I went.

* * *

When I came back with the doctor and the officer of the watch and two stewards, it was ten minutes later, perhaps a little more. She was still there, kneeling beside him, exactly where I had left her. She had closed his eyes.

The doctor knelt and looked and shook his head. The officer stood at the top of the stairs with a lantern and looked down, and swore under his breath, and then said he was sorry, miss.

"Stairs like glass in this," he said. "Poor devil must have gone straight down them in the dark. The whole deck's like a skating rink. I nearly went over myself an hour ago."

"Yes," said the doctor. "A fall. Look at the angle of the neck." He sat back on his heels. "Does anybody know who he is?"

"His name's Hatch," I said. "Second class, sir. He's a writer."

The officer looked at me as if he'd only just noticed I was there. Which I suppose he had.

I looked past him, up the stairs, at the fifth step.

The half moon was gone.

There was a smear there instead. A long, soft, shapeless smear across the wet teak, as if somebody had slipped on it. Somebody in soft shoes.

I looked at the young woman. She was getting up off her knees, with one hand on the rail, and her coat had come open, and I saw that the hem of her nightdress was wet and dark across the front, as if she'd gone down on a wet stair.

"I'm sorry," she said to the officer. Her voice was perfectly steady. "I slipped, coming down to him. I'm afraid I've made a mess of your stairs."

"Not at all, miss," said the officer. "Easily done. That's just what I'm saying. Easily done, in this."

He wrote it in his book, standing there under the lantern. I watched him write it. I could read it upside down.

*Fell on after companionway in fog. Death accidental.*

And the young woman pulled her coat around her, and nodded to the doctor, and went back down the corridor toward the second-class cabins, to get the little girl her milk. She didn't look at me as she went.

She didn't need to.

He stopped.

I realized I had been holding my pen without writing anything for some time. I put it down.

"Did she take it?" I said. "The notebook."

"That's what I thought," he said. "For about a day."

"And the mark on the step. She wiped it out. On purpose."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He didn't answer that. He looked at the window. Outside, the rain had stopped, and the field was standing in water, and the sky was low and white and close, almost like fog.

"The officer wrote down what was in front of him," I said. I heard my own voice, a little strange. "*Death accidental.* And went to bed."

"Yes," he said. "There's always one of those."

I thought of a young policeman with a face like a boiled ham, standing in an apartment on Calloway Street, writing *Desk* in his book and going away. And then I thought of something else, and it went through me, and I looked at him.

"Leather heels," I said.

He met my eyes.

"I told you," he said. "It reminded me of something a long way off."

"That's why your hand stopped. On the coffee pot."

"Yes," he said. "That's why."
