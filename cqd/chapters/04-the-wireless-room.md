# CQD

## Chapter 4: The Wireless Room

*April 14, 1912*

Sunday was a long day. They always were, at sea.

There was a service in the first-class saloon in the morning, and the bell boys stood at the back in clean collars and held the hymn books for the ladies who had forgotten theirs. The captain came down for it. He was a new captain, that year, a slight, quick man called Rostron, with a way of turning his whole body toward whatever he was looking at. The stewards called him the Electric Spark, though never where he could hear. He gave orders faster than any officer I had ever served under, in any life, and he gave them quietly, and he did not give them twice.

I watched him through the hymns. I watched everyone. It was a habit I could not have broken if I had wanted to, and on that ship it was the only thing I had that was mine. A boy who cannot speak still has his eyes.

In the afternoon I carried beef tea and letters and rugs. Mr. Hatch, the man with the typewriter, had set himself up in a corner of the second-class smoking room with the case open on his knees, and he was typing with two fingers, very fast, and smoking, and frowning at the window. He looked up as I went by with the tray and said, "Anything worth writing about yet, Bertie?" and I said, "No, sir," and he said, "Me neither," and went back to his two fingers.

Lyle found me at four and made me polish the brass on the main stair, all of it, because a lady had complained that I looked at her. I don't know which lady. I looked at all of them. It took until dinner.

That night the body fell asleep at ten, like a stone down a well, and I lay awake inside it and listened to the engines and to Stan, above me, talking to the deck about a girl in Bootle he had never spoken to.

At a quarter to twelve I got up.

* * *

Evans was in the pantry with his feet up on a crate and his collar undone, reading his Welsh Bible by the light over the sink. He looked up when I came in carrying my boots, and sighed, and put the kettle on without being asked.

"He'll be in bed, that one," he said. "He's been on that machine since seven this morning. Even a Marconi man has to sleep."

"His light's on, Mr. Evans."

"His light's always on." He made the cocoa in two mugs and put a saucer over each to keep the heat in. "Go on, then. And you come straight back down and get in your bunk, or Lyle will have your hide and mine with it."

I went up through the ship.

You have to understand how quiet a ship is, at that hour, on a calm night. The passengers are asleep. The stewards are asleep, except the one or two on watch, and they are sitting down. The engines are the only thing awake, and after a while you stop hearing them, and the whole great ship seems to be holding its breath and gliding.

I came out onto the boat deck, and the cold took the breath out of me.

It had been cold all evening. Now it was something else. The kind of cold that has no wind in it, that just stands there. The sea was perfectly flat. I had never seen the Atlantic like that, in any life, before or since. Not a ripple. Black, and still, and polished, like a floor somebody had just finished waxing. There was no moon. And the stars came all the way down to the water on every side, so many of them, so bright, that I could not tell where the sky stopped and the sea began, and for a moment I stood at the top of the ladder with a mug of cocoa in each hand and did not know which way was up.

Then I went along to the wireless cabin.

The door was hooked open, and the light was on. He was sitting on the edge of his bunk with his jacket off and his collar off and one boot already unlaced, bent over, working at the laces of the other. He still had the headphones on. The wire ran from his head across the little room to the desk, as if the machine had him on a leash.

"Bertie," he said, without looking up. "You're a saint. Put it on the desk."

I put it on the desk. I gave him the other mug, and he sat back against the wall of the cabin with his one boot off and the other half off and drank it, with his eyes closed.

"I'm going to bed," he said. "As soon as this is gone. I'm waiting on the Parisian. I sent her something this afternoon and she's to confirm it, and then I'm going to bed, and nobody is to wake me until we're in Gibraltar."

"Yes, sir."

"Don't call me sir." He opened his eyes. "Cape Cod's been at it all evening. Messages for the Titanic, piles of them. Private traffic. Rich men on board telling other rich men where to put their money." He tapped the headphones. "She's too far out for Cape Cod to reach. Phillips has been working Cape Race all evening with his own pile, and nobody's told him this lot is sitting there."

"Is she really as big as they say, sir? The Titanic?"

I want to be careful here, Ms. Barnett, because I have gone over this a great many times, and I want to tell it to you exactly as it was and no better.

I asked him that because I wanted to keep him talking.

That's all. I didn't know anything. I was a boy who couldn't sleep, holding a mug of cocoa in a warm room on a cold night, and I didn't want to go back down to the bunk under Stan and lie awake in the dark. If he went to bed, I'd have to go too. So I asked him a question I knew he would like to answer, because all the wireless men were talking about her that month, about her set, the most powerful at sea, and her two operators, who were Marconi men like him and whom he knew by their hands. I asked it to buy myself ten more minutes in that room.

He laughed. He liked the question, as I knew he would.

"Bigger," he said. "She's the biggest thing ever put on water. Phillips and Bride are on her. Jack Phillips. He's the senior man. They've been working that set flat out since Southampton, poor devils, every millionaire on board wanting to send a telegram to his mother." He drank his cocoa and looked at the desk, at the pile of forms from Cape Cod, all addressed to the same ship. "You know what, Bertie. I'll tell her. She can't hear Cape Cod, but she can hear me. We've been passing the time of day with her on and off since this afternoon, just keeping in touch. I'll tell them there's a pile waiting for them at Cape Cod, so they can have it sent on and not wonder why their passengers' answers never came." He set the mug down and leaned across to the desk, his one boot still on, the laces trailing. "Do a fellow operator a good turn."

He put his hand on the key.

* * *

I couldn't read what he sent. Not then. It was only a stutter of sound to me, his wrist moving, the spark crackling in the box behind the wall. He sent it, and sat back, and reached for his cocoa.

And then his hand stopped.

It stopped just short of the mug. I saw it. I had been watching his face, because I always watched faces, and I saw it change. Not much, at first. The way a man's face changes when he hears his name called in a crowd and isn't yet sure it was his. His head tilted. His other hand came up and pressed the headphones tighter to his ears.

Then he grabbed the pencil.

He wrote very fast, on the pad, in capitals, and I stood in the door and watched the pencil, because I could read that. I could read it upside down, as it went.

*COME AT ONCE. WE HAVE STRUCK A BERG. ITS A CQD OM.*

And then a position, in numbers. Latitude and longitude.

He sat and looked at it. He did not move for perhaps two seconds, and I will tell you that in all the lives I have lived I have seen a great many people receive bad news, and I have never seen it land on anyone as completely as it landed on Harold Cottam that night, sitting on the edge of his bunk with one boot off.

Then his hand went to the key again, very fast.

"What are you sending?" I said. My voice came out of Bertie high and thin.

"Asking if they're sure. Asking if I should tell my captain." He was listening hard. He wrote again, three words.

*YES COME QUICK.*

He was on his feet before the pencil hit the desk. He tore the headphones off, and the sheet off the pad, and he went out of that door past me so fast that I felt the air of it on my face. He was running along the boat deck in his shirtsleeves, with one boot laced and one boot flapping, toward the bridge.

I went after him. I still had the cocoa in my hand. I don't know why I didn't put it down. I think the body was holding it.

* * *

The bridge was dark and enormous and full of cold. The officer of the watch was the first officer, Mr. Dean. He was standing by the wheelhouse with his hands behind his back, and he turned when he heard the running feet, and saw the Marconi man in his shirtsleeves with a paper in his hand, and a bell boy behind him with a mug.

Cottam pushed the paper at him. Dean read it under the shaded lamp, and I watched his face do what Cottam's had done, only faster, because he was an officer and had been trained to let it.

He didn't say anything. He went. He went straight down the ladder toward the captain's quarters with Cottam at his heels, and I went after them both, and nobody stopped me, because nobody saw me. A boy on a ship at night is furniture.

Dean knocked once and opened the door.

The captain was asleep. He woke the way a man wakes who has been woken at sea before, all at once, up on one elbow in his bunk, and his first words were angry ones. He didn't like his door opened without leave, and he said so, sharply, to both of them.

Then Cottam told him.

I was standing in the passage, behind them, in the dark. I saw the captain's face in the light from the corridor. I saw the anger go out of it, all at once, like a lamp being turned down, and something else come into it, and settle, and stay.

"Are you sure?" he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Absolutely certain?"

"Yes, sir."

He was already getting up. He was in his nightshirt, a slight man with bare feet on a cold floor, and he was giving orders before his feet had found his slippers. Turn the ship. Steer north-west. He would work out the course himself and bring it to the bridge. Call the chief engineer. Call out another watch of stokers. He was pulling his trousers on while he spoke. He did not raise his voice once, and he did not say anything twice.

Then he turned around, buttoning his shirt, and saw me.

I don't know what he saw. A bell boy in socks, with no cap, holding a mug of cocoa, in the passage outside his cabin at half past midnight, where no bell boy had any business to be. He looked at me for exactly as long as it took to decide I was useful.

"You," he said. "What's your name?"

"Pike, sir."

"Pike. Do you know where the chief steward sleeps?"

"Yes, sir."

"Go and wake him. Tell him the captain wants him in the chart room at once. At once. Then wake the purser and tell him the same. Then come back here." He looked at the mug in my hand. "And put that down somewhere."

I put it down on the floor of the passage, very carefully, and ran.

* * *

I ran down the stairs I had polished that afternoon, and along the corridor on B deck, in my socks, faster than I had run in that body or perhaps any body, and as I ran I felt the ship begin to lean.

She was turning. Under my feet, slowly, all thirteen thousand tons of her, she was leaning over into the turn, the way a big ship does when the helm is put hard across, and the long red carpet tilted under me, and the electric lights in their little glass shades swung out on their chains and stayed there, all of them, all down the corridor, leaning together toward the north.

I have thought about those ten minutes for a hundred and fifteen years, Ms. Barnett.

I'll tell you what I know, and what I don't. I know Harold Cottam was going to bed. He told me so. I know he was waiting on the Parisian, and that he had the headphones on while he took his boots off, and that he was the one who decided to call the Titanic, out of kindness, to save two tired men some work. Those are his. Nobody gave them to him.

What I don't know is whether he would still have been sitting there, with his headphones on, if a bell boy had not come up the ladder with a mug of cocoa and asked him whether the Titanic was really as big as they said. Whether he would have drunk his cocoa a little faster without me, and finished his laces, and put the headphones on the hook, and lain down, five minutes before the call came. Or whether he would have sat there anyway, exactly as long, waiting on the Parisian, and called her anyway, and I was only a boy in the room.

I don't know. I have never known. I never will.

I had done a great many things in a great many lives on purpose, and some of them had worked and most of them had not. That night I did nothing on purpose at all. I carried a cup of cocoa up a ladder because I couldn't sleep.

And I have never been sure, in all the years since, whether it was the most important thing I have ever done, or nothing.
