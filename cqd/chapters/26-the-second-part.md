# CQD

## Chapter 26: The Second Part

The money came on a Wednesday.

This wasn't the regular money. The six thousand for each Thursday had come on Mondays since January, and on a Saturday once in March, and it had come that Monday too. This came on the Wednesday, the same Wednesday as Frederick, at a little after eleven in the morning, while I was at the kitchen table writing four hundred words about a dentist's new whitening service. My phone buzzed on the table. It was the bank.

It was more than the twenty thousand in the winter. It was more than I had made in the two years before the letter came, put together. And it had not come from Aldine, Mercer and Voss. It had come by wire from a bank in a different city, one I had never been to either, a long way from the firm's, and the line in the note was one I had read once before, in February, on the night of the courier and the Carpathia card.

*With our client's thanks, at the close of the second part.*

I looked at it until the screen went dark. Then I texted Freda, at school, because we had terms, and the terms said money too.

*Client paid. A lot. Tell you tonight. Not worried. Just telling you.*

She wrote back in under a minute, from what I very much hoped was the hallway between classes.

*ok. thank you for telling me. how much is a lot*

*Tonight.*

*thats a LOT a lot isnt it*

* * *

The card came that night, by courier, at a quarter to nine.

The same young man in the same reflective jacket. He knew my name by then. He said, "Evening, Ms. Barnett," and held out the tablet for my finger, and the envelope, and said, "Have a good one," and was gone.

Freda was at the kitchen table. She didn't say *open it.* She waited, with her hands folded on top of the closed laptop, and watched me do it.

One card. No letter. Heavy cream paper, the same as every time.

I could barely read it.

The hand had gone. That's the only way I can say it. The shapes were still the old schooled shapes, the long loops, the lean to the right, but they wandered now. They drifted off the line and came back. The pen had stopped and started so many times that the words were made of pieces. It had been written very slowly by someone who could no longer hold a pen properly and had refused to let anyone else hold it for him.

It took me a long minute, under the kitchen light, to make it out.

*Please ask him about New York. 1780.*

Freda read it upside down. She didn't say anything for a while.

"He's really sick," she said at last. Quietly.

"Yes."

"Like, really sick."

"I think so."

She looked at the card. Then she looked at me.

"Mom," she said. "What happened in New York in 1780?"

* * *

I read it to him on the porch.

It was the first week of May, and warm, properly warm, the first day of it. He was sitting on the bench in his shirtsleeves when I drove up, with the cardigan folded beside him, and he didn't get up. He raised one hand from the rail, which was as good as coming down the steps, and I came up and sat in the chair.

I gave him the card first. Before the words. He took it and held it in the sun, and looked at the hand for a long time, as he had in the winter, the long strokes, the stops, the places where the pen had wandered off the line.

"He wrote this in bed," he said quietly. "Propped up. With the card on a tray, or a book. You can see where it slipped." He touched one place, where a letter dragged sideways. "And somebody offered to write it for him. And he wouldn't let them." He was quiet. "I know that look on a page. I've written like that myself, once or twice. Toward the end."

He didn't say the end of what.

Then he read the words.

I watched his face. I had watched it for every card. Eleven seconds, the first day. Forty, at the Carpathia. This time I didn't count, and I didn't need to, because what came into his face wasn't stillness at all.

He smiled. Slowly. Not happily, quite. The smile of a man who has just been dealt a card he knew was in the deck, and had been waiting for, and wishes he hadn't been.

"Ah," he said.

"Were you there?"

"Yes."

"New York. 1780."

"Yes." He set the card down on the bench beside him, very gently, face up. "It was a war, Ms. Barnett. The same one everyone learns in school. Only I was on the other side of it."

"The British?"

"The losing one," he said. "As it turned out." He looked out over the field, which was green now, all of it, a low green fuzz over the furrows all the way to the trees. "I spent the best part of two years in that city hunting a spy. And I lost. And then there was a country, partly because I lost." He was quiet a moment. "I've never decided how I feel about that. Two hundred and forty-seven years, and I've never decided."

"Is that what you'll tell me? Next?"

"If you'll come," he said. "If you'd still like to."

* * *

I told him about Gerald Whitcombe, then. I had been saving it.

I told him about the catalog, and the foreword, and the apprentice of many years whose hands were already better than his. I didn't say my source's name, even then, and he didn't ask. I told him about the stone house on forty acres in the hunt country, and the talk every spring on the enemies of paper, and the laugh at thirty-one minutes. I told him about Mrs. Dunleavy with her eyes closed and her hands folded on the composition book, and *That's him,* and her small cold hand on top of Freda's.

He listened to all of it without moving. At the end he was quiet for a long time.

"His teacher," he said.

"Yes."

"The man who taught him the trade. Who'd known him since he was a boy, very likely. Who he'd have trusted with anything." He shook his head, slowly. "If anybody was finding him a place to live, that week, it would be somebody like that. Somebody you'd let choose for you, when you were more frightened than you'd ever been."

"If," I said.

"If," he agreed.

"And you haven't gone to see him."

"No," I said. "Not yet."

He looked at me, and I saw him work out why, and smile, a little.

"Who was driving," he said.

"That's exactly what I said."

"Of course it is." He touched the pocket of the folded cardigan beside him. "Freda wrote again." "She writes a very good letter. Short. She doesn't waste words." He looked at me sideways. "She says her father fixed your kitchen tap."

I felt my face go hot.

"It's dripped for nine years," I said. "Since he left, actually. He knew exactly what was wrong with it. He had it fixed in twenty minutes. With a part he'd brought with him, in his pocket, because he remembered."

"And you checked it."

"Afterward. Yes. Twice."

"And?"

"It doesn't drip," I said.

He laughed. He laughed until he had to hold onto the rail, there on the bench in the first warm sun of May, and after a moment, I did too.

* * *

He didn't go in, that day. Neither did I. We did the whole of session twenty-eight on the porch, the recorders on the little table between us, with the field going green in front of us and the birds back at the feeder, and he didn't tell me about New York. Not yet. He said he wanted to think about where to start. He said there were people in it he hadn't thought about properly in a very long time, and he wanted to do them justice.

So instead he told me about the boy on the pier. About Bertie. What happened to him after the bag and the street door and the rain, which he had said was another Thursday, and which turned out to be this one. I'm not going to put it here. It isn't part of this story. I'll tell you only that he made the best of it, as he said he would, and that it took him a very long time, and that somewhere in Bootle, in a box of family papers, there are letters to a mother in a careful boy's hand that never once mention the Carpathia.

At three he said that was enough. He stood up, slowly, with his hand on the rail, and picked up the cardigan, and the card from the bench, and held them both.

"Next Thursday, Ms. Barnett," he said.

"Next Thursday."

I went down the steps. At the bottom I turned around, because I had nearly forgotten.

"Should I bring the coat?" I said. "For New York."

He looked at me from the top of the steps, in his shirtsleeves, with the card in his hand, and the sun on him, and the empty frame on the mantel behind him through the open door, its glass polished clean, still waiting.

"No," he said. "You won't need the coat for this one." He smiled. "It was summer. And everybody was lying."
