# CQD

## Chapter 14: Spring Break

Freda changed her clothes three times before we left.

I didn't say anything. I sat in the car in the driveway with the engine running and the heater on and waited, and when she came out the third time, in the jeans she'd started with and a sweater of mine that she had stopped asking to borrow sometime around Christmas, she got in and put her feet on the dashboard and said, "Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

We drove north. The trees along the highway had gone a faint, uncertain green, the color of a thing that hasn't made up its mind yet, and past Frederick the fields were plowed and dark and wet, with gulls following the tractors. Freda didn't put her headphones on. She sat with her phone face down on her knee and watched it all go by.

"What do I call him?" she said, somewhere past the state line.

"Mr. Hale."

"He calls you Ms. Barnett. Still. After like six months." She thought about it. "That's so weird. That's like a guy in a movie about a butler."

"He's from another time."

"Mom," said Freda, "that's literally the whole question."

* * *

He was on the porch when we came up the gravel road. No umbrella, for once. It wasn't raining. He was standing at the top of the steps in his cardigan, buttoned wrong by one, with his hands behind his back, and when the car stopped he came down the steps one at a time with his hand on the rail and stood at the bottom to wait for us, and I realized that he had been waiting at the window for a long time.

Freda got out of the car slowly. She stood by the door and looked at him, and he looked at her, and for a moment neither of them said anything.

Then he put out his hand.

"Miss Barnett," he said.

"Freda," said Freda. She shook it. "Nobody calls me Miss Barnett. Not even at school."

"Freda, then." He didn't let go of her hand at once. He held it, and looked at her, and I saw his face do something I had only seen it do once before, on the porch the week before, when I asked him if she could come. "I'm Tom. I've heard a great deal about you."

"I've heard a great deal about *you*," said Freda. "Like, hours. I've typed about forty hours of you."

"I'm so sorry."

She laughed. It surprised her. It was the real laugh, the one she used to have when she was small, before she learned the careful one, and I had not heard it in a long time.

* * *

He made the eggs.

He made them the slow way, in a pan over a low flame, stirring all the time with a wooden spoon, and he made toast from the crying pillow, and he put a jar of marmalade on the table that I had never seen before and that I suspected he had bought that week in town for the occasion. Freda sat at the kitchen table with her chin on her hand and watched him do all of it as if she were watching a magic trick, trying to see where the card went.

"Mom says you do a thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"Where you look at somebody and tell them stuff about themselves. Like the guy on TV. Where they were, what they had for lunch." She tilted her head. "Do it to me."

He turned around from the stove with the spoon in his hand and looked at her. Freda sat up very straight and looked back, ready, as she used to stand at the plate in Little League.

He looked at her for what felt like a long time. Then he turned back to the pan.

"No," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because your mother tells it better than I do it," he said. "And because it's rude." He stirred the eggs. "And because you'd spend the rest of the morning wondering what I'd seen that I hadn't said. People always do. It spoils the eggs."

Freda looked at me. I shrugged. She looked back at him, at his back, at the cardigan buttoned wrong, and I saw her decide something about him, and file it, and I recognized the look, because it was mine.

* * *

She asked him about the song over breakfast.

"You hum it all the time," she said. "On the tapes. Every one. When you're drawing. Did you know?"

"Yes," he said. "I've been told before."

"What is it, really? Mom found it online. It's like the oldest song in the world."

"It's an old song," he said.

"You always say that."

"Then it must be true." He buttered a piece of toast and gave it to her, without asking, as you would for a child, and she took it without noticing, as a child does. "Would you like to hear it properly? Not on the tape."

Freda nodded.

So he hummed it, there at the kitchen table, over the plates, in his thin old voice, all the way through. It took less than a minute. It went up, and turned, and came down, and turned back on itself, and ended almost where it began. Freda sat very still with the toast in her hand. And the second time through, so quietly I almost didn't hear it, she hummed it with him. She had learned it off the tapes.

He stopped, and looked at her.

"You've got a good ear," he said. "Your mother told me."

Freda did the thing with her mouth, the small pressed thing, and looked at her plate.

* * *

My phone rang at a quarter past ten.

I almost didn't answer. It was a number I didn't know, a city exchange. But I had been waiting two months for a number I didn't know, so I took it out onto the porch and shut the door behind me.

It was a woman at the police department's records unit. She had the voice of somebody who had been reading out the same sentences all morning. My request for the complete file in the missing persons case had been processed, she said, and approved in part. Some material was withheld under the usual exemptions. The rest, including the responding officer's notes and the photographs taken at the scene, was ready to be collected in person, with identification, at the records office downtown, any weekday between nine and four.

"Photographs," I said.

"Fourteen photographs," said the woman. "And two pages of notes. You'll need to sign for them."

I said I would come on Monday. I stood on the porch for a while after she'd hung up, with the phone in my hand, looking at the field. Fourteen photographs. I had been in that apartment once, six years ago, and a cat had sat on my notebook. On Monday I was going to walk through it again.

It was cold on the porch, and I hadn't brought my coat out. Through the kitchen window beside me I could see the two of them at the table.

I didn't mean to watch. I want to say that. But I was a reporter for a long time, and a mother for longer, and the window was right there.

Freda was talking. She had her elbows on the table and her hands around her mug, and she was talking to him, quickly, looking down at the mug, and then up at him, and then down again. I couldn't hear anything through the glass. He was sitting very still across from her, just as he sat when I talked about Carrow, with his hands folded on the table and his head a little on one side, listening. Not interrupting. Not doing anything at all but letting her talk.

Then she stopped. She put one hand up, quickly, and wiped under her eye with the heel of it, the way she had done since she was small when she didn't want anyone to see.

He said something. Not much. A sentence, perhaps two.

Freda looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed, a small wet surprised laugh, and shook her head, and picked up her toast.

I went back in.

* * *

They both looked up when the door opened. I came across the kitchen, rubbing my arms against the cold, and as I came I heard the end of what he was saying, quietly, to her.

"...they don't get lighter, Freda. That's the thing nobody tells you. They get heavier, every day you carry them. And sooner or later, somebody else ends up carrying them for you."

Freda had her eyes on him. "Are you talking about me?"

"I'm talking about a notebook," he said. And then he looked up and saw me, and smiled, and said, "Was that your police file?"

"Yes. How did you know?"

"You went out with your shoulders up and came back with them down," he said. "And you said *photographs* on the porch loud enough to hear through a window." He got up to take my plate. "Good news?"

I told him. Fourteen photographs. Monday. He nodded slowly, and said that he'd like to hear about them, if I wanted to tell him, and put the plate in the sink.

"What did I miss?" I said, to the room.

Freda looked at her toast.

"We were talking about Bertie," said Tom. "His sisters, in Bootle. Freda wanted to know if he ever went home."

"Did he?"

"That's another Thursday," he said, and ran the water.

I looked at Freda. Freda looked at me, and then out of the window, at the field, and her face was perfectly calm and her eyes were a little red, and I knew my daughter, and I knew she was not going to tell me anything, and I let it go.

I let it go. I have thought about that since.

* * *

We went through to the living room at eleven. He sat in his chair and settled the notepad on his knee. I set out the recorders on the little table and said the date into them, and the time, and *Session twenty-five,* and then, because it was true and because it was going on the tape forever, *Freda Barnett present.*

Freda had not taken the chair I'd pulled up for her. She was sitting on the stairs, on the third step, among the books that climbed up them one to a step, with her knees drawn up and her arms around them. From there she could see him, and he could see her.

"Thursday," he said. "The last day."

He looked at Freda, on the stairs.

"I'm going to tell you how I lost," he said. "It's a long story. You might want to get comfortable."

"I'm comfortable," said Freda.

He nodded. He picked up the pencil, and without looking down he began to draw the star, around and around, and began.
