# CQD

## Chapter 18: Fourteen Photographs

The records office was in the basement of a building downtown that I had walked past a thousand times, back when I worked six blocks away, and never once gone into.

There was a counter with a scratched plastic window, and a ticket machine, and eleven chairs bolted together in a row, and a television on the wall showing a cooking show with the sound off. I took a ticket. I was number forty-one. They were serving number thirty-three. I sat in one of the bolted chairs with my bag on my knees and watched a man on the television fold egg whites into something, very slowly, for twenty minutes.

When they called forty-one, a woman behind the plastic took my license and my letter and looked at both for a long time, and went away, and came back with a manila envelope, the big kind, sealed with tape. She slid a form under the window for me to sign. I signed it. She slid the envelope after it.

"Fourteen photographs," she said, "and two pages of notes. Some material withheld under the exemptions on the attached sheet. You can appeal within thirty days."

"Thank you."

"You're the third person this year," she said, "who's come in for a file this old." She was already looking past me, at forty-two. "Most people don't bother. Most people just let it go."

* * *

I didn't open it there. I didn't open it in the elevator, or on the sidewalk, or in the car. I drove to a coffee place I used to go to, on the corner across from where the paper's building had been, which was a bank now, and I bought a coffee I didn't want, and sat at a table in the back by the window, and opened the envelope.

The notes first. Two pages, typed, in the flat language of a man who had a great many of these to do that week. I had read the two-page report so many times I could have recited it. These were the notes behind it, the officer's own, and they weren't much longer, and they weren't much better. *Landlord states tenant not seen since Wed approx. Tenant owes 2 wks rent. Movers booked Sat. Cat in BR, landlord removed.* Then, near the bottom of the second page, a line I had never seen.

*Neighbor 2F (Dunleavy) called station x2 re: noise Wed a.m. Msg passed to R/O. Not interviewed. See landlord stmt.*

I sat and looked at that line for a long time.

*Not interviewed.* She had called them twice. They had passed it on. And the responding officer, whose name was on the top of both pages, had read the message and written it down, very neatly, and gone to talk to the landlord about the carpet.

I thought of Mrs. Dunleavy in her wheelchair by the window, with her hands folded on the crocheted blanket. *Nobody ever calls me back.*

Then I opened the photographs.

* * *

They were color prints, glossy, four by six, each one with a number written on the back in marker and a date stamp in the corner. Fourteen of them. I laid them out on the table in order, in two rows of seven, and moved my coffee to the windowsill so I wouldn't spill it on them, and looked.

The first three were the building. The front, with the dry cleaner's sign and the steam coming out of a vent at the side. The front door. And the stairs, from the bottom, looking up. A narrow staircase with a wooden rail, and the walls painted a pale clean cream, fresh, glossy, the kind of paint that shows everything.

I picked up the third one and held it closer, because Tom had told me to look at that wall.

All the way up the left-hand wall of the staircase, at about the height of a man's waist, there was a line of marks. Scuffs, and dents, and in two places small gouges where the cream paint had come away and the old green showed through underneath. Every few steps. All the way up, as far as the photograph went.

*Something banging the wall all the way down. New paint!!*

It didn't say what had made the marks, or exactly when. Anybody moving anything up or down those stairs could have done it. But it was new paint, and it was marked all the way up, and a woman on the second floor had written down the day she heard it happen.

I put it down very carefully, as if it might break.

Four through eight were the living room. It was smaller than I remembered, and more ordinary. A brown couch with a blanket folded on the arm. A rug. A small television on a stand. A bookshelf, full, the books shelved by size. And the wall over the couch, with the photographs on it.

There were seven. I counted. Seven frames in a careful arrangement, the kind a person measures and marks in pencil before they put in a single nail. An older woman in a sun hat, laughing, in a garden. That would be his mother. A young man in a cap and gown with the same woman beside him, not laughing, very proud. A lake with a dock. A dog. A church. A group of people at a long table, a wedding or a birthday, too small to see faces.

And one more, at the end. Larger than the others, in a better frame.

I picked up photograph number seven and held it right up to my face, there at the table in the back of the coffee place, and I didn't care who saw.

It was him. My source. Bookbinder. In a dark suit that didn't fit him, standing in front of a pair of tall brass doors I would have known anywhere in the world, under a banner I could almost read: *CARROW HOUSE*, and something else, smaller, *SPRING SALE*, and a year. He was smiling. Shyly, as he used to smile at me across the diner table when he'd brought me something good and didn't want to seem proud of it.

And beside him, with one arm around his shoulders, was another man.

Older. Sixty, perhaps, or a fit seventy. Tall, heavy-shouldered, with a fine head of white hair swept back, and a bow tie, and a beautifully cut gray suit. He had his head thrown back. He was laughing. Not smiling. Laughing, with his whole body, his mouth wide open, as if somebody just out of the frame had said the funniest thing he had ever heard. And his arm was around my source's shoulders, hard, the way a proud father holds a son at a graduation. The way you hold someone who belongs to you.

I didn't know him. I sat with the photograph in my hand and went through every face I could remember from eight months of Carrow House, every name in every document, every lawyer, every executive in every annual report, and I didn't know him.

And I thought of the laugh. I had never heard it. I had only a furious little line in a composition book.

*A big one. Too big. The kind of laugh a man has when he's used to people laughing with him.*

A man laughing in a photograph at a party proves nothing. Half the men at any party are laughing in the pictures. I knew that. I looked at him anyway, for a long time.

* * *

The rest I went through quickly, because I couldn't help it, and then slowly, because I made myself.

Nine was the desk. A plain wooden desk under the window, with a lamp and a mug of pens. And on the desk, in the middle, a big pale rectangle in the dust, perfectly clean, with a power strip on the floor beneath it and three cables hanging down off the back of the desk into nothing. You could see exactly where the computer had been, and the scanner beside it. The officer had stood in front of that and written one word. *Desk.*

Ten was the closet by the door, opened. Coats on hooks. Shoes on the floor. And at the back of the floor, two more rectangles in the dust, side by side, the size and shape of banker's boxes.

Eleven and twelve were the bedroom. A bed, unmade. A window. A water bowl on the floor by the door, empty, and a litter box in the corner, and the door itself, from inside, with long pale scratches down the paint at the bottom where something with claws had tried for two days to get out.

Thirteen was the kitchen. Dishes in the sink. A cereal box on the counter. A mug, by the kettle, with a teabag in it, ready, as if he'd put it out to have when he got back.

Fourteen was the bookshelf again, closer. I don't know why the officer took it. Perhaps he thought he ought to take one more. On the middle shelf, between two books, there was a small gray stuffed mouse with one ear chewed off.

I put them all back in the envelope, in order, and closed it, and sat with both hands flat on top of it, and looked out of the window at the bank where the paper used to be, until the coffee was cold.

* * *

I called Freda from the car. It was ten past four. She would be home by now.

It went to voicemail. I texted. *On my way. Want anything from the store?*

The answer came a minute later. *at maddies, back by 7. no thx*

I sat in the car with the engine off and the envelope on the passenger seat, and I looked at the text, and I thought about how long it had been since I'd seen Maddie's mother. Not since the lab. Not since she called me in the winter, very quiet, to apologize for something she couldn't name. I had meant to call her back. I never had. And Maddie's house was on the way home, almost. And I had a car, and a free hour, and a daughter at someone else's kitchen table eating someone else's food.

I called Maddie's mother.

She answered on the second ring, and she sounded surprised, and then pleased, and then, when I said I was nearby and thought I'd pick Freda up and save her the bus, she didn't say anything at all for a moment.

"Freda?" she said.

"Yes. She's there, isn't she? With Maddie?"

"Iola." Her voice had gone careful. "Maddie's at her dad's this week. In Baltimore. She's been there since Saturday." A pause. "And Freda hasn't been over here since... well. Not since the lab. Not once. I thought you knew. I thought, after everything, you'd rather she didn't."

I said something. I don't know what. I said thank you, I think, and that I must have got the day wrong, and that we should have coffee, and she said yes, we should, in the voice people use when they know they never will.

I hung up.

I sat in my car in the parking garage under the building where my career had ended, with fourteen photographs of a man who had gone out one morning and never come back on the seat beside me, and my daughter's text on the screen in my hand.

*at maddies, back by 7.*

And under it, in my memory, as clear as if I were reading it off the page: *Maddie's sister was getting rid of them.* And *Maddie's sister drives that way.*

Maddie didn't have a sister. Maddie had two older brothers, and no sister at all. I had known that for eleven years. I had even made a little joke, in my own head, in the car on the way to Frederick, about how generous Maddie's sister had become. I had known it the whole time.

I had just never checked.
