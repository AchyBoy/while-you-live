# CQD

## Chapter 22: The End of the Bed

Freda typed the pier on Sunday night.

I didn't ask her to. I had not asked her to do anything since Monday. On Saturday the laptop was still closed on the kitchen table where she'd left it, and on Sunday afternoon it was still closed, and I didn't touch it, and I didn't say anything. And at about eight o'clock on Sunday night I came into the kitchen for a glass of water and she was sitting there with the headphones on and the recorder file open on the screen, typing.

I got my water and went back to my room. I didn't say anything then, either.

I could hear her, through the wall, for a long time. Not the words. Only the keys, very fast, and then stopping, and then the little click of the trackpad when she went back to listen again. Then fast again. Then stopping. Then, for a while, nothing at all.

I was in bed with a book I wasn't reading when the door opened, a little before midnight.

She didn't knock. She hadn't knocked on my door since she was nine. She stood in the doorway in her pajamas and one of my old sweaters with her arms wrapped around herself, and her face was blotched, and she didn't come in.

"I finished it," she said.

"Okay."

"The pier." Her voice was thick. "The boy with the bag. Nobody came for him. Everybody else had somebody, behind the ropes, and he just sat on his bag by the door in the rain. Twelve. By himself. In New York."

"I know."

"And Mr. Ainsley had his whole family." She swallowed. "And everybody laughed."

"I know, honey."

She stood in the doorway a moment longer. Then she came in, and sat down on the end of my bed, at the very end, on top of the covers, with her back against the footboard and her knees drawn up, the way she used to sit when she was small and had a bad dream and didn't want to admit it was a bad dream.

"I'm going to tell you all of it," she said. "Okay? The parts you didn't find."

* * *

"It was me," she said. "First. Not him."

I didn't say anything.

"In September. Before the letter came. You were out, and I was looking for my birth certificate for the driving thing at school, and it was in your box in the closet, the one with all my stuff. The baby teeth and the drawings." She looked at her knees. "And the card was in there. The one from Phoenix. The one that came in March."

I had kept it. Of course I had. I keep everything.

"It had a return address," said Freda. "So I looked him up. That's all. I just looked him up, like you would. And he wasn't in Phoenix anymore. He was here. He'd been back in DC for like a year. He was on the website for the hardware store on Georgia, in one of those staff pictures, in the apron. *Dez, plumbing and electrical.*" She almost laughed. "He's really good at plumbing, apparently."

"He always was."

"So I called the store. And asked for him. And he came to the phone, and I said, *it's Freda,* and he didn't say anything for like ten seconds." She pulled the sleeves of the sweater down over her hands. "And then he hung up."

I sat up a little.

"He hung up on me, Mom. I was so mad. I sat on my bed and cried for an hour." She shook her head. "And then a week later he called back. From his own phone. And he said he was sorry, he'd panicked, he didn't think he had any right to talk to me. He said he'd been sober two years and three months, and he'd started over, and he'd thought about calling me every day for two years, and every day he decided I was better off. And then I called him." She looked up at me. "And he said, *Okay. Then I guess I don't get to decide that anymore.*"

That was the phone call, in the fall. That was the low, careful laugh through the wall, the night the electric bill was red. That was the door in her that had stayed shut.

"And in October," said Freda, "the day he gave me his real number. I wrote it in the margin of my chem worksheet. Really carefully. Because I was scared I'd lose my phone and never find it again." She almost smiled. "You saw me do it. At the table. You looked right at me."

I had. I remembered it. She had read a text and her face had gone still and shut, and she had written something very carefully in the margin, and I had decided it was about Maddie, and the lab, and the kids who were being cruel to her. I had been right about the kids. I had been wrong about the margin.

"Why didn't you tell me," I said. "Then. In October. When you knew he was real."

"Because he asked me not to." She held up a hand before I could say anything. "Not like that. Not like *don't tell your mother.* He said..." She stopped, and got it right. "He said you'd been carrying everything by yourself for nine years. Me, and the house, and the money, and the story, and losing your job. All of it. He said he'd put most of it on you, by leaving. And he wasn't going to hand you one more thing to carry until he could carry his own. Until he had a real job that lasted, and his own place, and he'd been sober long enough that you'd believe it." She wiped her face with her sleeve. "He said when he could walk up to the door himself, he would. And I said okay. Because I wanted it. I wanted to have him for a while, by myself, before it got complicated."

"It was always going to be complicated."

"I know." She looked at her knees again. "I know that now."

* * *

"When I asked you," she said after a while, quietly. "In the kitchen. If people can tell when they've made a difference to somebody."

"I remember."

"He told me I did. Made a difference. To him." She pulled at a thread on the sweater. "He said when I called, he'd been thinking about drinking again. For the first time in like a year. Just that week. He'd had a bad week at work and he was standing outside a liquor store on Kennedy Street and he got the call from the store manager saying some girl named Freda had called asking for him." She laughed, a small wet sound. "He said he went back to work instead. And he hasn't been back to that store since."

I couldn't say anything for a moment.

"I didn't know if that was true," Freda said. "Or if he just said it. Like, to make me feel good. You can't check something like that."

"No," I said. "You can't."

"Mr. Hale said that's the worst kind. The kind you can't check." She looked at me. "He said that about the cocoa, too. Didn't he. That's the same thing. He never knew if he made a difference. And Dad says I did, and I'll never know either."

"Sometimes," I said, "you just have to decide whether to believe somebody."

Freda looked at me for a long time.

"You're doing that," she said. "With Mr. Hale. Aren't you. Every Thursday."

I didn't answer that. I didn't have to. She knew.

* * *

I told her about the money, then.

I hadn't planned to. It came out of me because she had told me everything, all of it, sitting on the end of my bed in my sweater at midnight, and I was sitting there with something I hadn't told her, and it had gotten heavy.

"The client," I said. "In January, the payments doubled. Six thousand a Thursday, not three, and they've stayed that way. I never told you. And the twenty thousand in February, on top of that, the night the Carpathia card came. I never told you what that was for, because I didn't know. I still don't." I looked at my hands. "I didn't tell you because I didn't know what it meant, and I didn't want you to worry about it, and I wanted to keep it until I understood it."

Freda stared at me.

"Mom," she said. "That's literally what I said. About Dad. Word for word."

"I know."

"You were doing the exact same thing."

"I know," I said. "I heard it. While you were saying it."

She looked at me, and then she started to laugh. Not the careful laugh. Not the real one either, quite. Something in between, wet and helpless and a little hysterical, at midnight, on the end of the bed. After a moment I was laughing too. I don't know why. It wasn't funny. We sat there and laughed until she had to wipe her face again, and so did I.

"We're so bad at this," said Freda.

"We're terrible at it."

"We should be better. We're like, professionally good at finding out stuff."

"Other people's stuff," I said.

* * *

We worked out the terms, there on the bed, at half past midnight, like two lawyers. I insisted on that. She insisted on writing them down, on the back of a gas bill from my bedside table, in her neat small handwriting, because she said I'd try to change them in the morning.

She could see him. Not secretly. I would know where, and when, every time. I would meet him first, myself, by myself, somewhere public, in daylight, and we'd talk. Not about her. About him. If I didn't like what I heard, we'd talk about that too, all three of us. No more lies, in either direction. Not about him, and not about money, and not about anything.

"And not about Mr. Hale," said Freda.

"What about him?"

"You have to go back." She was writing it down. "Thursday. And not be mad."

"I'm allowed to be mad."

"You can be mad. You have to go back, though." She looked up from the gas bill. "He didn't tell you because I asked him not to. Kind of. I mean, I didn't ask, but he could tell. And he made me promise to tell you, and I didn't, all weekend, because I was scared. That's on me. Not him." She held out the pen. "Sign it."

I signed it. She signed it under me. She folded it in half and put it in the pocket of my sweater, which she was wearing, which meant I would never see it again.

She fell asleep there, a little while later, on the end of my bed, on top of the covers, curled up like a cat, the way she used to when she was six. I didn't wake her. I put the blanket from the chair over her and turned off the light and lay in the dark listening to her breathe.

In the morning, before she woke, I took my phone into the kitchen and found the text from the number with the local exchange, and wrote back.

*Coffee. Saturday, ten. The place on the corner of Upshur. You're buying.*

The answer came in less than a minute. I think he had been awake all night too.

*Yes. Thank you. I'll be there at nine.*
