# CQD

## Chapter 10: Two Car Doors

The bakery had closed on Saturday, as promised, and on Thursday he made eggs.

"There's nothing to put soup next to," he said, at the door, by way of explanation. "I tried the bread from the supermarket in town. It's very soft. It's like eating a pillow somebody has been crying into." He took my coat, and looked at the moth hole in the sleeve with something like affection, and hung it on the hook. "So, eggs. Have you eaten?"

I had not. I had left before seven, because I wanted to be early, and I had said it was for the traffic.

We ate at the kitchen table. Scrambled eggs, very soft, as the French do them, with chives from a pot on the windowsill and toast from the crying pillow. He had made too much, and watched me eat it with the expression of a man who does not get to feed people often enough.

Then I took Mrs. Dunleavy's notebook out of my bag and put it on the table between the plates.

He didn't touch it at first. He looked at it. A black and white composition book, the marbled kind, with a date range on the cover in small furious capitals, and a strip of masking tape down the spine where it had been mended.

"You found her," he said.

"She found me. She took the phone off her son." I told him about the split-level, and the ramp, and the plastic tub in the garage with sixty notebooks in it. "You were right. She'd written down everything. Every pipe and every dog, for eight years. She said nobody ever asked her."

"No. They never do." He put out one finger and touched the cover, very lightly, as you would touch something in a museum when the guard was not looking. "May I?"

* * *

He read the Wednesday page for a long time.

I had read it perhaps forty times by then. I had copied it out by hand. I had it nearly by heart. And still I watched him read it, and found that I was holding my breath.

He read it once straight through. Then he went back to the top and read it again, slowly, with his finger moving down the margin, a line at a time, as if it were a contract.

"She's a good witness," he said at last.

"She's a furious witness."

"That's often the same thing. Look." He turned the book so I could see, and put his finger on a line. "*Man's voice, not his.* She doesn't say who she thinks it was. She doesn't guess. She writes down what she heard, and she stops. Most people can't do that. They tell you what it meant, and you have to dig down through the meaning to find out what actually happened." He moved the finger. "She only tells you what it meant once. Here, at the bottom. *He did not come back up.* And she's earned that one."

He sat back.

"Three knocks," he said. "Then two."

"I noticed that."

"Of course you did. What did you make of it?"

"That it's a knock you'd agree on," I said. "Somebody you'd told how to knock. So you'd know it was them and open the door." I had thought about it all week. "He was frightened, that week. I'd told him to stay home. He wouldn't have opened his door to just anybody."

"No. He shut the cat away and opened it to somebody he'd been expecting." He moved his finger down. "And they talked for half an hour first. With a great deal of laughing, on one side. That's not a man who's come to collect something. That's a man who's come to persuade."

"Persuade him of what?"

"To go with him," he said. "With everything." He looked at the next lines, and his face grew very still. "Two trips. Both heavy. The first time, she writes, something banging the wall all the way down. You told me there were two banker's boxes in the closet by the door."

"Yes."

"Two men. One box each. One trip." He tapped the line. "And the banging. A banker's box is light enough to carry in front of you, if you're used to carrying things. A man who bangs it into the wall on every step of a narrow stair, all the way down, is a man who doesn't carry his own boxes, as a rule." He looked up at me. "Then they came back up, quickly. Then down again, heavy, banging. What was the second trip, Ms. Barnett?"

I had been trying to answer that all week. I said what I had come up with, which was nothing: more papers, perhaps. A third box I'd never seen.

He shook his head, very slightly.

"You told me once what the police wrote down, in his apartment. The officer who only wrote down what was in front of him. You read it to me off the report." He waited. "What did the officer write down that wasn't there?"

It took me a moment. Then I had it, and I felt it go through me like cold water.

*Desk. No computer observed.*

"He had a computer," I said. "An old one. A big beige thing, on the desk, with a scanner. He scanned everything. Every document, before he copied it. I saw it the one time I was there. I never even thought about it." I put my hand flat on the table. "It wasn't in the report because it wasn't there. And I always thought, of course it wasn't there, he took it with him. Like the boxes."

"He did take it with him," he said. "Just not the way you thought. He carried it down the stairs himself, with his friend, at half past ten on a Wednesday morning, and put it in the car." He looked at the page again. "Everything. Every copy, every scan, every piece of paper that could prove a word of your story. He carried it all down to the alley with his own hands, and he left his cat behind a closed door, and his family's photographs on the wall, because he meant to come home that night."

"He thought they were helping him," I said.

It came out very quietly. I had not meant to say it out loud.

"I think so," he said. "I think somebody he trusted came and told him the evidence wasn't safe in his apartment, not with Carrow's lawyers and Carrow's people, and they had a place it would be safe. And a place he would be safe, too, when he moved on Saturday, if he'd let them find it for him." He was quiet for a moment. "*Customer to call with address.*"

I did not say anything for a while. The woodstove ticked. Outside, a crow walked along the fence by the field, and stopped, and looked at the house, and walked on.

* * *

"There's one more thing," he said.

I looked up.

"It's a small thing. It may be nothing. It's a reason to ask, that's all." He turned the book around to face him again and put his finger on the last lines. "Look at the car."

I looked. I knew those lines as well as I knew my own name.

*10:14. Back door. Car in alley. Engine running. Exhaust coming in my kitchen window AGAIN.*

*10:21. Two back UP. Quick.*

*10:33. Two DOWN again. Slow. Heavy. Banging.*

*10:41. Car door. Car door. Car gone.*

"The engine was running when they came down the first time," he said. "With the boxes. She smelled the exhaust from her kitchen. Then both of them went back upstairs, quickly, for the computer, and they were up there for twelve minutes. And all that time, the engine was still running."

"People leave engines running."

"They do. In an alley, in the middle of the morning, with nobody in the car, while they go up three flights of stairs and stay twelve minutes, and leave a car full of the only proof of a story the whole city is talking about, unlocked, with the engine running, in an alley." He let that sit. "Some people. Perhaps."

"Or somebody was sitting in it."

"Or somebody was sitting in it." He touched the last line. "And then, at the end. *Car door. Car door.* She heard two doors, Ms. Barnett. Not three. She was listening for doors. She'd been listening to that building for thirty years. If three people had got into that car she'd have written down three doors."

"Two men got in," I said slowly. "And the one who drove was already in it."

"It's a reason to ask," he said again. "That's all it is."

I wrote it down. *Three knocks, two. Persuaded. 2 boxes, then the computer. Engine running 27 min. Somebody in the car? 2 doors.* And under it: *Three people. Not two.*

Then I looked at what I had written, and at Mrs. Dunleavy's furious little capitals beside it, and I thought about a careful man who apologized to the stairs, going down them for the last time between a friend who laughed and a stranger he may never have seen.

* * *

"She said one more thing," I said. "About the other man. The one who laughed. She said he had leather heels. Hard ones. She could hear every step on the stairs. Click, click, click, like a lady, she said. She said you don't hear leather heels much anymore."

He had been reaching for the coffee pot. His hand stopped.

It was only for a second. I would not have noticed it, before. I had spent twenty weeks learning to notice things about him, and I noticed it now. His hand stopped just short of the pot, and stayed, and then went on, and poured.

"Leather heels," he said.

"Does that mean something?"

"It means he was dressed for an office," he said, "on a Wednesday morning, and he wanted to be." He put the pot down. He looked at the window, at the field, where the crow had gone. "It reminds me of something. That's all. Something a long way off."

He didn't say what. I didn't ask. I had learned, by then, that when he looked at the window like that, the answer was usually coming, and usually on tape.

* * *

He asked about Freda when we had cleared the plates. I told him she had come with me to Frederick, and had sat on the couch with the grandson and a can of soda, quiet as a mouse, for an hour.

"And then she asked the one question I didn't," I said. "Whether Mrs. Dunleavy had ever seen him. The other man."

"Had she?"

"No. Only heard him." I dried the last plate. "She was good. Freda. She just sat there and let it happen. Mrs. Dunleavy liked her. She said she had her own mind." I put the plate away. "She even got us around the fun run on Sixteenth Street. Up Georgia and across on Missouri. I didn't know she knew that part of town."

He was folding the dish towel. He folded it in half, and in half again, and hung it on the oven door, and didn't say anything at all.

Then he went through to the living room, and sat in his chair, and picked up the notepad.

"Tuesday," he said, when the recorders were running. "We'll start on the Tuesday. Most of what matters happened on the Tuesday, though none of us knew it."
