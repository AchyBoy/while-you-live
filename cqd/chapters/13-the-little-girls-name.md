# CQD

## Chapter 13: The Little Girl's Name

The card came on Monday night, by courier, at a quarter to nine.

It was the same young man in the same reflective jacket, with the same motorcycle idling at the curb and the same tablet for me to sign with my finger. He said, "Barnett?" and I said yes, and he gave me the envelope, heavy cream paper, *Aldine, Mercer and Voss*, and was gone before I had the door shut.

Freda was at the kitchen table. She had stopped typing when the doorbell went, and she had taken her headphones off, and she was watching me come back down the hall with the envelope just as she had watched me in the winter, when the last one came.

"Is it him?"

"It's the firm."

"It's him," said Freda. "Open it."

There was one card inside. No letter, no typed note. One line, in ink, in the old schooled hand that leaned too far to the right.

It was worse than the last one. I saw that before I had read a word. The tremor in the long strokes had got into all of them now, the short ones too, so that every letter wavered a little, like something seen through water. In two places the pen had stopped, and started again, and left a blot. Whoever had written it had held the pen very hard, and it had taken him a long time.

*Please ask him the little girl's name.*

I read it out. Freda held out her hand, and I gave it to her, and she tilted it under the kitchen light.

"His hand's worse," she said.

"Yes."

"Like, a lot worse." She turned the card over, as if there might be something on the back. There wasn't. "What little girl?"

"In the boat. The one the governess was holding. On the deck, at night, when she hummed." I sat down across from her. "It's in Thursday's tape. You typed it."

"I know who she is. I mean, why her?" Freda put the card down on the table between us, very carefully, as if it might break. "Of everybody on the whole ship, he wants to know her name. Not the bad guy's. Not the governess's. A little kid's."

"He's told me he won't give any names from the boat."

"I know. I typed that too." Freda looked at the card. "So it's like a test. Right? To see if he'll do it."

"Maybe."

"Or to see if he knows it," said Freda.

I didn't say anything to that. I didn't have anything to say. I picked up the card and put it in my notebook, beside the first letter and the Carpathia card, three of them now in a row, the steady hand and the shaking one and this one, which was something worse than shaking.

* * *

On Tuesday I went looking for Mr. Hatch.

I had been putting it off. I knew why. Every name Tom had given me so far that I could check, I had checked, and every one had come back true. Rostron, Cottam, Bride, the Californian, the four buried at four o'clock. But those were famous names, in a famous story, with a hundred books about them. A clever man could have read the books. Hatch was different. Hatch was a nobody. A thin man in a brown suit with a typewriter, going to Naples to write about a volcano. If Hatch had been on the Carpathia, and had died on the Carpathia, there might be nothing anywhere in the world to say so. And if there was nothing, I would never know whether that was because Hatch had never existed, or because nobody had cared.

So I didn't want to look, and I looked.

The Library of Congress has put millions of pages of old American newspapers online, every page photographed, every word run through a machine so you can search it, badly. I sat at the kitchen table after the school run with a cup of coffee going cold and searched April 1912 for *Hatch*, and got four hundred results, most of them about chickens.

I narrowed it down. *Hatch* and *Carpathia*. Nothing. *Hatch* and *fell*. Nothing useful. I tried the papers one at a time, the New York ones, for the days after the Carpathia came in, the nineteenth and the twentieth and the twenty-first, when every page of every paper was about the Titanic and nothing else, and the machine had read the small print so badly it was nearly useless. I read the pages myself. Column by column. It took most of the day.

I found it at a quarter past three, in the New-York Tribune for Saturday, April 20, 1912, on page seven, at the bottom of a long column of short items headed *INCIDENTS OF THE RESCUE SHIP'S VOYAGE.* Between a paragraph about a dog that had not been saved and a paragraph about a collection taken up among the Carpathia's passengers for her crew.

Three lines.

*Among the Carpathia's own passengers, a magazine writer named Lowell Hatch, bound for Naples, was killed on Wednesday night by a fall on a companionway, the decks being made slippery by the fog. His body was brought into port.*

That was all.

I sat at the kitchen table and read it, and read it again, and then I got up and walked around the kitchen, and came back, and read it again. *A magazine writer. Bound for Naples. Wednesday night. A fall on a companionway. The fog.*

Every fact in it was one he'd told me. Nothing in it was anything he couldn't have known if he'd been there. And I had just spent six hours finding it, and I had been very good at finding things, once. If it was anywhere easier, I hadn't found that either.

Or he had found it first. Years ago. On some long night of his own. And built a man around it.

I made myself write that down. I made myself look at it. Then I wrote under it: *Three lines. Brown suit, two fingers, "Good boy." Nobody builds that from three lines.*

And then: *I don't know that.*

* * *

I read him the card on Thursday morning, the twenty-fourth session, before the recorders, at the kitchen table, with the coffee.

He took it out of my hand and held it near the window, in the gray light, and looked at it for a long time. He looked at the handwriting more than at the words. I watched his eyes go along the long strokes, the blots, the places where the pen had stopped and started.

"He's not well," he said quietly.

"No."

"He's worse than in the winter. A good deal worse." He turned the card in his fingers. "And he's in a hurry now. He wasn't, before."

Then he read the words, and something in his face shut, very gently, like a door closed on a sleeping child.

"I told you," he said. "No names from the boat."

"I know. I told him that you'd said that. In the transcript."

"Then he knows." He put the card down on the table. He was quiet for a while. Then he reached past me for the notepad on the windowsill, the one he kept for shopping lists, and tore off the top sheet, and picked up a pencil, and turned his shoulder to me, a little, like a boy in a classroom covering his answers, and wrote something. One word, I thought. Perhaps two. He folded the paper in half, and in half again.

"Have you got an envelope?"

I had. I always had. He put the folded paper inside it, and licked the flap, and sealed it, and pressed it down with his thumb, all the way along. Then he handed it to me.

"Send him that," he said. "Sealed. Not in the transcript. Not typed. Just that."

I looked at the envelope in my hand. Then I looked at him.

"You won't tell me," I said. "But you'll tell him."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I'm not telling him," he said. "Not really." He folded his hands on the table. "Ms. Barnett, if I'm wrong about why he's asking, then it's a little girl's name, nearly a hundred and fifteen years old, sealed in an envelope, and it goes to an old man who will read it once and put it in a drawer, and no harm in the world comes of it. And if I'm right about why he's asking..." He stopped.

"Then what?"

"Then he knows it already," he said. "Better than I do."

He wouldn't say anything else about it. I asked him three ways, as I used to ask a source who had decided to stop talking, and he answered every one of them with that same gentle shut-door look. In the end I put the envelope in my notebook, beside the card, and didn't open it.

I want to be clear about that. I didn't open it. Not then, not in the car, not at the kitchen table that night with a kettle on the stove and nobody watching. I sent it to Aldine, Mercer and Voss by registered mail on Friday morning, sealed, exactly as he'd given it to me, with a short note of my own: *From the gentleman. At his request, not opened.*

When I told Freda, she looked at me as if I had grown a second head.

"You didn't open it?"

"No."

"Mom. You open *everything.* You opened my report card from the eighth grade before I got home and you resealed it with a glue stick."

"That was different."

"How?"

"He asked me not to," I said.

Freda looked at me for a long moment. Then she went back to her laptop, and put her headphones on, and said, to the screen, so quietly I almost didn't hear it, "Who even are you."

* * *

I told him about the Tribune, before I left.

I had not been going to. I had thought I would keep it, as I'd kept Pearl in the winter, to be the one who knew. But at the door, with my coat on, I found I wanted to see his face.

"I found Mr. Hatch," I said.

He had been holding the door. He stopped.

"In the New-York Tribune. April the twentieth. Page seven." I had it on my phone. I read it to him, all three lines, standing on his porch, with the field behind me still standing in water.

He listened to the end without moving. When I had finished, he looked out over my shoulder at the field for a moment.

"Three lines," he said.

"Yes."

"Bound for Naples. He'd have liked that they got Naples in." He was quiet. "That's more than most of them got, you know. Most of the ones who die like that. Three lines, with your name spelled right." He looked back at me. "Thank you for finding him, Ms. Barnett."

"It doesn't prove anything," I said. "You know that. You could have found it too."

"I could have," he said. "Yes."

"Did you?"

"No," he said. "I've never looked." And then, when I didn't say anything: "I didn't want to see how short it was."

* * *

On the step I turned back, because I had nearly forgotten.

"Next week is Freda's spring break," I said. "She asked if she could come. On Thursday. She's typed every one of these since the winter. She wants to meet you." I hesitated. "She says she's met you already, really. From the tapes. But she wants to see if you're the same."

Something came into his face that I hadn't seen there before. It took ten years off him, and then another five.

"I'd like that very much," he said. "Tell her I'll make the eggs."
