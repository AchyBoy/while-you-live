## Chapter 7: The Boats

*April 15, 1912, dawn*

The light came up slowly, as it does at sea, from nothing, out of the water itself.

And when it came, we saw where we were.

There was ice everywhere. I had known there was ice. I had watched the bergs go past us in the dark, one after another, white shapes in the starlight. But I had not understood. When the sun came up, the whole sea to the north and west of us was full of it, bergs of every size, some low and flat like islands and some standing up out of the water in peaks and towers, two hundred feet high, with the dawn on them turning them pink and gold. Between them, the water was crowded with smaller ice, a great field of it, broken and grinding, stretching away to the edge of the world. It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen. I hated it with my whole heart.

And between us and the ice, scattered across miles of flat water, the boats.

Little white boats, low in the water, a long way apart. Some rowing toward us. Some not moving at all. As the light grew you could count them, and then you could see the people in them, packed in, very still, and here and there one man standing up in the stern working a steering oar.

That was all. Boats, and ice, and the sea, and a few deck chairs, and some cork, floating. Almost nothing else. A ship as big as a town had been there five hours before, with two thousand people on her. You would not have known.

* * *

They came up all morning, one boat after another, for four hours.

I was at the port gangway the whole time, except when I was sent running. I gave out blankets. I carried coffee. I took children out of the canvas bags as they came up over the sill, and gave them to whoever reached for them, and sometimes nobody reached, and I carried them in myself and found them a chair and a blanket and somebody to sit with.

Most of them didn't speak. That's the thing I remember best, and the thing nobody believes. You would think there would have been screaming, or weeping, or crowds of people shouting names. There wasn't. Not at first. They came up the ladders, or in the slings, and they stood on the deck in the cold sunlight in whatever they had been wearing at midnight, evening dresses under men's overcoats, nightgowns under furs, a man in his pajamas and a dinner jacket, a girl in one shoe. And they looked about them. Not at us. At the deck. At the ship. As if they were trying to work out where they were, and whether it was real, and whether it would hold.

Then they went to the rail.

That was where they waited, all morning, the women, all along the rail, in a long row, looking down at each boat as it came in. Looking at the faces in it. Every time a boat came alongside, the whole row leaned over together and looked, and then, one at a time, they straightened up again. It was like watching a field of wheat in a wind.

Most of the boats were women and children. Some of them had a few men. There were very few men, anywhere. You learned, by the second or third boat, not to look at the women's faces when they straightened up.

* * *

I'll tell you about one boat. Only one.

It came in somewhere in the middle of the morning, when the sun was well up and the wind had begun to come up with it, and the sea that had been flat all night was starting to roughen, so the boats bumped and swung against our side as they came in. This one was fuller than most. There were men in her. More than in most of the others. I noticed that, and then I noticed I had noticed it, and put it away.

And there was a woman in the bow with two children.

She was young, perhaps twenty-five, very straight, with a dark coat buttoned to the throat and no hat, and her hair coming down. A little girl of about six sat against her, wrapped in the woman's own shawl, and she held a smaller boy on her lap, asleep, or nearly. When the boat came alongside, the seaman at the gangway reached down to take the boy from her, and she didn't let him. She held up one hand, quite calmly, palm out, the way you stop a carriage, and said something I couldn't hear. Then she put the boy into the canvas bag herself. She tied the neck of it herself, and tested the knot, and watched him all the way up until he was in somebody's arms on deck. Then she did the same with the girl. Then she came up the ladder.

She came up it well. Better than most of the men. And when she stepped through the gangway door, onto our deck, she did not do what all the others had done. She did not stand and look about her, at the deck, at the ship, as if it might not be real.

She looked at the people.

I saw her do it. I was standing by the stack of blankets, a yard away, and I watched her eyes go along the gangway. The purser at his table with his list. The two seamen at the door. The doctor. The stewards with coffee. Me. One after another, each of us, quick and complete, the way you read a page. Then the doors. Then the stairs. Then back to the children.

She did not look back at the sea. Not once.

I gave her a blanket. She took it, and said thank you, in an English voice, a good one, quiet. And for a moment she looked straight at me, a bell boy holding out a blanket, and I saw her see that I had been watching her. Then she put the blanket around the little girl and went to sit with them by the wall, and did not look at me again.

There was a woman with them, too. She came up after, in the sling, because she couldn't climb. Their mother, I learned later. She sat where they put her and didn't move, and didn't speak, and when the little girl climbed into her lap she held her without seeming to know who it was. The young woman in the dark coat went and knelt by her and talked to her, low, steadily, for a long time. The mother didn't answer.

The father had not been in the boat.

"What was her name?" I said.

He looked at me. He had been looking at the window, at the rain, while he told it, and now he turned and looked at me with a kind of patience I had not seen in him before.

"I'll tell you what I called her," he said. "Later. When it matters."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No." He set his cup down. "Ms. Barnett, I'm going to ask you to let me do something, and I know you won't like it. There are people in this part of the story who were real, and are on lists, and have families. Grandchildren. Great-grandchildren, some of them still alive, with their grandmother's photograph on the wall. Some of what happened in that boat, and on that ship, nobody has ever said aloud. If I give you their names, you'll check them. I know you will. That's why I asked for you." He looked at the notepad on his knee. "And then they'll be in a book."

"That's my job."

"I know it is. That's why I'm asking." He was very calm. "I won't give you the names of anyone in that boat. Not the woman with the children. Not the others. Not the number of the boat. Everyone else, you can have. Rostron, Cottam, Evans, Hatch. Check them all. But not the boat."

"Then how am I supposed to know any of it's true?"

"You aren't," he said. "Not that part. You'll have to decide whether to believe me." He smiled, a little, not happily. "I know. It's a great deal to ask of you, of all people."

"The lists are public," I said. "Every boat has been studied to death. If you tell me enough about what happened in it, I can work out which boat it was without you."

"You might," he said. "You're good enough. I'm asking you not to try."

I didn't promise. He noticed that I didn't.

"Every other Thursday," I said, "you've given me something I could check."

"Yes."

"Why not this?"

He was quiet for a long moment.

"Because I was twelve," he said, "and I couldn't do anything about it. And I've had a very long time to decide what I owe those people now." He picked up the pencil. "Not their names. That much I can still do."

I wrote it down. I wrote, *Won't name the boat. Won't name anyone in it.* And then, because I was honest on paper: *First time he's refused me anything.*

* * *

*April 15, 1912, morning*

The last boat came in at half past eight.

She was the worst. She was so full you could not see the wood of her sides, only people, packed shoulder to shoulder and standing in the bottom, seventy of them, more, in a boat built for sixty-five and riding so low that every swell came in over the gunwale. The wind was really blowing by then, and the sea was up, and she came toward us very slowly, rolling, with an officer standing in the stern steering her. We all stood at the rail and watched her come and did not breathe.

She made it. The officer was the last man up. A tall, hard-looking man, soaked to the waist, who came up the ladder like a man climbing out of a grave, and stood on our deck, and turned, and looked back out at the ice for a long moment before he let them lead him away. I learned afterward he was the senior officer saved. The most senior one left alive.

That was the end of it. Seven hundred and five people had come up our ladders. Behind us the boats we had emptied lay on the water where we'd left them, and nearby lay another, damaged and abandoned, with nobody in it. On the bridge the officers were still sweeping the sea with their glasses, among the ice, in case there was another boat out there that nobody had seen.

Before we had quite finished, another ship came up through the ice. A steamer, smaller than us. She came slowly and stopped a way off, and her wireless and ours talked, and on our bridge the officers looked across at her with faces I did not understand then.

She was the Californian. Later I read what the British inquiry made of her: that her officers had seen the Titanic's rockets in the night, and that her wireless man had gone to bed. Her captain disputed it for the rest of his life.

I didn't know any of that, that morning. I only saw the officers' faces.

* * *

The captain had the ship turn slowly around the place where the Titanic had gone down, once, while a clergyman who was one of our passengers held a service in the first-class saloon. A short one. Prayers for the dead, and thanks for the living, both at once, in the same breath, because there was no time to do them separately.

The saloon was full. Our passengers and theirs, side by side, the people who had slept through the night and the people who had sat in the boats. I stood at the back, against the wall, with the other boys, as we had the morning before, as if it were Sunday.

Most of the survivors didn't sing. They stood with the hymn books open and their mouths shut. Some of them wept, now, for the first time, quite silently. Some of them stared at the floor.

The young woman with the children was there, by the door, with the little boy asleep against her shoulder. She was not singing either. She was not praying, and she was not weeping, and she was not looking at the floor.

She was watching the room. Every face in it, one after another, the same as at the gangway. Quick and complete.

I knew that look. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting on an envelope you don't remember sending. It was the way I looked at a room.

It was nothing, I decided. A sharp young woman who had just lived through the worst night of her life, counting the doors because she would never again sit in a room without knowing where the doors were. Plenty of people came off those boats like that. I had seen a dozen of them that morning.

I told myself that, and I believed it, and I put her away.

That was not the first time in my long life I had done that, Ms. Barnett. The last time had been in a hotel dining room in Washington, to a waiter in a white jacket.

* * *

When the service was over, Rostron took the ship out of the ice, south and then west, and we turned for New York.

Mr. Hatch found me on the boat deck at a quarter to ten. He looked terrible. He had been out on deck since two in the morning, in the thin brown suit, and his face was gray with cold and his hands shook, and the notebook in his hand was so full of writing it had swollen to twice its size.

"Bertie," he said. "Do you know what's on this ship?"

"Survivors, sir."

"Seven hundred of them." He held up the notebook. "Seven hundred people who were there. Who saw it. Every newspaper in the world would give its eyeteeth for one of them, and we've got seven hundred, and four days before anybody else can get near them." He laughed. It was not a good laugh. It was the laugh of a man who has been awake too long, and has seen too much, and has just understood that it is also the best thing that ever happened to him. "I came to write about a volcano."

"Yes, sir."

"I'm going to talk to every one of them," he said. "Every single one who'll have me. I'm going to write it all down. What they saw. What they did." He put the notebook in his pocket, and buttoned the pocket, and patted it, once. "Somebody has to. You watch, Bertie. In New York they'll all have a story, and half of them will be lies, and nobody will ever know the difference. Unless somebody wrote it down first. Right here. While it was still true."

He went off along the deck, toward the first-class entrance, where a group of survivors was sitting in the sun in blankets, and I watched him go.

He was right, of course. That was the trouble with him. He was nearly always right.
