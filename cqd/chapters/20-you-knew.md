# CQD

## Chapter 20: You Knew

I didn't wear the coat.

It was hanging on the back of the kitchen chair where it had hung every Thursday since March, and I walked past it on the way out of the door and took my old rain jacket off the hook instead, the thin blue one with the broken zipper, and I knew exactly what I was doing and did it anyway.

Freda had left for school without breakfast. We had said perhaps forty words to each other since Monday, most of them about whether there was milk. She had typed nothing. Last Thursday's tape, the governess and the pages in the wake, the one she had sat on the stairs for, was sitting untouched on her laptop. I had not asked her to do it. She had not offered.

It was a bright, hard, cold morning, the kind April throws in once to remind you it can. The fields past Frederick were green now, properly green, and the trees along the road had leaves you could see from a distance. I didn't look at any of it. I drove with both hands on the wheel and the radio off and went over, the whole way, what I was going to say.

I had it word for word by the state line. I had been a reporter. I knew how to go into a room.

* * *

He was at the window. He was always at the window.

He came out onto the porch as I pulled up, and stopped at the top of the steps instead of coming down, and I saw him look at my face through the windshield, and then at the blue jacket, and I saw him understand both. He stood very still with one hand on the rail.

I got out of the car and shut the door and stood at the bottom of the steps.

"You knew," I said.

It wasn't what I had planned to say. I had planned something much longer.

"Yes," he said.

"For a week."

"Yes."

"She told you. At your kitchen table. While I was on the porch. And you sat there, and I came in, and you looked me in the eye and told me you were talking about Bertie's *sisters.*"

"Yes."

"And an hour later. *She's a good girl, Ms. Barnett.*" I heard my voice rising and let it. "You sat there with the recorders going, while she was in your kitchen making the tea, and said that to my face. And in March you folded a dish towel, when I told you she knew her way round Georgia Avenue. You folded a dish towel. I watched you do it. You knew then, too, didn't you? Before she ever told you."

He didn't answer that at once.

"I thought it," he said. "In March. I didn't know."

"But you thought it."

"Yes."

"And you didn't say one word."

"No," he said. "It wasn't mine to say."

* * *

We had it out in the kitchen. I don't remember going in. I remember standing by the counter with my bag still on my shoulder, and him by the stove, with the kettle on behind him because he had put it on before I came, as he always did, and neither of us touching it.

"I'm her mother," I said.

"Yes."

"She's sixteen. She was lying to me. Every week. For seven months. Getting on buses to the other side of the city to sit on a porch with a man who walked out on her when she was seven and sent her a birthday card two months late the year her mother lost everything. And you knew, and you let her."

"I didn't let her do anything, Ms. Barnett. She'd been doing it since September."

"You let her go on."

"I told her to tell you."

"You should have told me *yourself.*"

He was quiet for a moment.

"If I had," he said, "you'd have found out from me. Not from her. And she'd have known I told you. She'd have sat at my kitchen table and told me the thing she couldn't tell anyone, and watched me pick up the telephone. And she'd never have told anyone anything again, Ms. Barnett. Not you. Not me. Not anyone, for a very long time." He didn't raise his voice. "It was hers. It wasn't your story, and it wasn't mine. It was hers. The only thing I could do with it was give it back to her and ask her to carry it to you before it got too heavy."

"And if she hadn't?"

"Then I'd have had to decide," he said. "And I'd have been wrong, whichever I chose. I've had a great deal of practice at that."

"You decided anyway. You decided something about my daughter without asking me." I put my bag down on the counter, hard. "You don't get to do that. You don't get to know things about her that I don't. You've known her for one morning."

"Yes."

"You're a man who pretends to be other people for a living."

I heard it go across the kitchen and land on him. I saw it land. His face didn't change, very much. Something behind it did.

He didn't say anything. He turned around and took the kettle off the stove, because it had started to whistle, and stood holding it for a moment, as if he had forgotten what it was for.

I didn't take it back. I stood there and didn't take it back, and I was glad, for about a minute, that I'd said it.

* * *

"How is she?" he said at last, quietly, with his back to me.

"You don't get to ask that."

He set the kettle down.

"No," he said. "I suppose I don't."

He made the tea anyway. He made it as he always did, the pot warmed first, the cover on, the two cups, and he carried the tray through to the living room and put it down on the little table, and sat down in his chair, and picked up the notepad. I followed him in, because I didn't know what else to do, and because I was being paid six thousand dollars, and because I could not bear to drive away.

I set out the recorders. I pressed the buttons. I said the date into them, and the time, and *Session twenty-six,* and nothing else.

I didn't ask him how his week had been. He didn't ask about mine.

"Thursday night," I said. "Pier 54. You stopped on the ship coming into the harbor. Tell me about the pier."

He looked at me for a moment across the little table. He looked older, I thought, than he had looked a week ago. Older than he had looked all winter. There was a gray in his face under the weathering that I had not seen before, and his hands, on the notepad, were very still.

I noticed it. I didn't care, that morning. I'm telling you that because it's true.

"The pier," he said.

He looked down at the notepad, and didn't draw. He didn't draw for the whole of that session. It was the only time, in all the Thursdays, that he didn't. Freda told me that, later, when she typed it. She said the tape was the quietest one she had ever heard. No pencil. No humming. Only his voice, and the woodstove, and sometimes a long time when there was nothing at all.

"It was raining," he said. "In New York. That's the first thing."
