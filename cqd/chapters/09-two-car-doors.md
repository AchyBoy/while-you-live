# CQD

## Chapter 9: Two Car Doors

The bakery had been shut for twelve days, and on Thursday he made eggs.

"There's nothing to put soup next to," he said, at the door, by way of explanation. "I've been trying the bread from the supermarket in town. It's very soft. It's like eating a pillow somebody has been crying into." He took my coat, and looked at the moth hole in the sleeve with something like affection, and hung it on the hook. "So, eggs. Have you eaten?"

I had not. I had left before seven, because I wanted to be early, and I had said it was for the traffic.

We ate at the kitchen table. Scrambled eggs, very soft, as the French do them, with chives from a pot on the windowsill and toast from the crying pillow. He had made too much, and watched me eat it with the expression of a man who does not get to feed people often enough.

Then I took Mrs. Dunleavy's notebook out of my bag and put it on the table between the plates.

He didn't touch it at first. He looked at it. A black and white composition book, the marbled kind, with a date range on the cover in small furious capitals, and a strip of masking tape down the spine where it had been mended.

"You found her," he said.

"She found me. She took the phone off her son." I told him about the split-level, and the ramp, and the plastic tub in the garage with sixty notebooks in it. "You were right. She'd written down everything. Every pipe and every dog, for eight years. She said nobody ever asked her."

"No. They never do." He put out one finger and touched the cover, very lightly, as you would touch something in a museum when the guard was not looking. "May I?"

* * *

He read the Wednesday page for a long time.

I had read it perhaps forty times by then. I had copied it out by hand. I had it nearly by heart. And still I watched him read it, and found that I was holding my breath.

He read it once straight through. Then he went back to the top and read it again, slowly, with his finger moving down the margin, a line at a time, as if it were a contract.

"She's a good witness," he said at last.

"She's a furious witness."

"That's often the same thing. Look." He turned the book so I could see, and put his finger on a line. "*Man's voice, not his.* She doesn't say who she thinks it was. She doesn't guess. She writes down what she heard, and she stops. Most people can't do that. They tell you what it meant, and you have to dig down through the meaning to find out what actually happened." He moved the finger. "She only tells you what it meant once. Here, at the bottom. *He did not come back up.* And she's earned that one."

He sat back.

"Three knocks," he said. "Then two."

"I noticed that."

"Of course you did. What did you make of it?"

"That it's a knock you'd agree on," I said. "Somebody you'd told how to knock. So you'd know it was them and open the door." I had thought about it all week. "He was frightened, that week. I'd told him to stay home. He wouldn't have opened his door to just anybody."

"It might be that," he said. "Or it might be a man who knocked three times, heard nothing, and knocked again. People do. What she heard is five knocks with a gap in them. What it means, we don't know yet." He moved his finger down. "The cat doesn't settle it either. Petrakis told you he shut her away for anybody at the door. What gets us a little further is the next line. They talked for half an hour, and one of them laughed a great deal. A frightened man doesn't usually let a stranger in and sit with him for half an hour while the stranger laughs."

"So somebody he knew."

"Probably. And one of them, at least, was at his ease." He shook his head slightly. "I'd like to say the laughing man came to persuade him of something. I'd like it very much. But a laugh doesn't tell you what a man came for. It only tells you he wasn't worried." He looked at the next lines, and his face grew very still. "Two trips. Both heavy. The first time, she writes, something banging the wall all the way down. You told me there were two banker's boxes in the closet by the door."

"Yes."

"Then two men, one box each, one trip, would fit. It isn't the only thing that would." He tapped the line. "And the banging. On a narrow stair, a box in front of you hides your feet. Anybody might knock it against the wall. If somebody did it on every step, all the way down, he was either clumsy or carrying something awkward, and she can't tell us which. But a wall that gets banged all the way down a staircase usually shows it. If you get the police photographs, look at that wall." He looked up at me. "Then they came back up, quickly. Then down again, heavy, banging. What was the second trip, Ms. Barnett?"

I had been trying to answer that all week. I said what I had come up with, which was nothing: more papers, perhaps. A third box I'd never seen.

He shook his head, very slightly.

"You told me once what the police wrote down, in his apartment. The officer who only wrote down what was in front of him. You read it to me off the report." He waited. "What did the officer write about the desk?"

It took me a moment. Then I had it, and I felt it go through me like cold water.

*Desk.* One word, and nothing after it.

"He had a computer," I said. "An old one. A big beige thing, on the desk, with a scanner. He scanned everything. Every document, before he copied it. I saw it the one time I was there. I never even thought about it." I put my hand flat on the table. "It wasn't in the report because it wasn't there. And I always thought, of course it wasn't there, he took it with him. Like the boxes."

"It's the likeliest thing for the second trip," he said. "A computer and a scanner, down those stairs, would be heavy and awkward and would want two men. It's a guess, Ms. Barnett. It's a good guess, and it wants checking. Petrakis may remember whether it was there on the Friday. The movers may have listed it." He looked at the page again. "But if it was the computer, then think what went down those stairs. Every copy, every scan, every piece of paper that could prove a word of your story. And she says the light step was his. If she's right, he carried it down himself, and left his cat behind a closed door, and his family's photographs on the wall."

"He thought they were helping him," I said.

It came out very quietly. I had not meant to say it out loud.

"That's the shape I'd bet on," he said. "Somebody he trusted telling him the evidence wasn't safe in his apartment, with Carrow's lawyers about, and they had a place it would be. And a place for him, too, when he moved on Saturday, if he'd let them find it." He was quiet for a moment. "*Customer to call with address.* But it's a shape, Ms. Barnett. It isn't proof. You'll want to remember that when you start to believe it."

I got up and took the plates to the sink, because I needed something to do with my hands, and ran the water over them, and didn't wash them. When I came back he had turned the notebook around again.

* * *

"There's one more thing," he said.

I looked up.

"It's a small thing. It may be nothing. It's a reason to ask, that's all." He put his finger on the last lines. "Look at the car."

I looked. I knew those lines as well as I knew my own name.

*10:14. Back door. Somebody fighting it, it STICKS, been telling P. for a YEAR. Car in alley. Engine running. Exhaust coming in my kitchen window AGAIN.*

*10:21. Two back UP. Quick.*

*10:33. Two DOWN again. Slow. Heavy. Banging.*

*10:41. Car door. Car door. Car gone.*

"The engine was running when they came down the first time," he said. "With the boxes. She smelled the exhaust from her kitchen. Then both of them went back upstairs, quickly, for the computer, and they were up there for twelve minutes. And all that time, the engine was still running."

"People leave engines running."

"They do. In an alley, in the middle of the morning, with nobody in the car, while they go back up the stairs and stay twelve minutes, and leave a car full of the only proof of a story the whole city is talking about, unlocked, with the engine running, in an alley." He let that sit. "Some people. Perhaps."

"Or somebody was sitting in it."

"Or somebody was sitting in it." He touched the last line. "And then, at the end. *Car door. Car door.* Two doors."

"Two men got in," I said slowly. "And the one who drove was already in it."

"That's one way to hear it." He shook his head. "Two doors could be two people. They could be one man shutting a door that didn't catch the first time. Three people could shut three doors so close together that she wrote down two. The doors don't give us a number, Ms. Barnett. Nor does the engine. What they give us is a question. Was somebody waiting in that car while they went back up?" He took his finger off the page.

I wrote it down. *Knock: 3, then 2. Signal? Or knocked twice. Half an hour, laughing: at ease, so probably somebody he knew. Two heavy trips: boxes, then the computer? Check Petrakis, movers. Engine running 27 min, 2 doors.* And under it: *Somebody waiting in the car? Two people, or three?*

Then I looked at what I had written, and at Mrs. Dunleavy's furious little capitals beside it, and I thought about a careful man who apologized to the stairs, going down them for the last time between a friend who laughed and a stranger he may never have seen.

* * *

"She said one more thing," I said. "About the other man. The one who laughed. She said he had leather heels. Hard ones. She could hear every step on the stairs. Click, click, click, like a lady, she said. She said you don't hear leather heels much anymore."

He had been reaching for the coffee pot. His hand stopped.

It was only for a second. I would not have noticed it, before. I had spent five months learning to notice things about him, and I noticed it now. His hand stopped just short of the pot, and stayed, and then went on, and poured.

"Leather heels," he said.

"Does that mean something?"

"It means he had leather soles and hard heels on," he said. "Men who wear those are usually dressed for an office. That's as far as it goes." He put the pot down and looked at the window. "It reminds me of something. That's all. Something a long way off."

He didn't say what. I didn't ask. I had learned, by then, that when he looked at the window like that, the answer was usually coming, and usually on tape.

* * *

He asked about Freda when we had cleared the plates. I told him she had come with me to Frederick, and had sat on the couch with the grandson and a can of soda, quiet as a mouse, for an hour.

"And then she asked the one question I didn't," I said. "Whether Mrs. Dunleavy had ever seen him. The other man."

"Had she?"

"No. Only heard him." I dried the last plate. "She was good. Freda. She just sat there and let it happen. Mrs. Dunleavy liked her. She said she had her own mind." I put the plate away. "She even got us around the fun run on Sixteenth Street. Up Georgia and across on Missouri. I didn't know she knew that part of town."

He was folding the dish towel. He folded it in half, and in half again, and hung it on the oven door, and didn't say anything at all.

Then he went through to the living room, and sat in his chair, and picked up the notepad.

"Monday," he said, when I had said *Session twenty-one* into the recorders. "We stopped on the Monday morning, with the boats in and Mr. Hatch going off along the deck with his notebook. We'll go on from the Monday afternoon. A good deal of what mattered started that afternoon, though none of us knew it."
