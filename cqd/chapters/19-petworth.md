# CQD

## Chapter 19: Petworth

I had her location on my phone. Every mother I knew did. We had set it up when she got the phone, at twelve, both of us, mine on hers and hers on mine, as you buy a fire extinguisher. In four years I had looked at it twice, both times when she was late from a school trip and the bus had broken down on the highway.

I looked at it now, in the parking garage, with the engine off.

It took a moment to load. A map of the city, gray and green, and a small round picture of my daughter's face, the one from her school ID, with the bad bangs she had cut herself in the ninth grade. It was not in the west part of town, where Maddie lived. It was not anywhere near school, or the library, or the coffee place by the metro where she sometimes sat with her friends.

It was up off Georgia Avenue. In Petworth. On a short street of rowhouses two blocks east of the avenue, between a church and a laundromat.

*Go up Georgia. Cut across at Missouri.*

I started the car.

* * *

It took twenty-five minutes, in the traffic. I didn't turn the radio on. I drove up Sixteenth and across and up Georgia, past the hardware store and the Ethiopian place and the new apartment buildings going up where the old ones had been, with the envelope of photographs still on the passenger seat, and the whole way I told myself I was going to be calm. That there would be a reason. A friend I didn't know. A tutor. A boy.

I knew. I think I had known since the parking garage. I think I had known since the headphones, if I was honest, and I'm honest on paper, and I was trying very hard not to think on paper.

The street was quiet, with trees just coming into leaf and cars parked tight along both curbs. The rowhouses were brick, two stories, with little iron fences and porches, some painted and some not. The dot on my phone was on the fourth one from the corner on the left. A house with a green door and a porch with two plastic chairs on it.

Freda was sitting in one of them.

She had her feet up on the porch rail, and her headphones around her neck, and a can of soda in her hand. She was laughing. The real laugh. I could see it from the car before I could hear it.

The man in the other chair was tall. Too tall for the chair. He sat with his long legs folded up and his elbows on his knees, as he always used to sit, as if the furniture of the world had been made for somebody smaller and he had stopped complaining about it. He was thinner than I remembered. There was gray at his temples now, and he had a short beard, gray at the chin, that he hadn't had. He was wearing a canvas work jacket with the name of the hardware store on Georgia Avenue stitched over the pocket.

He was listening to her. He wasn't talking. He was just listening, with his head a little on one side, the way you listen to somebody when you've been waiting a long time to be allowed to.

I had not seen Desmond Barnett in nine years.

* * *

I pulled into a space across the street, between a pickup and a minivan, and turned off the engine, and sat there.

I could have driven away. I want to be honest about that. For about a minute, I could have. I could have gone home and made dinner and waited until seven and let her come in and say *hi, I was at Maddie's,* and said *how's Maddie,* and kept it, as I had kept so many things, until I knew exactly what I wanted to do with it.

Then Freda turned her head, laughing at something, and looked across the street, and saw my car.

I watched it go out of her. All of it. The laugh, and the color, and the ease, gone out of her face in one second, like water out of a sink. She took her feet off the rail. She put the can down on the porch floor. She didn't get up.

Desmond saw her face, and turned, and followed her eyes, and saw me through the windshield.

He stood up.

* * *

I got out of the car. I crossed the street. I didn't hurry. I stopped at the bottom of the porch steps, on the sidewalk, outside the little iron gate, and didn't open it.

"Iola," he said.

"Get in the car, Freda."

"Mom..."

"Get in the car."

"Iola, please. This isn't her fault. I asked her..."

"I know you did," I said. I didn't look at him. I looked at my daughter. "I'm not talking to you, Desmond. I'm talking to my daughter. Freda. Get your things and get in the car."

Freda looked at him. She looked at me. She picked up her bag from beside the chair, very slowly, and put her headphones in it. The white ones.

"Those are nice," I said. "Maddie's sister has very good taste."

She went past me through the gate without looking at me, and across the street, and got into the passenger seat, and shut the door, and sat there looking straight ahead through the windshield.

Desmond came down one step. He stopped there, when I looked at him.

"She found me," he said quietly. "In September. I want you to know that. I didn't..."

"I don't want to know anything," I said. "Not today. Not standing on your sidewalk." I heard my own voice. It was perfectly level. I sounded, I thought, like somebody's lawyer. "If you want to talk to me, Desmond, you can call me. You've had my number for nine years."

I turned around and walked back across the street.

* * *

Neither of us said anything for the first ten minutes. I drove down Georgia in the evening traffic with the photographs on the dashboard, where I'd moved them, and Freda in the passenger seat with her bag on her knees and her feet flat on the floor.

At a red light by the old theater she said, very quietly, to the window, "You'd have said no."

"You don't know that."

"Yes, I do." She turned around. Her face was red and wet and furious. "You'd have said no. You'd have said he was a drunk and he left and he sent a card in March and he'd do it again. You'd have said all of it. You'd have been *right.*" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "And then I'd never have known him. Ever. Not even once."

"So you lied to me."

"Yes."

"For seven months."

"Since September," said Freda. "Yes."

September. Before the letter. Before Thursdays, and the old man, and 1865. I thought of a night in the fall, the electric bill in red on top of the pile, and Freda on the phone through the wall, laughing the low careful laugh she saved for friends, and the door in her that had stayed shut, and how I had told myself I would find out what was behind it.

The light changed. I drove.

"I check everything," I said. "Everything. It's the only thing I've ever been good at. I checked the lab. I checked the scale. I checked a dead man's cat and a dead man's movers and an old woman's notebooks. I checked the Library of Congress." I heard my voice crack, and stopped it. "And I never once checked you."

"I know," said Freda. "Because you trusted me." She was crying again, quietly, looking straight ahead. "I knew you did. And I used it. I know I did. I'm sorry. I'm not sorry I found him, but I'm sorry about that." She took a breath. "And it wasn't just me. Mr. Hale knew."

I nearly missed the turn.

"What?"

"At spring break. When you went out on the porch for the phone call." Freda wiped her nose on the back of her hand. "He knew. He didn't even do the thing. He just looked at me for like one minute, and I could tell he knew. So I told him. All of it." She swallowed. "He didn't tell me what to do. He just said I had to tell you. That it wasn't his to tell, but it was mine, and the longer I waited, the more it'd cost. Things you keep back don't get lighter, they get heavier, and somebody else ends up carrying them for you." Her voice wobbled. "He said it'd be me, carrying it. And then you, when you found out. And then him, a little, for knowing."

I thought of the kitchen window. Freda's elbows on the table, her hands around her mug, talking fast. Her hand coming up to her eye. A sentence from him, perhaps two. A small wet laugh.

*I'm talking about a notebook.*

And on the porch, holding my plate: *We were talking about Bertie. His sisters.*

And on Thursday, in the living room, with the recorders running between us, while Freda made the tea: *She's a good girl, Ms. Barnett.* And holding my eyes a moment longer than he needed to.

"He knew," I said. "Since Thursday."

"Mom, he said it wasn't his to tell."

"I know what he said."

I drove the rest of the way home without saying anything else. When we got there, Freda went to her room and shut the door. It didn't slam. She shut it very quietly, which was worse.

I sat in the kitchen with the envelope of photographs in front of me, unopened again, and my phone face up beside it, and I didn't make dinner. At nine o'clock the phone lit up with a number I didn't know, with a local exchange, and I watched it ring, and let it go.

At ten past nine it lit up again with a text, from the same number.

*It's Dez. I'm sorry. I'll wait as long as you need. She's the best thing I ever had any part of, and I know whose doing that was. D.*

D.

I turned the phone face down on the table, the way my daughter did, and sat there in the kitchen until the light under her door went out.
