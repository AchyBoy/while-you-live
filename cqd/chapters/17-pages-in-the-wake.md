## Chapter 17: Pages in the Wake

*April 18, 1912, afternoon*

I made one try. I want you to know that. Just one.

At two o'clock I went to find the officer who had stood at the top of the after companionway with a lantern and written *Death accidental* in his book. He was on the boat deck, by the bridge ladder, with his hands behind his back, looking at the fog, which had begun to thin. You could see the rail now, and the boats, and a little gray water beyond them.

I stood in front of him with my cap in my hands.

"Please, sir," I said. "Can I ask you something? About last night."

He looked down at me. I don't think he remembered me at all.

"What is it?"

"If a gentleman slipped at the top of those stairs, sir, and went down them," I said, "and you found a mark on the steps where his heel had gone. Would the mark be going up, sir, or down?"

He stared at me.

"Down, I should think," he said. "If he was going down." He frowned. "What sort of question is that?"

"Only, sir, I thought I saw one going up. On the fifth step, in the blood, before the lady slipped on it. With a notch out of the heel." I made myself say the rest. "And there's a gentleman's shoe in B-52, sir, with a notch just like it. And something brown in the notch. If somebody looked before it's cleaned..."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then something happened in his face that I had seen happen in a great many faces, in a great many lives, when a person of no account says something that would be a great deal of trouble if it were true. He decided it wasn't.

"You thought you saw," he said. "In the fog. At one in the morning. Where you had no business being. And now you've been handling a first-class gentleman's shoes and making up stories about them." He bent down a little, so his face was level with mine. "Listen to me, boy. A man fell down a flight of stairs in the fog, and the doctor's seen him, and it's written in the log, and there's an end of it. We've got seven hundred people on this ship who've lost everything, and a captain who hasn't slept since Sunday, and we'll be in New York tonight with half the world waiting on the pier. The last thing anybody needs is a bell boy with an imagination." He straightened up. "Now cut along before I tell Mr. Lyle where you were last night."

I cut along.

That was my one try. I had known before I made it how it would end. I had known it on the floor of the bell boys' cabin at four in the morning. But I had to make it, Ms. Barnett. You understand. If I hadn't, I'd have had to wonder.

* * *

The fog lifted at about three.

It went quite suddenly, as fog does at sea, as if someone had pulled a sheet off a table. One minute the ship was wrapped in white, and the whistle going, and the next the white was tearing apart into rags and drifting off over the water, and there was a pale sun, low in the west, and blue sky in pieces, and a long, slow, gray-green swell. The whistle stopped. The engines came up. You could feel her lift and begin to move again.

People came out on deck. Survivors, and our own passengers, to stand in the sun. Nobody said much. They stood along the rails and looked west, where New York was, still below the edge of the world.

I went aft, because I was sent with a rug for a lady by the after rail, and on my way back I saw her.

She was at the very stern, alone. Right at the back of the ship, by the flagstaff, where the rail curved round and nobody went, because there was nothing to see there but the wake. She was in her dark coat again, with her hair up. Her back was to me.

She had something in her hands. White. Paper.

As I watched, she took one sheet from the top and held it out over the rail, at arm's length, and opened her fingers.

The wind took it. It went up, first, a little, turning over in the sun, white and then white again, like a bird that has been startled off a fence. Then it came down into the wake behind the ship, into the long churned road of green and white water the screws were making, and lay flat on it for a second, and was rolled under.

She took another sheet and did the same.

I stood by the deckhouse with the rug over my arm and watched her do it. Five times. I counted. I knew how many there were. Five pages, typed with two fingers in the second-class library on a Wednesday afternoon by a man in a brown suit, going up into the sun, one after another, and turning over, and falling into the wake, and lying there, white, and gone.

She didn't hurry. She did each one separately, carefully, the way you'd lay flowers on a grave one at a time.

When the last one had gone, she stood with her hands on the rail for a moment, looking down at the water where it had gone under.

Then she turned around, and saw me.

* * *

She didn't seem surprised. I think she had known I was there for some time.

She came along the rail toward me, unhurried, with her hands in the pockets of her coat, and stopped a little way off. The sun was behind her. I couldn't see her face well.

"Bertie," she said.

"Miss."

"I owe Stan another shilling," she said. "She says he looked very unhappy about it."

"She," I said.

"I offered to go myself." She glanced back at the wake. "She wouldn't let me. She said it was her name in them, not mine."

I thought of the lower berth, and the hollow in the pillow, and the cork still in the bottle.

"The notebook's gone too," she said. "If you were wondering. In case you thought you might still find it. Mr. Ainsley went down to the stokehold at six this morning, before anyone was about, with a cigar he didn't light, and came up again without the notebook. I was on the stair." She looked past me, at the funnel, where the smoke was going up. "There's nothing left. Not a page. Not anywhere."

"There's me," I said.

She looked at me then. A long look. I couldn't see her eyes, with the sun behind her, but I felt it.

"Yes," she said. "There's you."

"I saw the step. I saw his shoe. I saw his jaw at breakfast."

"Yes. And so did I. And neither of us will ever say so to anyone who matters, because no one who matters will ever listen to a bell boy and a governess against a gentleman with a bruise from slipping in the fog." She said it quite gently. "You know that. You've known it since four o'clock this morning. You went and asked that officer anyway, at two, by the bridge ladder. I saw you." She was quiet a moment. "That was brave. It was also quite useless. I'm sorry."

"You're not sorry."

"No," she agreed. "I'm not sorry I did it. I'm sorry it had to be done. That isn't the same thing." She took one hand out of her pocket and pushed a strand of hair off her face. "I don't expect you to forgive me, Bertie. I'm not sure I would, in your place."

I didn't say anything for a while. The wake ran away behind us, white and green, very straight, all the way back toward the east, toward the ice, and the place where the boats had been.

"Why did you hum it?" I said. "That song. To the little girl. On Tuesday night."

I don't know why I asked. It wasn't what I'd meant to say. It came out of me before I could stop it.

She went very still. For the first time since I had known her, I saw her not know what to say.

"It's an old song," she said at last.

"Yes, miss."

"My father..."

"Found it in a book," I said. "Yes, miss. You told her."

She looked at me for a long time. The wind moved her coat. Down below us, in the wake, there was nothing at all.

"How old are you, Bertie?" she said.

"Twelve, miss."

"No," she said quietly. "You're not."

And she turned, and walked away along the rail toward the door, and went in, and didn't look back.

He stopped. He put the pencil down.

Nobody said anything. Freda was sitting on the stairs with her arms tight around her knees, perfectly still.

"What did she mean?" said Freda. "You're not."

"I don't know," he said. "I've never known." He looked at the window. "Perhaps she meant that I didn't talk like a boy of twelve. I didn't, by then. I'd stopped trying, with her. Perhaps she meant only that."

"But you don't think so."

He didn't answer that.

"It was a long time ago, Freda," he said instead. "And there's still the rest of Thursday. The worst of it." He picked the pencil up again, and looked at it, and put it down. "I think I'd like a cup of tea first. If nobody minds."

Freda got up off the stairs and said she'd make it. He started to protest, and she said, "Sit," in exactly my voice, and went into the kitchen. He sat.

He looked at me across the little table, with the recorders running between us.

"She's a good girl," he said quietly.

"I know."

"No," he said. "I mean, she's a good girl, Ms. Barnett." He held my eyes a moment longer than he needed to. Then he looked at the notepad on his knee, and said nothing else until the tea came.

*April 18, 1912, evening*

They came for me at five.

I was in the pantry with Evans, drying cups, because there was nothing else for me to do and I couldn't sit still. Lyle came in. He had the purser behind him, and behind the purser, in the doorway, in his good gray suit and his shined shoes, Mr. Ainsley.

"Pike," said Lyle. "Where's the writer's case?"

I put the cup down.

It had been very simple, as the worst things often are. At four the purser had finally found time to see to the dead man's effects. He had sent a steward to the library. The steward had found a pen on the table and nothing else. The purser had said that was odd, a writer with no papers, and Mr. Ainsley, who happened to be passing the purser's office, had very kindly mentioned that the poor fellow had a typewriter, and a notebook, a fat one, which he carried everywhere and which, Mr. Ainsley understood, he had been hoping to sell to the New York newspapers. Things like that were worth a great deal of money this week, Mr. Ainsley said. It might be worth asking who had been in the library.

And a lady sleeping in the library had seen a bell boy carry a case out of it at seven that morning. A small one, with a mended handle.

"In my locker, sir," I said.

They went and got it. Lyle carried it back up to the pantry himself and put it on the counter and opened it in front of all of us. A typewriter, and a ribbon tin, and a letter to a sister in Ohio, half finished, and nothing in the lid.

"And the notebook?" said the purser.

"I never had the notebook, sir."

"Then where is it?"

I looked past him at Mr. Ainsley in the doorway. He was looking back at me with an expression of great kindness and a little sorrow, like a man looking at a boy who has disappointed him.

"I don't know, sir," I said.

"Took the case and not the notebook," said Lyle. "That's a likely story. And what were you going to do with a typewriter, Pike? Write your memoirs?" He turned to the purser. "He's been out of his bunk every night this week, sir. I've had complaints. And there's papers on this ship offering a year's wages for any scrap of a survivor's story."

"I was keeping it safe," I said. "For his family. He had a sister."

It was the truth. Nobody in that pantry believed it, and I don't blame them. I wouldn't have.

The purser looked at me for a long moment. He was a fair man, I think. He was also a tired one.

"He'll be put ashore in New York," he said to Lyle. "Discharged. No character." He closed the case. "The company can decide about the notebook. I haven't time."

"Go easy on the boy, Purser," said Mr. Ainsley, from the doorway. His voice was warm. "It's been a hard week for everyone. Boys get ideas, at times like these." He smiled at me. "I'm sure he meant no harm."

And he turned and went away down the corridor, and I listened to his heels go, click, click, click, all the way to the end, until a door shut.

At six o'clock we passed the Sandy Hook lightship, and the passengers lined the rails to see the lights of America, and I was in the bell boys' cabin on E deck, with my things in a canvas bag on my bunk, and Stan sitting on the bunk above with his half of a shilling, not saying anything at all.
