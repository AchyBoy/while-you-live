# CQD

## Chapter 25: Spring Sale

He was there at nine. I know because I was there at a quarter to, and parked across the street, and watched him go in.

I want to tell you about that coffee, and I'm not going to, not all of it. It isn't mine to tell, any more than Freda's was Tom's. I'll tell you that he stood up when I came in, and didn't know what to do with his hands, and that he had bought me a coffee already and it had gone cold, and he went and bought another. That he looked at me across the table for a long time before he said anything, and then said, *You look exactly the same,* which was a lie, and *You look tired,* which wasn't.

I'll tell you I asked him everything. Very politely. With my notebook in my bag where he couldn't see it. Where he lived, and since when. Where he worked, and since when. How long sober, and how he knew, and who he called when it was bad. Phoenix, and what happened there. The card in March.

"I forgot her birthday," he said. He looked at the table. "I was drunk the whole of that January. I don't remember most of it. I found the card in a drawer in March, already bought, never sent, with her name on it. And I thought, if I send it now it's worse. And then I thought, if I don't send it at all, that's worse still." He turned his cup around. "I sent it. I wrote *sorry it's late.* That's all I wrote. Three words, on a card two months late, the year you lost everything." He looked up at me. "I knew about the story. I read it in Phoenix, online. I knew what was happening to you. I sent three words."

I didn't say anything to that. I didn't have to. He had said it all himself.

I wrote everything down in the car afterward, for forty minutes, in the parking space outside. Then I drove home and spent the rest of the weekend checking it. The hardware store. The apartment. The meeting on Tuesday nights at the church on Upshur. Most of it checked. Some of it I couldn't check, and never will.

Freda asked me on Sunday night what I'd decided.

"I haven't," I said.

"But you're going to see him again."

"Yes," I said. "I'm going to see him again."

* * *

On Monday I went back to the Library of Congress.

I hadn't been since February. The same reading room, the same lamps, the same librarian with her glasses on a chain, who looked at my reader card with its terrible photograph and then at me, and said, "You came back," as if she had been keeping track.

"I'm looking for auction catalogs," I said. "From a house here in the city. Carrow House. Spring sales, about seven years ago."

She didn't ask why. She went and looked, and came back, and said that the Library had collected auction catalogs for a very long time, from all the major houses and a good many minor ones, because the catalogs were often the only record of where a document had gone. Carrow House was in the collection. Every catalog they had ever printed, more or less, back to the 1950s. She could have the ones I wanted at the desk in an hour.

I waited the hour. I read the same page of a newspaper eleven times.

There were three, from the right year and the two around it. Heavy cream paper, as I remembered. Bound in gray card, with the brass doors embossed on the cover in gold. *CARROW HOUSE. SPRING SALE. Autograph Letters, Manuscripts and Historical Documents.* I had held a dozen of these, once, when I was working on the story, and I had read every lot description in every one of them, looking for forgeries. I had never once read the front matter. Who reads the front matter of an auction catalog?

The middle one had a foreword. Two pages, with a photograph at the top of the first.

It was him.

The same man as in photograph number seven, on the wall over the brown couch. White hair swept back. Bow tie. A beautifully cut gray suit. Not laughing, in this one. Smiling, with his chin up, at the camera, like a man who has been photographed a great many times and knows exactly what his face is doing. Under the picture, in small italic capitals:

*GERALD WHITCOMBE, DIRECTOR OF CONSERVATION AND MANUSCRIPTS.*

I read the foreword. I read it slowly, as Tom read Mrs. Dunleavy's page. It was about paper. About the care of old documents, and the patience it took, and the honor of handing a letter from one century into the next without harm. It was well written. It was charming. It was the voice of a man who was used to people listening.

And at the end of the second page, there was a paragraph of thanks. To the staff of the conservation studio, *without whom nothing in these pages would have come safely to sale.* And then one more line, by itself.

*And to my apprentice of many years,* it said, and gave his name.

My source's name. His real one. The one I had never written down, in any notebook, anywhere, in six years.

*whose hands are already better than mine.*

I sat in the reading room under the lamp, with the catalog open in front of me, and put my hand flat over that line, as Hatch used to keep his hand over his notebook. I didn't want anybody else to see it. There was nobody else at the table.

His apprentice. Of many years. The man who had taught him everything he knew about paper. Who had stood in front of the brass doors with his arm around his shoulders, hard, laughing, as you hold someone who belongs to you.

*Somebody he trusted enough to let them choose where he would live.*

I photographed the foreword, both pages, with no flash, as the sign on the wall asked. I didn't ask for a copy. I didn't need to hold this one.

* * *

It took me an hour that night to find out where Gerald Whitcombe was now.

He had retired from Carrow House the year after my story ran. There had been a little piece about it in the trade newsletter, with a photograph and a quote about forty years of happy work. He was seventy-nine. A widower. He lived in a stone house on forty acres outside Middleburg, in the hunt country in Virginia, an hour and a half west of my kitchen table. He was on the board of a historical society and a library foundation. He gave a talk, every spring, on the care of old paper.

And somebody had filmed one of the talks, three years ago, and put it online.

I watched it at the kitchen table with my headphones on. Forty minutes. He stood at a lectern in a bow tie, with slides behind him, and talked about the enemies of paper: light, damp, acid, and the human hand. He was very good. He was funny. The audience laughed in the right places.

At thirty-one minutes, somebody in the audience asked a question I couldn't hear. And Gerald Whitcombe put his head back and laughed.

I took the headphones off. I sat in the kitchen with the screen frozen on him, mid-laugh, his mouth wide open, his head back, exactly as he was in photograph number seven.

I knew that laugh. I had never heard it before tonight. And I knew it.

* * *

Freda came with me to Frederick on Wednesday, after school. She asked. I didn't have to think about it this time.

Kevin opened the door before we knocked again. Mrs. Dunleavy was by the window in her wheelchair with the crocheted blanket over her knees, in the same cardigan buttoned all the way to the top, and she watched us come up the ramp with her bird's eyes, and when we came in she said, "You came back," before we had our coats off.

"I said I would."

"People say all sorts of things." She held out her hands. I gave her the composition book, the one with the masking tape on the spine, and she took it in both hands and held it in her lap and looked at it, and then looked at me. "Well? Did you find out where the car went?"

"Not yet," I said. "But I think I've found out who was in it. One of them."

I sat down on the couch next to her chair. Freda sat on the arm of it. I took out my phone.

"Mrs. Dunleavy," I said. "You never saw him. You told me. You only heard him. Through the ceiling, for half an hour, and on the stairs."

"Every step," she said. "Click, click, click."

"And the laugh."

"And the laugh." Her mouth went thin. "I'd know that laugh in my grave."

"I'm going to play you something," I said. "A man talking. For a minute or two. I'm not going to tell you anything about him. I just want you to listen."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and folded her hands on top of the composition book, and closed her eyes.

I found the place. Thirty minutes and ten seconds. I turned the volume up all the way and held the phone near her ear.

Gerald Whitcombe's voice came out of it, warm and easy and carrying, talking about the human hand, and how it was the greatest enemy of old paper and also its only friend. Mrs. Dunleavy sat perfectly still with her eyes closed. Freda had stopped breathing. Somewhere in the house, Darren's music thumped faintly through a door.

At thirty-one minutes, the audience member asked a question. And Gerald Whitcombe laughed.

Mrs. Dunleavy's eyes opened.

She didn't say anything at first. She sat there with her hands on the notebook, very small in her wheelchair, and her face did something I had seen a great many faces do in my working life, the moment when a thing you have carried by yourself for years turns out to have been real, and you are not alone with it anymore.

"That's him," she said.

"Are you sure?"

"Six years," said Mrs. Dunleavy, "I've been hearing that laugh in my sleep." She looked at the phone. "That's him. That's the man who went down my stairs with that boy's whole life in a box and banged my new paint all the way to the bottom." Her voice cracked. She put one small cold hand over Freda's, on the arm of the chair, without seeming to notice she'd done it. "Who is he?"

I told her.

She said the name over to herself twice, quietly, as if she were writing it down in her head, with the date, and the time.

* * *

In the car, at the bottom of Kevin Dunleavy's cul-de-sac, I took my notebook out and wrote it down.

I had never once written down my source's name. Not in eight months of reporting, not in the six years since. Petrakis was in my notebooks, and Delacroix, and the movers, and every lawyer Carrow ever hired. He wasn't, and he wasn't now, even after the catalog. I wrote this name anyway. This man wasn't my source. He wasn't somebody I was protecting.

*Gerald Whitcombe.*

*His teacher. Mrs. D knows the laugh, by ear, six years on, off a phone. Not proof. Leather heels? Two trips?*

And under it, because there might have been three of them, and Tom had made me see it, and I couldn't stop seeing it now:

*Who was driving?*

Freda read it upside down from the passenger seat. She'd been reading over my shoulder since she was four.

"Shouldn't you tell the police?"

"With what?" I said. "A notebook, a laugh off a phone, and a photograph from a party. They'd put it in the file next to Mrs. Dunleavy's two phone calls, the ones nobody returned. Or somebody would ring him up and ask him about it, politely, and then he'd know exactly who was asking, and how little they had." I put the notebook in my bag. "When I go to them, I want to bring them something they can't file."

Freda thought about that.

"Are you going to go see him?" she said. "The teacher?"

"Not yet."

"When?"

"When I know who was driving," I said, and started the car.
