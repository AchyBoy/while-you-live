## Chapter 10: Monday

*April 15, 1912, afternoon*

By noon on that Monday there were more than fourteen hundred passengers on a ship that had sailed with half that many, and nobody was comfortable, and nobody complained.

You have to imagine it. Every bunk was full, and every sofa. The officers had given up their cabins. Our own first-class passengers had given up theirs, many of them, and moved in two and three together, and the survivors who were not ill or hurt lay on the floors of the saloons and the smoking rooms and the library, wrapped in blankets, in rows, like wounded after a battle. Which is what they were. Women slept sitting up in chairs with children across their laps. In the third-class dining room, the Hungarian doctor went up and down the rows in his shirtsleeves, and the families from our steerage, who were going home to the mountains with everything they owned in a bundle, opened the bundles and gave away what was in them.

That was the thing I hadn't expected. Our own passengers.

They'd been told to stay in their cabins and keep out of the way, and the moment the boats came in, almost every one of them disobeyed. The American ladies going to see Italy took off their own coats and put them around strangers. They gave up their nightgowns and their stockings and their hairbrushes. By the afternoon there were a dozen of them sitting in the second-class saloon with scissors and needles, cutting up the ship's blankets and sewing them into little coats and trousers for the children who had come up out of the boats in their nightclothes. They sewed all afternoon, and talked very little, and when they had finished one coat they started another.

I carried thread. I carried scissors. I carried everything. I don't believe I stopped once between dawn and dark.

* * *

The captain had made up his mind by then about where to go.

He could have gone on to the Mediterranean, I suppose, and carried seven hundred people who had nothing, not even shoes, across the Atlantic and back. He could have gone to Halifax, which was nearer. He went back to New York. It was the right choice, the only one, and he made it as he made everything, all at once and without fuss. I heard about it from Stan, who heard it from a quartermaster, who had heard the captain say that the survivors would need their own country, and their own people, and their own papers, and all of that was in New York.

So we turned our back on the Mediterranean, and on our own passengers' holidays, and on the Hungarian families going home, and steamed west. Nobody aboard complained about that, either. Not one person, that I ever heard.

* * *

The wireless never stopped.

I went up to the cabin twice that afternoon with tea, and both times Cottam was at the key, in the same shirt he had been wearing at midnight. He had not slept. He did not look as if he knew what sleep was. He was sending names.

That was the captain's order. Two messages had gone first, one to the Cunard company and one to the White Star company, and then a short one to the Associated Press that the captain wrote out himself. After that, names. Every survivor, every name, one by one, to the shore stations and to the ships within reach, so that somewhere in New York, or in London, or in a village in Ireland or Sweden or Lebanon, somebody could stop waiting. He was sending them as fast as his hand would go, and his hand was very tired, and there were seven hundred of them, and the purser's lists had been made in a hurry at a cold gangway door by men whose fingers were numb, and half the names were spelled wrong.

And on the desk beside him, there was another pile. A growing one.

"What are those?" I said, the second time I came.

He didn't look up from the key. "Newspapers. Every newspaper in America. And a good many in England." He sent something, fast. "They want the story. They want to know what happened. Who's alive. Who's dead. Whether it's true about the millionaires. What it was like. They'll pay anything. One of them's offered me more than I make in a year."

"What are you going to do?"

"Nothing." He reached for the tea without looking at it. "Captain's orders. The press has had its one message, the one he wrote himself, and that's all it gets. After that it's names and official business. Nothing else." He drank. "He's right. If I start answering newspapers, I'll never get the names out. And it's the names that matter. It's the names people are waiting for." He put the cup down. "Let them wait. Let them all wait."

I looked at the pile. It was very tall. I could read the top one from where I stood. It was from a newspaper in New York, and it asked, politely, at great length, for a statement from any survivor, at any price.

I thought of Mr. Hatch, in his brown suit, going from blanket to blanket with his notebook.

I thought, then, for the first time, that what Mr. Hatch was doing on that ship might be worth a very great deal of money. And that he was not the only man aboard who would know it.

* * *

At four o'clock they buried the dead.

There were four. Some had been lifted up out of the boats that morning already dead, under blankets. One had come aboard alive, and died later that morning with a doctor beside him. One of the four had been a passenger, a gentleman, pulled out of the water still alive in the night, who died in the boat before dawn. The other three were Titanic's own crew. Two of them had names. One of them, nobody knew. Nobody in his boat had known him, and there was nothing in his pockets to tell.

They sewed them into canvas, the way it's done at sea, with a weight at the feet. They laid them on planks at the rail on the after deck, under flags. The engines were stopped again, for the first time since dawn, and in the silence you could hear the wind and the water and the gulls that had found us somewhere in the afternoon.

The same clergyman read the service. A few of us stood there. Not many. Most of the survivors stayed inside. I don't blame them.

I stood at the back, as I always stood at the back, with my cap in my hands. I have stood at a great many burials, Ms. Barnett, in a great many lives. I have stood at them for people I loved and for people I had never met, and I have buried people with my own hands, more than once. That was the only one I ever stood at for a man whose name nobody knew.

When the clergyman reached the words, the seamen tilted the planks, and one by one the four of them slid out from under the flags and went down into the gray water, and were gone.

I said the man's name to myself. The nameless one. I didn't know it, so I gave him one. I won't tell you what it was. It wasn't his, and he's had no use for it, and that's between him and me.

* * *

The young woman in the dark coat was at the burial.

I didn't see her come. She was standing a little apart, at the rail, with her hands in her pockets, alone. No children. She must have left them sleeping with their mother. She stood very straight and watched the service from beginning to end, and when the planks tilted she didn't look away, as most of us did. She watched each of the four go down into the water, all the way, until there was nothing to watch.

Then she turned to go in, and saw me.

I was standing by the hatch, with my cap in my hands, and I had been watching her watch. She knew it. I saw her know it. She stopped, and looked at me for a moment, quite steadily, as you look at a dog you don't know, to decide if it bites.

"You're the boy from the gangway," she said.

"Yes, miss."

"You gave me a blanket."

"Yes, miss."

She came a step closer. She had gray eyes, I remember, very clear, set a little too far apart, and a small white scar at the corner of her mouth, and she looked at me as if I were a page in a book she had not expected to find interesting.

"Do you always look at people like that?" she said.

"Like what, miss?"

"Like you're counting them."

I should have said *No, miss,* and looked at the deck, as Bertie Pike would have. That is what a bell boy says to a lady who speaks to him. I knew that perfectly well.

I didn't. I was tired, and I had been on my feet for sixteen hours, and I had just watched four men go into the sea, and I was a very old man, and I had not been spoken to as anything but furniture for three weeks.

"Somebody has to, miss," I said. "The purser wants every name."

Something moved in her face. Not a smile. Something underneath one, quick, and then gone.

"Yes," she said. "Every name." She looked at me a moment longer. Then she nodded, once, as if to herself, as if something had been filed. "Good afternoon," she said, and went in.

I stood by the hatch for some time after she'd gone.

I have told you that I put her away, that morning, in the saloon, just as I had once put away a waiter in a white jacket. That isn't quite true. I took her out again, by the hatch, for perhaps a minute. I turned her over. A young Englishwoman, educated, very composed, with gray eyes too far apart and a way of looking at a room. And then Lyle shouted for me from the companionway, and there was a lady in first class who wanted her trunk moved, and I put her away again, and went.

I have asked myself since, more times than I could count, what I would have done if Lyle had not shouted.

* * *

It was dark when I next saw Mr. Hatch.

He was in the second-class smoking room, in the corner by the window where he had set himself up on the Sunday afternoon with his typewriter. The typewriter was shut now. He had a cup of coffee in front of him that had gone cold, and his notebook open, and he was writing, fast, in pencil, in a hand so small and so cramped that I couldn't have read it if I had stood over him.

There were survivors asleep on the benches all around the walls. He was writing very quietly, so as not to wake them.

I brought him tea. He looked up, and saw who it was, and smiled, and his face was gray.

"Bertie," he said. "You're a saint."

"That's what the Marconi man says, sir."

"Then it must be true. He's a man of science." He took the tea and wrapped both hands around it. "Sit down a minute."

"I'm not allowed, sir."

"Nobody's looking. Everybody's asleep." He nodded at the bench. I sat on the very edge of it. He looked at me over the cup. "I've been talking to them all day," he said. "Since the boats. Forty-one people. Forty-one so far." He touched the notebook. "Do you know what they say, mostly?"

"No, sir."

"They say what they think they ought to say." He drank. "That everyone was very brave. That it was very calm. That the band played. That the men stood back and put the women in the boats and waved goodbye like gentlemen." He set the cup down. "And most of it's true, Bertie. That's the strange thing. Most of it's quite true. But they've all been on this ship eleven hours now, sitting together, telling each other, and every hour it gets a little more the same. By New York they'll all be telling one story. You'll see. The one everyone agrees on."

"Is that bad, sir?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and I saw him decide to tell me something, as a man tells a dog things, because the dog won't repeat them.

"It is when it isn't true," he said. "Not all the boats were the same, Bertie. Some of them went back. Most of them didn't. Some of them were half empty and could have gone back and didn't, because the people in them were afraid of being swamped. You can understand that. God knows I can understand that. But some of them..." He stopped. He turned his cup on the table, a quarter turn, and did not finish. "Some of them did things in the dark that the people in them would like very much to forget. And they're going to forget them, by Thursday. They're going to agree to forget them. Unless somebody wrote it down first."

"Did somebody tell you, sir? What happened? In one of the boats?"

He didn't answer that. He put his hand flat on the notebook, the way you keep a hand on a thing that might blow away.

"They'll want to know who had the oars," he said. "That's all. In New York, when the questions start. Somebody's going to ask who had the oars in every boat, and what they did with them." He picked up his pencil. "And I'm going to know."

Then he sent me away, quite gently, and went back to his writing.

I stood in the door of the smoking room for a moment with the empty tray, and looked back at him, a thin man in a brown suit, in the corner by the dark window, bent over a notebook, writing very small, among all those sleeping people.

I've thought about that picture a great deal, since. I thought about it two nights later, more than I wanted to.
