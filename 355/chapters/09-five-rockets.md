## Chapter 9: Five Rockets

Freda watched the lecture four times that week, all forty minutes, start to finish, and found nothing.

She watched the beginning, where a man from the library foundation came to the lectern and read a very long introduction off a card. She watched the slides. She watched the questions at the end, all six of them, and the faces of the people asking them, as far as the camera showed them, which wasn't far. She watched the last ninety seconds, after the applause, where the camera stayed on for no reason and people stood up and put on their coats and a woman in the second row dropped her program. She made notes, in a document on her laptop with a title I wasn't allowed to see. On Wednesday night she shut the laptop and said, in a voice I knew from her chemistry homework, that it was a stupid video and the camera person was an idiot and she was going to bed.

I didn't say anything. I'd watched it eleven times myself.

* * *

On the Thursday it rained again, a long gray rain that came in sideways across the field and drummed on the porch roof, and we sat in the kitchen with the door shut and the stove going and the window streaming. I said the date into the recorders, *session thirty-three, Thursday, June tenth*, and he poured the tea, and looked at the rain on the window, and smiled.

"Good," he said. "That's about right for this one. Only colder."

* * *

*February 1780*

Beckwith wanted Washington.

He didn't put it like that, the first time he spoke to me about it. He put it as a question about roads, the way he always did. But I knew what he meant before he'd finished the sentence, and so did he, and he watched me know it, and that was the first time I saw him nervous.

It was the week after Young's House. The ice was still hard. And Washington was sitting in a house in Morristown, in Jersey, thirty miles off, in the middle of a starving army in its huts, with a guard of a few hundred men round him, in the worst winter in living memory, when nobody in his army could imagine anything coming at them across the snow except more snow.

"A troop of horse," Beckwith said. "Three hundred, perhaps. Over the ice, at night, and across Jersey before light, by roads nobody's watching. Into the town. Out again with him in a sleigh, before his army knows he's gone." He had a map of Jersey on the table between us. His finger kept going back to one place on it. "What would they do, without him? That army? It's held together with string as it is. Take him away and the string goes."

"You'd have to get there, sir."

"That's what I'm asking you." He looked up. "Can it be done?"

I thought about it for a long time. I want you to know that I thought about it honestly, with everything I had, the way I'd have thought about it if it had been my own idea. Because there was a part of me, I'll admit it, that wanted to see it done. To be the man who brought George Washington across the ice to New York in a sleigh, and set him down in front of Knyphausen like a parcel. It would have been the greatest stroke of the whole war, from that side. It might have ended it.

And I didn't know how it would come out. I want you to understand that, because people always assume I must have. I don't know the future of any life I'm in, Ms. Barnett. I only know the ones I've finished. In February of 1780 Washington was a tired man in a farmhouse in Jersey, and for all I knew he'd be in New York by the end of the week, in a sleigh, and the war would be over, and I'd have done it. I wanted it, a little. And I was afraid of it, a little more, though I couldn't have told you why.

So I didn't tell Beckwith it couldn't be done. I told him the truth about the roads.

"It can be done, sir," I said. "In good snow. Not this snow."

* * *

He wanted to know why, and I told him.

The snow in Jersey was deep and soft that week, after the storms of January, too deep for horses to go fast. That was bad, but it wasn't the worst of it. The worst of it was the weather that was coming. I could smell it, the way Caesar's body had smelled it every winter of its life. A warm wind from the south, and rain on top of the snow, and then the cold coming back behind the rain, hard, overnight. When that happens, the top of the snow melts and freezes again, into a crust. A hard crust, like a pie, an inch thick, with soft snow underneath.

"A man can walk on a crust like that, sir, mostly, if he's careful," I said. "A horse can't. A horse goes through it at every step. Down through the crust into the soft snow, and up again, and down. And every time it goes through, the edge of the crust is like the edge of a knife, round the leg, just above the hoof, where there's no meat at all." I touched my own ankle. "Three miles of that and you'll have horses bleeding from all four legs. Six miles and they'll stop. They won't go on. No rider alive can make them."

"How do you know the rain's coming?"

"I don't know, sir. I'm telling you what the wind smells like."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he looked at the map. His finger was on Morristown again.

"Thank you," he said. "That'll be all."

* * *

He went to Knyphausen with the plan on the seventh of February, and the old general approved it. On the eighth, the rain came. Snow first, then rain, all day, warm and gray, just as I'd said. They put it off.

And then, on the night of the tenth, the cold came back hard, and froze the whole country over again, crust on top of snow, just as I'd said, and they went anyway.

I wasn't with them. Jersey wasn't my country, and they had Jersey Loyalists for guides who'd been born on those roads. About three hundred horse: the hussars of the Queen's Rangers, and the Seventeenth Dragoons, under their lieutenant colonel, a man called Birch, a good cavalry officer, quiet, who looked after his horses. And at the same time, to keep the rebels looking the wrong way, parties of foot went over from Staten Island under Simcoe and General Skinner, to make a noise along the shore.

Beckwith sent me to the Jersey shore that night, to the British post at Paulus Hook, across the river from the city, to listen. That was my whole part. To lie on the ice in the dark at the edge of Jersey and tell him in the morning what I'd heard.

I heard them go. A long way off, inland, the sound of horses moving fast on hard snow, and then not fast, and then hardly at all. I heard, very faintly, a horse screaming. Then another. If you've never heard a horse scream you won't know what I mean, and I hope you never do.

And at about two in the morning, I saw the rockets.

* * *

Five of them. One after another, going up out of the dark country to the west, very small at that distance, little threads of red fire climbing into the sky and bursting, one, two, three, four, five. Then nothing.

It was the signal for every party to turn back. Birch had got five miles, perhaps six, with his horses bleeding from every leg on the crust exactly as I'd told Beckwith they would, and he'd looked at the country still ahead of him, twenty-odd miles of it, and at his horses, and he'd done the only thing a good cavalry officer could do. He'd sent up the rockets and turned round.

I lay there on the ice and watched the last one fade, and I'll tell you honestly what I felt. I felt two things at once, and I've never been able to separate them, not in two hundred and forty-seven years. I felt sorry. For Beckwith, who'd wanted it so much, and for Birch's horses, and a little, God help me, for myself, who'd been one road away from being the man who caught Washington. And under that, lower down, I felt something I didn't have a name for, that was very much like relief.

Because I hadn't had to choose. I'd told the truth about the snow, and the truth hadn't been enough, and the weather had decided it instead of me. Whatever came of that war now, I hadn't been the man who carried Washington across the ice in a sack.

Hugh Gaine, the printer, wrote in his journal the next day that the troops had gone out to surprise General Washington at Morristown. That's very nearly all anybody wrote. That's all it ever was, in the record. A night ride that came to nothing.

I don't know that Washington ever knew how close it came. I've never found that he did.

He stopped, and got up, and stood at the window with his back to me, looking at the rain. It had settled in for the afternoon. The field was gray with it, and the hay rows had gone dark and flat.

"You were right," I said. "About the crust."

"I was right about the crust," he said, to the window. "Remember that, later. You'll need it." He came back and sat down. "That's twice now I've been right and been thanked and sent away. The first time it didn't matter. It didn't matter at all." He picked up his tea. "Let's do something smaller. I could do with something smaller."

* * *

*February 1780*

The thaw came at the end of February. The ice in the harbor went gray, and then soft, and then it began to break up at the edges, with great groans and cracks you could hear all over the city at night, and the first boats tried the river again and got through. Everybody in the city was glad. The wood boats came back. The firewood came back. People stopped dying in their cellars.

And everybody who wanted to leave the city without being seen knew that they had about a week left.

There was a soldier in one of the British regiments in the city, a corporal, whose name I won't give you, because he has descendants, and he was a fool rather than a villain, and he's been punished enough. He'd decided to desert to the rebels. Lots of men did, from both sides, every week of that war. But he'd decided to take something with him, so that the rebels would be glad to see him. He'd made a drawing. All the posts on the north end of the island, the guns at each, the number of men, the hours the guard changed. He'd done it well. He'd been a draughtsman's apprentice in Bristol before the army got him.

Prince found out about it first. He was building a gun platform at one of those posts, and he'd watched the corporal pacing out distances with his lips moving, and drawing in a little book when he thought nobody was looking. Nobody ever thinks a carpenter is looking.

Prince told me, in the shed, at night. He wasn't sure what to do with it. A Black workman accusing a British corporal of anything, in that city, with nothing but his own eyes to show for it, was likely to end up with his back laid open for insolence, and the corporal free.

"Then we don't accuse him," I said. "We wait till he goes. And we bring him back with the book in his pocket. Nobody argues with a book."

* * *

There was a third man with us. A wagoner called Isaac, who'd come in from Jersey the year before, a big slow good-tempered man who could lift the back of a cart off the ground by himself. We needed him for that. We didn't need anything clever. We needed somebody the corporal couldn't argue with.

I worked out where the corporal would go. It wasn't hard. The ice was going everywhere except in one place, at the top of the island, in the narrow water by Kingsbridge, where it still held, just, in the cold mornings. And I worked out when: the first night the guard on that post was men from another regiment, who didn't know his face. It took him four nights to find one. We waited all four, the three of us, in the cold, in a cowshed above the shore. Prince sang hymns under his breath. Isaac slept. I watched.

On the fourth night he came down to the shore in the dark with a bundle, and stepped out onto the ice, and we stepped out behind him.

He ran, of course. They always run. He got about forty yards out, and then the ice under him made the long low singing sound it made when it was thinking about going, and he stopped dead, as anybody would, and Isaac walked out behind him, quite calmly, on the thicker ice I showed him, and picked him up round the waist like a sack of meal and carried him back to the shore.

The book was in his coat. Prince found it. He held it up in the starlight so the corporal could see it, and didn't say a word.

We walked him to the guard at Kingsbridge, the three of us, with his hands tied with Isaac's belt, and handed him and his little book to the officer of the guard. The officer read the book by his lantern for a long time, turning the pages, and then he looked at the three of us standing in the snow, and he looked at the corporal, and he didn't say thank you. He said, "Wait there," and went in, and wrote something, and came out again, and gave each of us a shilling, and told us to go home.

"That's it?" I said. "A shilling?"

"A shilling each." He smiled. "Isaac bought a hat with his. A good one. He wore it till he died."

"What happened to the corporal?"

"They didn't hang him. They should have, by their own rules. But he'd been caught before he got anywhere, by three men nobody was ever going to call as witnesses at a court martial, and so it was easier to flog him and send him to the West Indies, where the fever would very likely do it for them." He was quiet. "It did, I think. A year later."

"And you three?"

"We went back to the shed." He looked at the rain. "But that one's in the record, Ms. Barnett. I've always rather liked that. It's the only time I'm in the record that whole war, under any name at all. In Clinton's papers, somewhere, there's a line. The officer wrote it up, that night, and it went to headquarters with the corporal's book, and it's still there, as far as I know, in a box in a library." He turned his cup. "It doesn't say Caesar Freeman. It doesn't say Prince, or Isaac. It says the corporal was caught and brought in by three Black men. That's all. Three of them, no names, in a line on a page." He looked at me. "But it's true. It's the truest line anybody ever wrote about me."

I wrote down the words exactly as he'd said them. *A deserter, caught and brought in by three Black men.* And under it, *Clinton's papers.* And under that, *Find it.* Then I underlined *Find it* twice, which I really don't do, and he watched me do it from across the table, with the rain behind him, and didn't say anything at all, and I saw that he was pleased.
