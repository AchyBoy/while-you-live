# 355

## Chapter 17: What Ice Does to Sound

Freda came.

I'd asked her on the Thursday night, at the kitchen table, carefully, the way you ask a sixteen-year-old anything in July. He'd asked for her, I said. He'd said he didn't want to tell September twice. She'd looked at me over her laptop for a long moment, and then said, "Is it the bad part?" and I'd said I thought it was, and she'd said, "Okay," and gone back to typing, and then stopped typing and said, "Should I bring something? Like, a thing?"

She brought a pie. She made it herself, on the Wednesday night, peach, from a recipe of her father's mother's that Dez had texted her, and it came out of the oven at eleven o'clock looking like something from a magazine, and she stood over it with her hands on her hips and said, "That's going to be a disaster," and covered it with a dish towel and wouldn't let me look at it again.

She sat in the front seat with it on her knees the whole way up. Two hours. She didn't put her headphones on once. When we turned onto the gravel road, she sat up and looked at everything: the long field, mowed short and pale, and the line of trees, and the little house at the end with the porch and the maple, and him at the bottom of the steps, under the maple, with his hands in his pockets, watching the car come up the road.

"He's waiting for us," Freda said.

"He's been doing that."

"Since when?"

"Since last week."

She was quiet. Then she said, very quietly, "That's nice," in a voice I hadn't heard her use since she was about nine.

* * *

He was delighted with the pie. Not polite. Delighted. He lifted the dish towel and looked at it for a long time, and then at Freda, and then he said, "Peach," as if she'd brought him something he'd been trying to remember the name of for a hundred years. He made her tell him the recipe, all of it, standing in his kitchen, and asked whose it was, and when she said her grandmother's, her dad's mom's, he said, "Then it'll be good," and she went pink to the ears.

We had it for breakfast. He said that was allowed, in July, and that he had it on good authority. He made coffee, and put the pie on the kitchen table with three plates and three forks and a knife, and let Freda cut it, and it was not a disaster. It was the best pie I've ever eaten. I've told her so since, and she says I'm just saying it, and I'm not.

I said the date into the recorders with my mouth full. *Session thirty-eight. Thursday, July fifteenth. Freda's here.*

"Freda's here," he said, and lifted his coffee to her.

* * *

I told him about the silence while we ate. Two weeks, now. No letter from Carrow House. No call from their lawyers. No call from Claire Whitcombe. Nothing.

"I think she's protecting herself," I said. "I think she's decided the safest thing is to do nothing. If she tells her father I came, he'll panic, and call the lawyers, and then there'll be lawyers, and then there'll be questions, and somebody might ask her where she was on a Wednesday morning six years ago. So she says nothing. And if she says nothing to him, she'll certainly say nothing to me." I put my fork down. "I think it's over. I think she's just going to wait me out."

He finished his piece of pie first. He took his time.

"Freda," he said. "Did your mother ever tell you about the ice?"

Freda looked up, surprised. "The ice in the harbor? I typed it. When it froze. When you lay on the blanket and listened to the sleighs."

"What did I say about it?"

She thought. She'd typed every word of it. I watched her find it.

"You said sound carries. On the ice. When it's still and cold. You could hear a dog bark in New Jersey."

"I did. And what's the other half of it?"

She didn't know. Neither did I. He waited, and when neither of us said anything, he turned his cup a quarter turn on the table.

"When an army's coming," he said, "the dogs stop barking. On a still night on the ice you can hear every dog on the Jersey shore, every night, all night, barking at the cold and the dark and each other. And one night, they stop. A whole village of dogs, gone quiet. And it's the loudest thing you've ever heard. Because a dog stops barking for one reason. Somebody it knows has come into the yard, and told it to be still."

Freda had stopped eating.

"Your mother's right," he said to her, "that the dog's gone quiet. She's wrong about why." He looked at me. "If Claire Whitcombe were protecting herself, she'd have told her father the same night. That's what you do, if it's yourself you're afraid for. You call the strongest person you know and say, *somebody came*. You get your lawyers ready. You get ahead of it. Every frightened person in the world does that."

"She didn't," I said slowly.

"She didn't. She's kept quiet two weeks. She's kept it from the strongest person she knows, who's also the person who'd most want to know." He set his cup down. "People don't keep quiet like that for themselves, Ms. Barnett. They keep quiet like that for somebody else. Somebody who'd be in danger if her father panicked, and started making telephone calls. Somebody the telephone calls might reach."

* * *

Nobody said anything for a while. Outside, the bees were working in the clover. In the field a hawk was hanging over the short grass, quite still, with its wings moving very slightly, the way they do when they're watching something.

"Who?" Freda said at last. She said it to him, not to me.

He didn't answer. He looked at me. He was waiting for me to say it, the way he'd waited in the spring for me to find the eggs, and the way he'd waited on the first day, last October, for me to decide whether he was mad.

I didn't want to say it. I'd stopped letting myself think it, years ago. You do, after a while. You put a thing down so you can carry everything else.

"Somebody they're hiding," I said.

"Yes."

"Somebody who'd be in danger if Carrow House's lawyers started asking where they'd gone. Somebody who's been somewhere, all this time, that only the Whitcombes know about." I could hear my voice going thin. "You think he's alive."

"I think," he said carefully, "that a woman sat in an alley for twenty-seven minutes with the engine running, while her father and another man carried two boxes and a computer down a flight of stairs. And that the other man was the one whose cat was shut in the bedroom. And that she drove them both away. And that six years later, when a stranger came to her barn and asked how long she'd waited, she went white, and asked her to leave, and then told nobody, not even her father. Not for her own sake." He paused. "I think you should let yourself think it, Ms. Barnett. I think you've earned that much. I don't know any more than you do. But I've been on the ice. I know what it means when the dogs go quiet."

* * *

Freda reached across the table and put her hand on top of mine. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. She'd been ten, the summer it happened. She'd sat on the stairs at night, the week after, and listened to me crying in the kitchen and pretending to be on the phone. We'd never once talked about it.

"So what do I do?" I said.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing at all. You've done the one thing. She knows you know. She knows you came yourself, with nothing in your hands, and asked her one question, and went away when she asked you to. She knows you haven't told her father, or the lawyers, or the newspaper, because if you had, she'd have heard about it by now." He picked up the knife and cut himself a second, very small piece of pie. "You've left her a card on a bench. That's the most frightening thing you can give a person who's been keeping a secret for six years. A way out that they have to choose for themselves."

"How long?" I said. "You said longer than I'd like."

He thought about it.

"If I'm right about why she's quiet," he said, "she's asking him. Whoever it is. She's driven out to wherever it is and sat with him and told him a woman came to the barn, and he's had to decide what he wants. And he's had six years of wanting not to be found." He ate the piece of pie. "But he chose you, once. Out of every reporter in the city. He picked up a telephone and called a newsroom and asked for you by name. A man doesn't forget why he did that."

* * *

Then he got up, and took the plates to the sink, and stood there for a moment with his back to us and his hands on the edge of the counter, looking out of the window at the hawk over the field.

"Freda," he said, without turning round. "Have you typed all of it? The whole year so far?"

"Everything," Freda said. "Up to last week. The boat and the hounds and the man coming down the stairs."

"Then you'll have noticed the eggs."

I looked at Freda. She looked at me. Then she looked at the back of his head, and said, with great dignity, "I'm not stupid."

He laughed. He turned round, still laughing, with his hand on the counter.

"No," he said. "No, you are very much not. Don't tell me what you think. I don't want to hear it. Not yet. I want you to hear the next part the way I lived it." He came back to the table and sat down, and folded his hands on it, and the laughing went out of his face, slowly, the way the light goes off a field when a cloud comes over. "Because I didn't know, then, about the eggs. I didn't know anything. I knew a young man in a beautiful coat had trusted me with a secret, and that I'd have done anything for him. And that's what I want you to have, both of you, for the next part. Just that."

He looked at the recorders, and at me, and nodded.

"September," he said.
