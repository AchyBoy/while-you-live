## Chapter 10: The Woman at the Edge of the Frame

It was the last week of school, and Freda had stopped pretending to care about it.

She had two exams left and a locker to empty and a chemistry teacher who had given up teaching and was showing films. She came home at two every day instead of four. On the Friday after the rain, I came back from the grocery store at three and found her at the kitchen table with the laptop and the big white headphones and a bowl of dry cereal she was eating with her fingers, one piece at a time, without looking at it.

"I said it was a stupid video," she said, without taking the headphones off. "I was right. It is."

"I know."

"So I stopped watching it." She pulled one side of the headphones away from her ear. "He does it every year. The talk. You said."

"Every spring. The enemies of paper."

"So there's more than one video." She turned the laptop round so I could see. It was the historical society's page, the plain blue one I'd found in the spring, with the link to the talk. Only she'd gone past the link, to a page I hadn't known was there, a list. *Annual Spring Lecture, Archive.* Five years of them, each with its own little gray thumbnail of a man in a bow tie at a lectern. "They put them all up. Nobody's watched most of them. One of them has nine views. I'm like four of them."

I put the groceries down on the counter.

"You watched all five."

"Not all of all five. That would be over three hours of a man talking about mold." She ate a piece of cereal. "Just the ends. The last five minutes of each one. After everybody claps."

"Why the ends?"

"Because that's when the camera person forgets the camera's on," Freda said. "In every single one of them, they forget. They just leave it running on the stage, and he picks up his notes, and people come up and talk to him, and the camera just sits there and watches. Like that one at the end of the first video where the woman drops her program." She tapped the screen. "That's where the people are. I said."

"You did say."

"So." She dragged the cursor to the third thumbnail on the list. *Spring Lecture, 2024.* "Watch this. It's at thirty-eight forty."

She took the headphones off and unplugged them, and turned the sound up, and pressed play.

* * *

It was a different room from the one in the video I knew. A smaller one, with a low ceiling and folding chairs and a portrait of a man in a wig on the wall behind the lectern. The same bow tie. A different color. He was saying thank you for coming, and the audience was clapping, the thin, dutiful clapping of fifty people in a library on a Sunday afternoon in March.

Then he stepped back from the lectern and began gathering his notes, and the clapping died away, and the camera stayed on him, exactly as Freda had said, with nobody at all thinking about it.

There was a step down from the little stage at the front of the room. One step. Six inches, perhaps, carpeted. He came to the edge of it with his notes in one hand, and stopped, and looked down at it, and you could see him decide it was further than it looked.

And a woman came into the frame from the right.

She came quickly, the way you come for someone you've come for a hundred times before. Fifty or so. Tall, with gray coming into short dark hair, in jeans and a fleece vest over a flannel shirt and the kind of boots people wear for actual mud. She didn't make anything of it. She just put her hand under his elbow as he stepped down, not taking his weight, just there, and he leaned on it for exactly as long as the step took and no longer, and didn't look at her, and she didn't look at him either. They'd done it before. You could tell from how little either of them did.

She said something to him. The microphone on the lectern was still live, a little way off, and it caught her, just.

*Dad, I'm in a loading zone. Don't let them keep you.*

He laughed. Not the big laugh. A small one, for her. And said something I couldn't hear. And she put her other hand in her vest pocket and took it out with her keys, and stood there jingling them, impatient and patient at once, while a man in a cardigan came up and shook his hand and began to tell him something at length.

Freda reached past me and paused it.

"Look at her keys," she said.

I looked. Car keys, two of them, and a little bunch of smaller ones, and a key ring. A brass key ring in the shape of a horse's head. And on the fleece vest, on the left side, over her heart, an embroidered patch, too small to read at that size.

"I made it bigger," Freda said, and clicked to another window on her laptop, where she'd taken a screenshot of that frame and blown it up until it went to squares. The squares were enough. A horse, in outline, jumping. And a name in an arc over it, and under it a smaller word.

*PURCELLVILLE.*

* * *

I didn't say anything for a long moment. Then I went and got my bag off the hook in the hall and came back and took out my notebook, and opened it at the page with my fourteen names on it in a column, and put it on the table in front of her, next to the laptop. And I put my finger on one of them.

*Claire (daughter), Loudoun Co.*

And then on the line I'd written next to Margaret Whitcombe's name, from her obituary, the week before, because I make a note of everything.

*In lieu of flowers, horse rescue, Purcellville.*

Freda looked at it. Then at the squares on her screen. Then at me.

"Oh my God," she said.

"Yes."

"His daughter."

"His daughter drives him," I said. "Three years ago she drove him to a library on a Sunday afternoon and parked in a loading zone and came in and stood at the side of the room for forty minutes so she could put her hand under his elbow for one step." I could hear my own voice, and it didn't sound like mine. "She's done it a hundred times. You can see she has. Tom asked me who drives a man of seventy-three into the city. That's who. That's exactly who."

"The person who wasn't laughing," Freda said.

"Maybe. It's not proof."

"I know it's not proof." She looked offended. "I'm not stupid. It's a fleece." She looked at the frozen frame, at the woman with her hand still half out of her pocket with the keys. "But she's somebody you'd sit in a car for. If you were her. You'd sit there in the car for him for half an hour and not even ask, because he's your dad and he's old and he asked you." She stopped. Then she said, in a different voice, quieter, "I'd do it for mine."

I looked at her. She was looking at the screen.

"I know you would," I said.

* * *

That night I did what I should have done a week before, and looked up the daughter.

It was easy, now I knew where to stand. Claire Whitcombe had kept her name. She was a veterinarian, a large-animal vet, with a practice of her own outside Purcellville, horses mostly, and a page on the practice's website with a photograph of her in a barn, in the same kind of fleece vest, with her hand on a horse's neck, not smiling for the camera, looking at the horse. She was on the board of the horse rescue her mother's obituary had named. She'd written an article for a county horse magazine about laminitis that I read all the way through without understanding a word of it. She'd been divorced for nine years. Two sons, both in college. She lived twenty minutes from her father.

She had no record of anything. Of course she didn't. She'd been the one who stayed in the car.

I wrote it all down on a fresh page under her name, and then I sat with my hand flat on it for a long time, the way I'd sat in a library reading room in May, with my hand over a page in an old auction catalog.

"You should go," Freda said from the couch. She was supposed to be asleep. "Tomorrow. Before she finds out."

"Finds out what?"

"That somebody's looking. People always find out. Maddie's mom found out about the thing with Maddie's chemistry lab in like a day."

"She won't find out," I said. "I'm not going tomorrow."

"Why not?"

Because of what he'd said. Because the man who was laughing was used to being believed, and the woman who wasn't laughing had been telling herself the same story every night for six years until it was smooth as a stone. Because a person can be asked a question for the first time exactly once, and I'd get one chance at her, at the most, before she called her father, and her father called Carrow House, and the door shut for good.

"Because I want to get it right," I said. "I'm going to ask Tom first."

Freda was quiet on the couch for a while. Then she said, into the dark, "Can I tell him? In my letter. That it was me that found her."

She'd written to him a few times, since the spring. Short letters, on lined paper, folded in thirds. He wrote back. I'd never read either side, and I wasn't going to.

"Yes," I said. "You should tell him it was you."

"It was me," Freda said, to the ceiling, with enormous satisfaction, and turned over, and was asleep, I think, in about a minute.

* * *

I stayed at the table.

Before I went to bed I watched the end of that video once more, by myself, with the sound low. The step. The hand under the elbow. *Dad, I'm in a loading zone.* The keys.

And then something else, that I hadn't seen the first time because I'd been looking at the keys. As the man in the cardigan talked, and her father listened and nodded and was charming, the daughter stopped jingling her keys and looked away from them both, toward the back of the room, toward the door, toward wherever the car was waiting in its loading zone. Just for a second. Not impatient. Something else.

I've seen that look on a great many faces, in a great many parking lots, in the old days. It's the look of a person who is listening for an engine.
