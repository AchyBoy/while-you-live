# 355

## Chapter 2: The Side That Let Me In

The hay was down in the long field when I drove up on the Thursday, cut and lying in rows to dry, and the whole valley smelled of it with the windows open. I had never been up there in hay weather. In the fall it had been leaves and woodsmoke, and in the spring it had been mud, and now it was this, sweet and heavy and a little like tea, and for the last mile of the gravel road I drove slowly so it would last.

He was on the porch bench, in his shirtsleeves again, with the cardigan folded beside him out of habit. He didn't need it. It was eighty degrees at eleven in the morning. When he saw the car he raised one hand off the rail, the way he had the week before, and I came up the steps and sat in my chair, and put the two recorders on the little table between us, and he watched me do it with the look he had for small rituals.

"You found the coat a good home," he said.

"Back of the closet. Behind the vacuum."

"Good." He looked out at the field.

I started both recorders. The red lights came on. "Session twenty-nine," I said to them. "Thursday, the thirteenth of May."

"You said it was summer," I said.

"It was."

"I looked it up. You said you spent two years in New York hunting a spy. I found the spies. And I found Benedict Arnold, and Major André, and a lot of websites about a woman called Agent 355."

He turned his head and looked at me, and something happened at the corner of his mouth that wasn't quite a smile.

"Did you," he said.

"They don't agree about her. Some of them say she was arrested and died on a prison ship. Some of them say she had a baby on it. One of them says nobody knows anything about her at all."

"That one's right."

"So she was real."

"There was no Agent 355," he said. "Nobody in that war was ever called that. It was made up a long time afterward, by people who liked the sound of it." He settled back against the porch wall. "Washington's spies wrote to each other in a little code. They had a book of numbers, each number a word, so a letter that fell into the wrong hands would read like a page out of an almanac. Seven hundred and eleven was Washington. Seven hundred and twenty-seven was New York. Three hundred and fifty-five," he said, "was the word *lady*."

I wrote that down. I wrote it down exactly, and the number, and then I looked at it.

"Just lady," I said.

"Just lady. One of them used it once, in one letter, in August of my first summer there. He wrote that he meant to go into New York, past the men searching everybody at the ferry, *by the assistance of a lady of my acquaintance.* That's the whole of her. One line. A word in a code book." He was quiet a moment. "Everything else people have said about her since, they made up because one line wasn't enough for them."

"Was she real?" I said. "The lady."

He didn't answer straight away. Down in the field a pair of crows had come down on the hay rows and were walking along them like men inspecting a fence.

"Oh, yes," he said. "She was real."

And then he didn't say anything else about her at all, and I knew him well enough by then to know that he wasn't going to, not that day, and that I'd be a fool to push. I put a small mark in the margin next to *lady*, which is a thing I do when I've been told I'll be told later.

* * *

"Why the British?" I said.

I'd been carrying it since the porch the week before, and I'd meant to come to it slowly. It came out instead, the way questions do when you've practiced them too much in the car.

"I asked you, in the winter, why the Confederate side," I said. "You said you didn't choose. You woke up in a body that was already wearing the uniform. Is that what happened again?"

"No," he said. "Not this time. This time I chose."

"Why?"

"Because they let me in."

I waited. He looked at me, and I saw him decide something, the way I had seen him decide things all winter: not whether to tell me, I think, but how much it was going to cost to say it plainly, and whether I was going to be worth the cost. Then he folded his hands on his knee.

"My name that time was Caesar," he said. "I woke up in him in the winter of 1778, in the dark, in a loft over a barn in Westchester County, north of New York, under a horse blanket, with two other men breathing next to me. He was about twenty-nine years old. He was strong, and he'd been worked hard all his life, and he belonged to a man called Reynolds, who had a farm with good bottom land and a captain's commission in the rebel militia." He paused. "He was owned, Ms. Barnett. That body was owned. That's how I came into that war."

I didn't say anything. I don't think there was anything to say. In the field the crows went up all at once, for no reason I could see, and came down again further along.

"You don't have to make a face," he said, gently. "You're thinking of the winter. Of the Keene farm in Maryland, and Damon, who worked it twenty years for nothing and read in secret, fast, when nobody was looking. And now me, here, on the other end of the same thing."

"Something like that."

"It's the same thing," he said. "It's exactly the same thing, eighty-five years earlier and two hundred miles north. Slavery wasn't a Southern word yet, in 1778. There were more enslaved people in New York City than in any city in the North. It was everywhere. It was normal. The men who signed the Declaration of Independence owned people while they signed it, some of them, and the men I'd be fighting the next two years were fighting for liberty with their own property waiting at home to bring in the hay." He looked at the field. "Captain Reynolds was a decent man by the measure of his neighbors. He went to church. He didn't beat anybody that I saw. He believed every word of the Declaration. He just didn't think it meant me."

* * *

"I want to tell you how it felt," he said, after a while, "so that you understand the rest. Not because it's the interesting part. It's not the interesting part. But I'd be lying to you about the whole two years if I skipped it."

"All right."

"I've woken up in a great many bodies," he said. "I told you that, the first day. Some of them were rich. Some of them were sick. Some of them were children, which is its own kind of prison. But I had never once, in all of it, woken up as property. As a thing on a list, between the plow and the cow, with a value written next to me in a man's account book." He was quiet a moment. "The first morning, I went out to the barn with the other two men in the dark, and I did the work, because the body knew how, and I watched my own hands do it. And around noon, Reynolds came out of the house with his account book under his arm, to count the hay, and he looked straight at me, and I understood that he wasn't seeing anybody. He was seeing a pair of arms he'd paid for. And I had been, in that life and a hundred others, a man people listened to."

"What did you do?"

"I did the work," he said. "For six months. I didn't have a choice. The roads were watched by both armies, and by men who weren't in either one, who'd sell a runaway back to his owner for a reward or to somebody else for more. There was nowhere to go. I'd have been dead or sold before I reached the river." He turned one hand over on his knee and looked at the palm. "So I learned. That body knew every road in that county, and every ford, and every farm, and which dogs bit. It knew horses better than I've ever known them in any life since. It knew how to read the ice on a pond from a hundred yards away. Those were his, not mine. He'd learned them from being sent everywhere, all his life, to fetch and carry. And I learned the rest. Who was on which side. Which neighbor was a rebel and which one was only pretending to be, for safety. Where the British lines were, down at Kingsbridge, and how the men who crossed them did it."

"And then you crossed."

"In June of 1779," he said. "Because of two pieces of paper."

* * *

He told me about the two pieces of paper the way he'd told me about the knots and the coal in the Carpathia's stokehold, in March: slowly, with numbers, as if he wanted me to be able to check every one of them later. Which I did.

The first was older, and it wasn't even New York's. In November of 1775, at the very beginning of the war, the British governor of Virginia, Lord Dunmore, had promised freedom to any enslaved man who belonged to a rebel and could carry a gun for the King. Only rebels' men, only men who could fight, only in Virginia. But word of it went everywhere. It went up and down the coast in a few weeks, in the way that news goes when the people carrying it can't read and can't be stopped from talking.

"Every enslaved man in Westchester knew about Dunmore," he said, "years before I got there. They'd talked about nothing else. And they knew what happened to most of the men who went: they died of smallpox in camps on the Virginia shore. The other two in that loft could tell you the whole story of it, and they did, at night, over and over. It was the only story they had that went anywhere."

The second paper was the one that mattered. In June of 1779, the British commander in New York, Sir Henry Clinton, issued a proclamation from a place called Philipsburg, up the Hudson. He said that any enslaved person who ran away from a rebel master to the British lines would be given full security, inside those lines, to follow any occupation he thought proper.

"Full security," Tom said. "To follow any occupation which he shall think proper. I can still say it. I read it over a hundred times, because I wanted to find the word that wasn't in it."

"Which word?"

"*Free*," he said. "It isn't there. It never once says free. It says you'll be safe and you can work, which isn't the same thing at all, and a man who reads the way I read could see exactly how much room the King's lawyers had left themselves. It said nothing about Loyalists' people. A man who belonged to a Loyalist could run to the British and be handed straight back. And it said that any Black man taken in arms in the rebel army would be bought from whoever caught him, at a fixed price, like a horse." He shook his head slightly. "It was a careful piece of paper. It was written by men who wanted the rebels' farms emptied of workers, and didn't want to frighten their own side, and didn't much mind what happened to anybody in between."

"So why go?"

"Because of the other one." He held up one finger. "A few weeks before Clinton's proclamation, the commandant of New York City, a general called Daniel Jones, issued an order to his own officers, plainer than anything his commander ever signed. One line. *All Negroes that fly from the Enemy's Country are Free.*" He let it sit a moment. "Free. Capital F. In a general order, in New York, in June of 1779. That one made it up to the farms in about a week, the same way Dunmore had."

I wrote *Gen. Daniel Jones, June 1779, "Free"* in my notebook and drew a box round it. I could see him watching me do it.

"Check it," he said. "People get his name wrong. Somebody misprinted it once, in a book people trust, and he's been David ever since. It was Daniel."

* * *

He went in at noon and came out with lunch on a tray: cold chicken, a loaf of the bread from the bakery in town, which had opened again, and a jar of iced tea with mint in it from the bed by the back step. We ate on the porch with the plates on our knees and the recorders running between us. He ate more than I'd seen him eat in weeks. I noticed it, and I noticed myself noticing it, and I didn't write it down.

"How did you get away?" I said. "In June."

"I walked." He tore a piece of bread in half. "Reynolds sent me to a mill on the Croton with a wagon of grain, on a Saturday, alone, because I'd been there a dozen times and always come back. I left the wagon at the mill, with the grain still in it and the horses fed and watered, so nobody could say I'd stolen a single thing from him. And I walked south. Two nights, through the Neutral Ground. That's what they called the country between the armies, thirty miles of it, where neither side could keep order and both sides' bandits did as they liked. I slept in the day in a hollow by a creek. I knew every road, so I didn't use any of them." He chewed. "The second night I came down to the British lines at Kingsbridge, at the top of Manhattan Island, and walked up to a picket of Hessians with my hands open, and said, in English, that I was come in on the General's proclamation. They didn't understand a word. They sent for somebody who did."

"And they let you in."

"They let me in." He set the bread down. "I've been asked, more than once, in more than one century, how I could have served the King against a country being born. That's how. I knocked on a door, and one side opened it, and the other side owned me." He looked at me. "I don't say the British were good. They weren't, particularly. Their officers used men like me exactly as long as we were useful, and their Loyalist friends in the city spat at us in the street. Half the men who came in on those proclamations died of fever in the camps. Some of them were sold to the West Indies by the very officers who'd promised them security. The British were the side that wanted something from me. That's all. But they were the side that let me in."

"You said last week," I said, carefully, "that you'd never decided how you felt about it. About losing. About there being a country, partly because you lost."

"I haven't."

"Because of this."

"Because of this," he said. "Britain passed the law ending slavery in its empire in 1833. In this country it took another thirty years, and a war that killed more than six hundred thousand. If the side I was on that summer had won..." He stopped himself, and shook his head. "No. That's the kind of sum I don't do. You can't run history backward and pick out the parts you like. A different war is a different everything. Maybe it ends sooner. Maybe it never ends at all. I don't know. Nobody knows." He looked out at the field. "I only know that I've lived in this country a long time, since. I've loved it. I still do. And that the first time I ever saw it, it was a man in a militia coat counting me with his hay."

* * *

We didn't do any more 1780 that day. I think we both knew we wouldn't. At half past two he said he'd start properly next Thursday, at the beginning, at Kingsbridge, if I'd bring a fresh notebook, and I said I always did.

I put the recorders in my bag. Then I stopped, on the top step, because there was a question I'd forgotten I had, and it had come back to me with the hay smell, the way things come back.

"What was his name?" I said. "Caesar what?"

He had picked up the cardigan, out of habit, though he didn't need it. He held it in both hands.

"He didn't have one," he said. "Not when I woke up in him. He was Reynolds's Caesar, the way the gray mare was Reynolds's gray mare. That's how they wrote him down." He folded the cardigan over his arm. "The British wanted a surname, for their books. The officer at Kingsbridge asked me for one, that first night, with his pen over the page. A lot of men who came in that year took their master's name, because it was the only one they'd ever been called. I wasn't going to do that."

"What did you tell him?"

"Freeman," he said. "Caesar Freeman. It was the first thing I'd owned in that body. I chose it myself, with a Hessian holding a lantern over me, and I watched him write it down." He smiled, very slightly. "He spelled it right. I checked."

I went down the steps and across the gravel to the car. When I looked back, he had gone in, and the door was open behind him, and through it I could see the mantel and the empty frame, and the hay smell came across the yard on the heat, and somewhere in the field the crows were still walking up and down the rows, counting.
