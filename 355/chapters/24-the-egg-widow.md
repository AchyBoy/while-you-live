## Chapter 24: The Egg Widow

It rained on the Thursday. A steady, gentle, all-day rain, the first in three weeks, the kind farmers stand at windows and watch. He had the stove lit in the kitchen when we came in, though it wasn't cold, and the kettle on, and three cups set out on the table, and in the middle of the table, on a plate, there was a little brown bowl with a dozen eggs in it.

Freda saw them and stopped in the kitchen doorway.

"Sit down," he said. "They're for later. I'll make you an omelet when it's done. I make a very good omelet." He poured the tea. "Session forty-one, Ms. Barnett."

I said it into the recorders. *Session forty-one. Thursday, August twelfth. Raining. Freda's here.* And then, because I couldn't help it, *There are eggs on the table.*

"Twelve," Freda said, from the doorway. She hadn't moved.

"Twelve," he agreed, and smiled at her. "Come and sit down, Freda. Nothing's happening today. It's a dozen."

* * *

*December 1780*

They let the ferryman out of the Provost two days after Christmas.

I was there. I'd asked to be told, and I was told, and I went and stood across the street from the gate in the gray morning and waited. It was cold, not like the winter before, an ordinary December cold with a little snow in the gutters. The gate opened at about nine, and the old man came out alone, in his own clothes, with my blanket folded over his arm, blinking, and stood in the street for a moment as if he didn't know which way the river was.

Then he turned and walked down toward the East River. Slowly, an old man's walk, in the cold. And I followed him, the way I'd followed a hundred people that year, at a distance, on the other side of the street.

He went straight to the ferry. I'd thought he would. Thirty years on those sweeps. Where else would he go?

He didn't go down the stairs to the boats. He stopped at the top. At the cart.

* * *

I'd seen her there a hundred times. Two hundred. Twice a week for a year and a half, with her basket of eggs under a cloth and her butter in its crock and her little iron pot of coals for the cold mornings. I'd bought eggs from her and put them inside my shirt and walked away without ever once looking at her face.

I watched her look up and see him.

She didn't say anything. She didn't make anything of it. She took two eggs out of the basket, without asking, and put them in his hand, and closed his fingers over them with her own. And held his hand closed, round the eggs, for a moment longer than anybody would have needed to. And said something to him, very low. And he nodded, and put the eggs in his coat, and went down the stairs to the boats without looking back.

She didn't charge him. I watched her not charge him.

And she looked up, then, across the street, straight at me.

Just for a moment. Not long. The way you'd look at a man you'd sold eggs to twice a week for a year and a half, standing across the street on a cold morning for no reason. And then she looked away, and pulled the cloth back over her basket, and rubbed her hands over her little pot of coals.

I stood there in the snow in the gutter. And something went down my arms, the way it does when a thing goes click, the way your mother's told me it does for her. And I didn't know yet what it was. I knew only that I'd been looking at the wrong thing for a year and a half, and that I'd just looked at the right one, and that it had looked back.

* * *

[now]

He stopped. The rain was steady on the roof. Freda had come and sat down, finally, at the table, with her hands round her cup, and she was looking at the bowl of eggs.

"You didn't know," Freda said. "Even then."

"Not then. Not on the street. It took me the whole night." He turned his cup a quarter turn. "I want to tell you how. Because it's the only time in my life that body's eyes did exactly what they were for. And I want you to see it the way I saw it."

* * *

*December 1780*

I went back to the shed by the North River that night, and lay down on my bed with my coat over me, and shut my eyes, and went back.

You've never had eyes like Caesar's. Nobody has. I can't make you understand what it was like. The nearest I can come is this: imagine every day of your life were a book on a shelf, and you could take any one of them down and open it at any page and the page would be there, exactly as it was, every word, every face, the light on it, the smell of the morning. And imagine that you'd never once taken down most of the books, because you hadn't known there was anything in them worth reading.

I took them down. All night. One after another.

I took down every morning I'd stood at the top of the ferry stairs, from the summer of '79 to that very morning. Every one. And I didn't look at the people waiting for the boat this time. I didn't look at the carters or the boatmen or the farmers with their baskets. I looked at the corner where the cart stood, and the woman behind it, and the basket on top of it, under its cloth.

* * *

I'll give you what I found. In the order I found it.

The first thing was her face. I'd never looked at it, in a year and a half. But Caesar's eyes had, every time, without me. It was there in every morning, on every page, waiting. A woman of perhaps thirty-five, or a little more, with a broad plain face, weathered, and a strong chin, and gray hair coming out from under a plain cap, and a little white scar through her left eyebrow. Gray cloak. Every morning, the same gray cloak.

The second thing was the eggs. Your mother wrote them down for me, Freda, every time I told her, and so did you, I suppose, when you typed it. In January, on the frozen stairs, with no boat running at all, eleven. They'd found some other way across the ice that week, I suppose. I never learned it. In March, a morning I'd been watching for the carter, eleven. In May, every single day, twelve. And on the twentieth of July, at dawn, with the carter going over in the first boat with his face gray and no cart, eleven.

Now I went through every morning. Every one. And it was the same. On the days I'd seen the carter come over with a parcel the shape of a quire, or go back in a hurry, or anything at all to do with their road, there were eleven eggs in her basket when the sun came up. One gone, before she'd sold a single one. On every other morning there were twelve. A full dozen. Every morning in May, when the road was shut, twelve. Every morning since October, when the man in the city stopped writing, twelve.

I'd counted them every morning for a year and a half. I couldn't help it. I counted everything. And I'd never once asked myself why a woman who sold eggs by the dozen started some mornings with one missing.

She'd been standing at the top of the only road out of the city, in plain sight, telling them. One egg gone: there's something to carry today. A full basket: there's nothing; stay home. Every carter and boatman and courier on that road had only to walk past her cart on his way to the boat, and look at her basket, and he'd know. And nobody had to say a word. Nobody had to carry a scrap of paper. Hill could search them all to the skin.

The cleanest thing I ever saw in that war. From either side.

* * *

And the third thing.

I went back. Back past all of it, back into the summer before, the summer I came in, to a morning in late August of '79 at Mr. Hill's new table on the Brooklyn landing. A morning I'd told your mother about, Freda, months ago, because I remember everything and can't leave anything out. A thin anxious farmer of about thirty, in a brown coat, coming off the boat from the city. And his wife on his arm, a little older than him. In a gray cloak and a plain cap. Scolding him the whole way up to the table about what he'd paid in the city for a pound of tea.

I took that page down and looked at her face.

A broad plain face, weathered. A strong chin. A little white scar through her left eyebrow.

She wasn't his wife. She'd walked a frightened man past Hill's table with his letters on him, in the first week Hill was there, when every man was searched, by scolding him so loudly about the price of tea that Hill had been glad to see the back of them both. Everybody in the line had laughed. I'd laughed.

That was her. The first time I ever saw her. And she'd been at the top of the ferry stairs every morning since, with her basket, for a year and a half, and I'd bought eggs from her twice a week, and never once looked up.

* * *

[now]

Nobody said anything.

The rain went on, soft and steady, on the roof and in the yard. The stove ticked. Freda was holding her cup in both hands and looking at him with her eyes very wide, and I realized I'd stopped writing a while ago, and that my pen was lying across the page on the word *eggs*.

"I said it," Freda said, in a small voice. "I said it was the egg lady. In the car. I said that's what you do with the eggs."

"You did," he said. "And your mother saw it in July. You were both a great deal quicker than I was." He smiled at her, a little. "I had every single piece of it in my head from the first week. Every page. And it took me a year and a half and an old man with one eye to make me open the book."

"Why didn't you see it?" Freda said. "If you saw everything?"

He thought about that for a long time.

"Because I was looking for a man," he said. "A gentleman. A man of business, with good paper and a good coat. That's who I told Beckwith I was looking for, in March, with my finger on his map. And I never once thought to look at a woman selling eggs. Not because I thought she couldn't. Because I didn't think of her at all." He looked down at his hands. "And I'd spent my whole life in that body being the man at the edge of the room nobody thought of at all. I should have known better than anybody alive. That's what I've never forgiven myself for, Freda. Not that I lost her. That I didn't see her, because she was standing exactly where I stood."

* * *

*December 1780*

I didn't sleep. At first light I was at the top of the ferry stairs.

The cart was there. The basket on top, under the cloth. The butter in its crock. The little iron pot of coals, with the coals just catching.

And a different woman behind it.

Younger. Stout, red-faced, cheerful, with her hands wrapped in rags against the cold, stamping her feet. I'd never seen her in my life. And Caesar's eyes were quite certain of that.

I asked her, as carelessly as I could, as a man who bought eggs there twice a week and was used to another face, where the widow was.

"Gone over to the Island," the young woman said. "Yesterday, on the noon boat. Her sister's poorly, out Setauket way. I'm minding the cart for her." She lifted the cloth off the basket. "Eggs? They're good. She'd want me to keep her people."

I looked down into the basket. I didn't need to count them. I counted them anyway.

Twelve.

"What's her name?" I said. "The widow."

The young woman looked at me oddly. A Black man asking a white woman's name, at the top of the ferry stairs, at dawn.

"Widow Ames," she said. "Everybody knows Widow Ames." She put the cloth back over the eggs. "You'd know her if you saw her. She's been here forever."

I bought two eggs. I don't know why. I stood at the top of the stairs with them warm in my hand, and watched the first boat of the morning push off for Brooklyn, with half a dozen people in it, none of whom was a woman in a gray cloak. She'd gone over the day before. On the noon boat. A few hours after an old one-eyed ferryman came out of the Provost and walked straight to her cart, and she'd looked up and seen me across the street.

One day.

If I'd seen it on the street, instead of in the night. If I'd gone to the cart, then and there, in the snow in the gutter, instead of home to my bed to take down my books. One day.

* * *

[now]

He stopped. He got up from the table, slowly, with one hand on its edge, and went to the counter, and took a pan down from the hook over the stove, and put it on the burner, and a lump of butter in it, and stood watching the butter melt.

"Did you go after her?" I said. "To Setauket?"

He didn't turn round.

"No," he said.

"Did you tell DeLancey? Her name? What she looked like?"

The butter began to foam. He picked up the brown bowl from the table, and took out four eggs, one at a time, and cracked them, one-handed, into a bowl, the way people do who've done it ten thousand times.

"Next week," he said. "That's next week. The last one. I'd like to make you both an omelet now."

He beat the eggs with a fork, briskly, and tipped them into the pan, and the kitchen filled with the smell of it, warm and plain and good, with the rain on the roof. And I looked at the bowl on the table.

Eight left. He'd taken four.

Freda was looking at it too. She looked up at me. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to.

He made the omelet, and folded it in the pan, and cut it in three, and put it on three plates, with toast, and sat down with us, and we ate it, the three of us, at the kitchen table, in the rain, and it was the best omelet I have ever had in my life, and nobody said one word about eggs for the rest of the afternoon.
