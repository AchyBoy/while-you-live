## Chapter 21: The Provost

Freda made a blueberry pie, the second time, and it fell apart when she cut it, and he said it was the best thing that had happened to him all week and had two pieces to prove it. She came up with me again. She hadn't asked if she could. She'd just been sitting in the car when I came out of the house on the Thursday morning, with the pie on her knees and her seat belt on, looking straight ahead, and I'd got in and started the engine and neither of us said anything about it.

He was at the bottom of the steps again, under the maple.

*Session thirty-nine. Thursday, July twenty-second.* It was cooler. A front had come through in the night with a long grumble of thunder that never quite turned into rain, and the air had a clean, washed edge to it, and there was a little wind in the maple. He sat on the bench in the cardigan, for the first time in weeks, and Freda sat on the top step with her back against the post, which was her place now, and he looked at the two of us and said, "October," and then, "This is the part where I win."

"After André?" Freda said.

"After André." He looked out at the field. "I've told you the truth about everything else. I'll tell you the truth about this. I don't think I've ever worked as hard as I did that autumn, or as well. And I've never been as unhappy doing it."

* * *

*October 1780*

Arnold gave them a list.

That was the first thing that happened, before André was even dead. Arnold came over to the King on the twenty-sixth of September, and on the twenty-eighth, while André was being moved to Tappan, men from the Provost Marshal's office went through the city with a list of names in Arnold's hand and took people out of their houses. Merchants, mostly. Gentlemen. A few tradesmen. People Arnold said, from what he'd learned as a rebel general, had been sending news to Washington. Some of them had, I expect. Most of them hadn't. A Hessian officer wrote in his diary that more of them were taken to the prison every day.

I watched them taken. I watched a man I knew by sight, a respectable old merchant from Hanover Square who'd once given me a penny for holding his horse, led down the Broadway between two soldiers in his nightcap, with his wife running after them in the street. They held him six weeks and let him go. I don't believe he'd ever written a word to anybody.

That's what a list from a man like Arnold is worth, in a war. He didn't know who Washington's people in New York were. Nobody had ever told him. He was a general. Generals aren't told. So he gave them every name he'd ever heard spoken in a rebel camp with any kind of look on the speaker's face, and they filled the Provost with them.

It frightened the city. I'll give it that. It frightened everybody. That's the trouble with a list like that. It frightens the innocent and the guilty exactly the same amount, and the innocent have nothing to hide, so they go on as before. The guilty were frightened already.

* * *

There was a new man in André's office by the middle of October. Oliver DeLancey, a New Yorker, a Loyalist, a cavalry officer, cousin to the DeLanceys whose refugee horse I'd ridden with in the Neutral Ground. He was made Adjutant General in André's place, and he took over André's secret business, with Beckwith beside him.

He sent for me his first week.

He wasn't André. Nobody was. He was a brisk, square, practical man with no imagination, and he didn't offer me a chair. But he'd read what Beckwith had written about me, and he'd read what André had written, and he looked at me across André's long table under the window, with André's drawing board still in the corner with nothing pinned to it, and he said, "The Major thought very highly of you."

"Yes, sir."

"So did he of a great many people. He was a generous man." He put his hands flat on the table. "I'm not. I want Washington's people in this city, Freeman. The real ones. Not Arnold's merchants. I want them in the Provost, and then I want them on the end of a rope, the way they put the Major on the end of one. Can you find them?"

I looked at him. And then I looked at the drawing board in the corner, for longer than I should have.

"I can make them stop, sir," I said. "I think that's better."

* * *

I want to tell you why I said that, because Freda's going to ask me if I don't.

It wasn't mercy. Not only. I'd spent a year in that city by then. I'd watched what the Provost did to people, and what the gallows did, and what a list did. And I'd watched André go up the river and come back folded over his servant's arm. And I'd found, sitting in a cedar wood above the White Plains road on the morning of the twenty-third of September, that I didn't want to put anybody else in a coat that would hang them. Not even the people I was hunting. Not even the man in the city.

And it was better. That was the other half of it, and it was true. A hanged spy tells you nothing. A frightened one stops, and goes on being frightened, and frightens the next one, and the one after. André had said that. I'd heard him. *A hanged spy is a wasted opportunity.* I wasn't going to waste his last idea on DeLancey.

DeLancey looked at me for a long time. Then he said, "Make them stop, then," in the voice of a man who thought I was being soft, and would hang me too if I failed, and I went out.

* * *

So I made them stop. I'll tell it quickly, because it's mostly small things, done well, one after another.

I watched the ferry. I'd never stopped watching it. And on Monday, the ninth of October, I went past the dry goods shop in Hanover Square that I'd watched in the spring, the one the carter had come out of with the quire of paper. And the shutters were up. In the middle of a Monday. And they stayed up. I went past every day for eighteen days, and the shutters stayed up, and on the twenty-seventh they came down again, and the young partner was back behind the counter, very pale. That day, I read long afterward in his own account book, he drew a great deal of money out of the business in cash.

I didn't go in. I didn't tell DeLancey. I knew what DeLancey would do with a shop that had shut its shutters for eighteen days, two weeks after Arnold came over. He'd have had the partner in the Provost by dinner, and the shop turned out, and nothing would have been found, because there'd be nothing to find. And then DeLancey would have had to decide what to do with a frightened young merchant with nothing against him but his shutters. And DeLancey didn't like waste.

I let it be. I'd frightened that shop once. It was frightened again. That was the round.

I've told you the man in the city wrote one more letter, after André died. His last for a long time. He said in it that he was glad Arnold didn't know his name. And then he stopped writing. For the whole winter. Their man in Setauket wrote to Tallmadge at the end of October that the man in the city had *resolved for the present to quit writing and retire into the country for a time*, and that the enemy were *very severe*, and that he himself *did not think myself safe there a moment*.

Very severe. That was me, Ms. Barnett. And Freda. That was me, and DeLancey, and the ghost of a young man in a scarlet coat.

* * *

The second thing was the boat.

The rebels had boats on the Sound besides Brewster's: whaleboats, with crews of Long Island men who'd gone over to Connecticut and came back raiding. One of them was commanded by a Captain Conklin. And there was a man in Setauket, a Loyalist, called Abijah Tooker, who knew a great deal about who came and went on that shore and liked to be paid for it. I'd found him in August, through Glover. I'd never met him. I'd sent him a little money now and then by a Loyalist sloop, and he sent back what he knew.

At the beginning of October, Tooker sent word of where Conklin's crew would be. Refugee boats went out and took them, near a place on that shore called Treadwell's Banks. A rebel lieutenant was killed. The rest were taken. And the man in Setauket wrote to Tallmadge that week, angry and frightened, that *their misfortune is owing to Abijah Tooker of Seatauket*.

He was right. It was. It was owing to Abijah Tooker, and to me.

* * *

And the third thing. The one I want to tell you properly.

There was a man who helped them. Not one of the ring. One of the people around it, who carried a thing here and there, who held a horse, who kept a letter in his house for a night. Every chain has people like that. They're never in the books. They're never in anybody's books. He was a ferryman on the Brooklyn ferry, an old man with one eye, who'd worked the sweeps on those flat boats for thirty years. I'd watched him all year. I'd watched him take a folded paper from the carter, once, in the spring, and put it inside his hat, and give it to somebody on the Brooklyn side. I'd never done anything about it. I'd been saving him.

In the first week of November, after the shutters, I gave him to DeLancey. They had to see one of their own taken and not hanged. Taken, so they'd stop. Not hanged, so I could live with it.

Not as a spy. I was very careful. As a man who'd been seen carrying letters for persons unknown. DeLancey had him taken up at the ferry stairs and put in the Provost. And they questioned him, and he said nothing. Nothing at all. He said he was an old man who rowed a boat, and he'd carried a great many things for a great many people in thirty years, and he couldn't remember any of them, and he was sorry. He said it for three weeks. They didn't hurt him. I'd asked DeLancey not to, and DeLancey had looked at me as if I were mad, and then, I think because André would have, agreed.

I went to see him in the Provost. Twice. I brought him a blanket, because it was cold, and bread, and once a little tobacco. He looked at me through the bars with his one eye and knew perfectly well who'd put him there, I think. And he took the blanket. And he said thank you. And he said nothing else, to me or to anybody, ever.

The man in Setauket wrote to Tallmadge in the middle of November. *Several of our dear friends were imprisoned,* he wrote. *In particular one that hath been ever serviceable to this correspondence. This step so dejected the spirits of C. Junr.,* he wrote, meaning the man in the city, *that he resolved to leave New York for a time.*

Ever serviceable. That was my ferryman. With one eye, and my blanket, and his mouth shut.

They let him out after Christmas. Nobody had ever been able to say what he'd done. He went back to the ferry and worked the sweeps till he died.

And that was the end of it. The road was shut. The man in the city had gone to the country. The man in Setauket was afraid to go to New York. The boats were watched, and some of them were taken. The ring that had written to Washington every two weeks all summer was silent, all that winter. Nobody was hanged. Nobody was ever going to be.

He stopped and drank some water. The wind moved in the maple. Freda was sitting very still on the top step.

"You won," she said.

"I won."

"But you don't look like you won."

He thought about it.

"There's a thing that happens," he said, "after a loss like André. You go on. You do the work. You do it better than you've ever done it, because the work's the only thing in front of you that you can get right. And you win, and you win, and you win, and it doesn't help at all." He looked at her. "I'm not telling you that so you'll be sad. I'm telling you because one day you'll lose something, and you'll go on, and win things, and wonder what's wrong with you that it doesn't help. Nothing's wrong with you. It just doesn't. Not for a while."

Freda looked at him for a long moment.

"How long's a while?" she said.

"In that case," he said, "about two hundred years." Then he smiled at her, properly, the first real smile all morning. "But I'm very slow."

* * *

He was right about Claire. I want to put that down here, out of order, because it happened six days later, and I don't want it to get lost in the rest.

It was the Wednesday. The twenty-eighth of July. I was at the kitchen table at a little after ten in the morning, writing three hundred words about a roofing company. Freda was asleep. The house was quiet. And my phone, face down on the table where it had been face down for four weeks, buzzed once, and then again.

A number I didn't know. A Virginia number.

I turned it over and looked at it for a long time, while it buzzed. Then I picked it up.

"Hello?"

There was nothing on the line for a moment. Just breathing, and behind it, very faint, a radio playing country music, and a horse blowing out through its nose, a long, soft, rattling breath.

"It was twenty-seven minutes," Claire Whitcombe said.

I looked at the clock on the stove. It said ten fourteen. On a Wednesday morning, in summer.

"I know," I said.
