## Chapter 14: Major André

"She didn't ask what you meant," he said.

It was the first thing he said. I'd told him all of it, on the porch, before I'd even said the date into the recorders, standing at the top of the steps with my bag still on my shoulder. The barn, the hoof testers, the towel, the *please*. He'd listened without moving. And then he said that, and I knew he'd seen it before I had, which is to say about a second after I'd said it.

"No."

"And there's been no letter."

"Two days. It's early."

"There won't be one." He moved the cardigan off my chair for me, though it was eighty-five degrees, and I sat. "She hasn't told him. She'll be deciding whether to, for a while. She'll decide not to. She'll decide it every night." He looked out at the field. The hay was all in now, baled and gone, and the field was short and pale and already greening at the roots. "And one night she'll decide something else. Not because of anything you say. Because you came to her barn and didn't say anything else."

"How long?"

"I don't know. Longer than you'd like." He smiled. "Say the date, Ms. Barnett. It's July. That's when he came back."

*Session thirty-six, Thursday, July first.*

*June 1780*

Clinton came home in the middle of June, with Charleston in his pocket.

You have to understand what that meant to the city. Charleston was the greatest victory the King's army ever had in that whole war. A rebel army of five thousand men, surrendered, with all its guns and its general, in the biggest city in the South. The bells rang for a day. The Loyalists drank the King's health in every tavern on Queen Street. Rivington printed the surrender terms on the front of the *Gazette* with a border round them like a wedding notice. For the first time since I'd come into the lines, the city felt like a place that thought it might win.

And Major André came home with the General, and the whole secret business of the army came home with him.

I'd seen him twice before, at a distance, as I've told you. Now Robinson took me to him.

He was too busy to see me for a month, with the General home and a war to restart. It was a morning at the end of July before Robinson took me to him, at headquarters on the Broadway, at the bottom of the island, a big house with a garden that went down toward the water. André had an office on the first floor at the back, looking out over the garden, with a long table under the window covered in papers and maps, and a smaller table to one side with a drawing board on it, and a half-finished pencil sketch pinned to the board of a man in a cocked hat asleep in a chair. I recognized the man. It was one of Clinton's aides. It was very good, and very unkind.

André was standing at the window with a letter when we came in. He turned round.

He was beautiful. I don't know how else to put it. Not in a soft way. He was thirty, slim, not tall, with dark hair tied back and very dark, very bright eyes, and a way of standing that made everybody else in a room look as if they'd been put together in a hurry. His coat was the best-cut coat I saw in that whole war. And he smiled the moment he saw Robinson, a real smile, delighted, and came across the room with his hand out, as if Robinson were the one person in New York he'd been hoping would walk through his door that morning.

He made everybody feel like that. I was going to tell you it was a trick. It wasn't. That was the extraordinary thing about him. He meant it every time.

"Colonel," he said. "And this is the famous Freeman."

* * *

He knew all about me. Beckwith had written it all down for him, the ice and the deserters and the paper and the road, and he'd read it on the ship coming home, he said, twice, the way he read everything. He'd been in Charleston for six months and come back to find that Beckwith had shut down Washington's best correspondence in his absence, and Beckwith had told him, with the scrupulous honesty that was the best thing about Beckwith, exactly whose idea it had been.

"I don't believe a word of it," André said cheerfully. "So I'm going to test you. You don't mind?"

"No, sir."

He went to the long table and took a paper off it, and held it up in front of my face, about a foot away, for perhaps two seconds, and took it away again.

It was a return. A list of figures. The strength of every British regiment in the southern army, as it had stood at the end of May, regiment by regiment, rank and file, sick and present, with the totals at the bottom.

"Well?" André said.

I gave it to him. All of it. Twenty-three regiments and corps, in the order they were written, with every figure in every column, and the totals. It took me about four minutes. A man who can't read can recognize numbers, Ms. Barnett. Lots of men who couldn't read could count, in those days, and knew their figures off a bill. I'd decided, on the way up the stairs, that numbers were safe.

André listened to the whole thing with the paper in his hand, following it down with his finger. When I'd finished he looked up at Robinson, and then at me, and his eyes were shining.

"That is the most remarkable thing I have ever seen," he said. And then, to Robinson, "He's got the sick wrong in the Seventy-first. Hasn't he? Two hundred and twelve?"

"Two hundred and twenty-one, sir," I said. "The clerk's made his ones like sevens."

André looked at the paper. He laughed out loud.

"So he has," he said. "So he has."

* * *

That would have been the end of it, if it had been anybody else. It was the end of it for Robinson. He went off to see Clinton about something, and left me there, to be told what André wanted done with me.

André wasn't finished.

He was a man who couldn't leave a puzzle alone. It was the thing that made him so good at what he did, and in the end it was the thing that killed him, though neither of us knew that yet. He walked round his drawing table a couple of times with his hands behind his back, looking at the floor. Then he went to a little writing desk in the corner, and picked up a sheet of paper that was covered in his own handwriting, crossed out and written over and crossed out again, and came back and held it up in front of me, the same way, a foot from my face, for two seconds.

And I read it. Of course I read it. I couldn't not. That's what reading is, once you can do it; you can't look at words and not read them, any more than you can hear your own name and not turn round.

It was a poem. Verses, rhymed, very bad on purpose, about a rebel general called Wayne who'd attacked a Loyalist blockhouse on the Jersey shore a few days before and been beaten off, and had consoled himself by stealing the local farmers' cattle. André was writing a mock epic about it, for Rivington to print. It was called *The Cow-Chace*. It was rather funny.

He took it away.

"What's the fourth line?" he said, quite gently.

I didn't answer for a long moment. And then I made the only mistake I made with him in all those months.

"*And all the cows in Jersey*," I said.

And then I heard myself, and stopped.

* * *

He didn't say anything at all for a while. He stood there with the poem in his hand and looked at me, and I looked at the floor, and I thought about the man outside the tavern on Queen Street, the free man born on Long Island, who'd come out of the Provost after six weeks with his papers gone. I thought that I'd spent a year in that city and been careful every single day, every hour, and that it had taken a man who couldn't leave a puzzle alone about two minutes.

Then André went and shut his office door. Quietly. And came back. And sat down on the edge of his long table, so that his face was lower than mine, which I don't think any white man had done in that body's whole life.

"Where did you learn?" he said.

"I don't know, sir."

He smiled. "That's not true. But I can see why you'd say it." He turned the poem over in his hands. "Does Robinson know?"

"I think he wonders, sir. I've never told him."

"And Beckwith?"

"No, sir."

"Good." He put the poem down on the table beside him. "Then we shan't tell either of them. They're both very fine officers, and neither of them could keep that sort of thing to himself for a week if his life depended on it. Robinson would tell his wife, and Beckwith would put it in a report." He looked at me. "And then it would go round the town, and some Loyalist with a grudge would decide that a reading Black man was a forger, and you'd be in the Provost, and I'd have to waste a great deal of my time getting you out. And I'm very busy." He paused. "So you'll go on not being able to read. And I'll go on not having noticed. And you'll be a great deal more use to me than either of them knows. Is that agreeable?"

"Yes, sir."

"Then we shall both have a secret, Freeman." He stood up, and picked up the poem, and looked at it, and made a face. "Was the fourth line any good?"

"No, sir," I said. "It was very bad."

He laughed so hard he had to sit down again.

He stopped. He was smiling, out at the field, the way you smile at something nobody else can see.

"That was the once," I said. "You told me someone found out. Once."

"That was the once." He turned the smile on me. "Nobody else, in that body. Not ever. He kept it to the end." He was quiet for a moment. "You'll want to know whether I liked him. I liked him more than anybody I met in that war. More than Prince, and I loved Prince. André was the only man in the British army who ever looked at me as if I might be interesting. Not useful. Interesting. And I've thought, since, that that's a very dangerous thing for a man in my position to be grateful for, and that I was grateful for it anyway, with my whole heart, and that he knew it, and used it." He picked up his glass. "Both of those are true. You don't get to pick."

* * *

*August 1780*

He used me, that summer, for the thing he cared about most. And I didn't know what it was for nearly two months.

He never told me. That was the rule of his correspondence, which he'd written down himself in a letter long before, though I didn't see that letter till much later: every messenger was to remain ignorant of what he carried. There was a man, on the other side, an American general, writing to André in secret. That was all I knew, that summer. Letters came in from him, by roundabout roads: under flags of truce with other business, with ladies' letters about bonnets and gloves, with exchanged prisoners coming back. The letters were in a cipher made of numbers, three numbers to a word. Page, line, word, out of some big book. I glimpsed one, once, on André's table, and kept it, and could have read it in a moment if I'd had the book. I didn't have the book. I didn't try to find it. I knew better.

What I did was the thing I was good at. I watched the roads the letters came by, and the men who touched them, and made sure nothing leaked.

Something did, once, in August. A clerk in the Adjutant General's office, a young Loyalist from Philadelphia with debts, who copied the outsides of letters, the covers and the seals and the names they were addressed to, and sold the copies to a man from Jersey who came into town on market days. He didn't know what was in them. He didn't need to. Somebody on the other side, putting those covers together, week by week, would have seen a pattern in where they went, and who they went to, and when.

I saw him do it. I saw him slip a folded paper to the man from Jersey at the Fly Market, between two baskets of peaches, one hot Saturday. I told André. André thanked me, and said nothing more about it. And the next week the clerk was posted to a regiment in Halifax, Nova Scotia, with a good character and no explanation, and the man from Jersey stopped coming to market.

Nobody was hanged for it. André wasn't a man for hanging people if he could help it. He said once, in my hearing, that a hanged spy was a wasted opportunity, and that he'd much rather have a frightened one. Beckwith had told him what I'd said, I suppose, about frightening Setauket. He'd liked it.

"The general," I said. "The American general writing to André."

"Yes."

"Arnold."

He looked at me for a long moment.

"You looked it up," he said.

"Everybody knows it. It's the one thing everybody knows about that whole war. Benedict Arnold."

"Everybody knows it now." He set his glass down. "That summer, in all New York, I'd guess a dozen people knew it. Clinton. André. Beckwith. Robinson. A clergyman called Odell, who decoded for them. Perhaps the General's secretary. I wasn't one of them. I carried his letters for two months and didn't know whose they were. I found out in the worst way it's possible to find anything out. You'll hear it. Not today."

"Did you like him? Arnold?"

"I never met him," he said. "Not until the end. And then I didn't." He looked out at the pale field, at the line of trees beyond it, blue in the heat. "But I knew what he was doing. Even before I knew who he was. A man selling his own people for money. I knew exactly what that was." He looked back at me. "I'd been sold, Ms. Barnett. In that body. Reynolds bought Caesar for forty pounds from a man in White Plains when he was nineteen. It's in Reynolds's account book, under the cow."

He picked up his glass again, and found it empty, and set it down.

"I worked very hard for André that summer," he said. "I'd do it again. But I never once, in all of it, stopped knowing what the letters were for."
