## Chapter 20: A Deserter Is Never Given Up

*September 1780*

Clinton wrote to Washington the day Arnold came in. He said Major André had gone up the river under a flag, at General Arnold's invitation, and so could not be held as a prisoner, still less as anything worse, and he asked for him back. Very correct. Very polite. Everybody at headquarters agreed it was a very good letter.

The town didn't know yet. Not for a day or two. The town knew only that General Arnold had come over to the King, and it was delighted. People stood outside headquarters to see him come and go. A printer called Gaine wrote in his journal that it was to the great surprise of everybody. And then, the next day, he wrote that Major André had been taken by General Washington, and that it was supposed he would be ill used.

Supposed he would be ill used. That's how the city found out.

* * *

I'll tell you the week the way I lived it. In pieces. That's how it came.

On the twenty-eighth, word came down the river, the way word does, by boatmen and deserters and market women, that the rebels had moved André. Across the Hudson, under a heavy guard, to a village called Tappan, on the Jersey side, near the border with New York, where Washington had his headquarters that week. They'd put him in a tavern, in an upstairs room, with two officers in the room with him day and night, and the door open.

On the thirtieth, in the morning, the news that there'd been a board. Fourteen of Washington's generals, at a table in a church in Tappan, with André in front of them, answering their questions. He'd answered them all, truthfully, the deserters said. He'd told them everything about himself and nothing about anybody else. He'd been so frank and so courteous that some of the generals hadn't been able to look at him. And at the end of the afternoon they'd found that he'd come behind their lines in disguise, under a false name, with papers, and that by the laws of war that made him a spy. And that he ought to suffer death.

I heard that in the yard at headquarters, from a groom, while I was holding a horse. I remember the horse. A bay mare, with a white sock behind. She stood on my foot, and I didn't notice until somebody pointed it out.

* * *

On the thirtieth, a Saturday, I went over to Paulus Hook.

It was an accident. I want to tell you that, because if I'd planned it I'd have been prouder of it, and I wasn't proud of anything that week. An officer of the garrison there needed a guide to take a party up into Bergen the next day after some cattle, and Beckwith sent me, because he wanted me out of the city and out of his sight, I think. I'd been going about headquarters for three days like a ghost.

That evening, a rebel captain came down to the post at Paulus Hook under a flag of truce, with a troop of dragoons and a packet of letters from Washington for Clinton. The commandant of the post asked him to supper, as officers did, under a flag, in that war. And because the commandant's servant had a fever, and I was there, and a Black man in a decent coat is a servant to anybody who needs one, I waited at the table.

I stood behind the commandant's chair with a bottle, and filled the glasses, and listened.

They talked about nothing, at first. The weather. The cattle in Bergen. The roads. And then, near the end, when the wine had gone round twice, the rebel captain leaned a little toward the commandant, and lowered his voice, and said something that he plainly hadn't been told to write down.

He said, as one of them remembered it long afterward, that if Sir Henry Clinton were to give up General Arnold to General Washington, Major André would very likely be set free.

* * *

I didn't drop the bottle. I've always been rather proud of that.

The commandant didn't answer. He looked at the rebel captain for a long moment, and then he got up from the table, and called for his horse, and went down to the boat, and was rowed across the river to the city in the dark to see the General himself, at that hour, with that in his pocket. The rebel captain sat on alone at the table with his wine. I filled his glass. He looked up at me once, a young, tired, decent face, and I think he was ashamed of what he'd been sent to say, and I think he hoped it would work, as much as I did.

I stood behind that empty chair for two hours.

I've thought, since, about what I'd have given, in those two hours. Everything. Arnold. Of course Arnold. I'd have carried him over to Tappan myself, on my back, tied in a sack, and laid him at Washington's feet. A man who'd sold his country for money, and talked the best man I knew into somebody else's coat to save his own skin, and then run. What was he worth? What was he worth to anybody?

The boat came back in two hours. The commandant came up the steps from the landing, slowly, and stood in the doorway, and the rebel captain stood up.

"Sir Henry's answer," the commandant said, "is that a deserter was never given up."

He stopped.

He wasn't looking at the field now. He was looking at Freda, on the top step, with her back against the post. She'd stopped crying some time back. She was very white.

"That's not fair," she said. "He could have. He could have just... swapped."

"He could have," he said. "And every man who'd ever come over to the King, in that war, on a promise, would have known that the King's promise was good only until it became inconvenient. Every Loyalist in New York. Every deserter. Every Black man who'd come in on a proclamation." He looked at her steadily. "Every one of us, Freda. Me. If Clinton gave up Arnold, then the next time it was convenient, he could give up any of us. To be hanged, or to be sold back to the men who'd owned us. And everybody would know it."

Freda stared at him.

"So he saved you," she said slowly. "By not saving André."

"That's what I've had to think about, ever since," he said. "Yes. A deserter was never given up. Arnold was a deserter. So was I."

* * *

*October 1780*

On the first of October, a schooner called the *Greyhound* went up the river under a flag, with three gentlemen from the city aboard: a general, and the lieutenant governor, and the Chief Justice. They were to plead for André. They'd been up half the night before, at a council at headquarters, deciding what to say, and they'd decided he wasn't a spy at all, and they'd written it all down very carefully.

The rebels let one of them ashore. General Greene came down to meet him at Dobbs Ferry. They talked for a long time, by the river. Greene listened, and was courteous, and said, at the end, that the decision had been made, and nothing could alter it. And then he hinted, the general wrote afterward, that André might be safe if Arnold were given up.

And our general, the record says, *answered with a Look*.

That's all. Answered with a look. I've read those three words in an old letter more times than I can count. I can see the look. I don't need anybody to describe it to me.

They hanged André the next day, at noon, on a hill outside Tappan. The second of October.

* * *

We didn't know for three days.

On the third, Gaine wrote in his journal that the general had come back down the river, and that Major André was not hanged. The whole city believed it, for a day. I believed it. I went down to the slip at the bottom of Queen Street, where I'd watched him go up the river in his scarlet coat, and stood there half the afternoon, watching every boat that came down, as if he'd be in one of them.

On the fifth, it came. The lieutenant governor wrote that an account had come to town that morning that Major André was executed at Tappan. And on the sixth and seventh, Gaine wrote that it was confirmed, and that there were great lamentations.

There were. I'll say that for the city that week, and for the army. They loved him. The General put out an order to the whole army mourning him as a gentleman of the highest integrity and honour. Robinson, I was told, sat on the edge of his bed in his lodgings with his face in his hands. Every officer in the army put a band of black crêpe round his arm.

* * *

They told it all over the city, afterward, what had happened at Tappan. The rebels had told it to the boatmen and the boatmen to everybody.

That he'd been courteous to everybody to the end. To his guards, who wept. To the officers who came to see him. That he'd drawn a picture of himself, the night before, with a pen, sitting at the table by the window, and given it to the officer of the guard. That he'd shaved, and put on his uniform, which his servant had been allowed to bring him from the city, and come out and walked to the hill between two officers, arm in arm, talking to them. That he'd asked Washington to let him be shot, like a soldier, and not hanged, like a thief, and Washington hadn't answered. That when he saw the gallows, he'd stopped for a moment, and then gone on. That he'd put the noose round his own neck, and tied the handkerchief over his own eyes. That when they asked if he had anything to say, he'd said only that he wanted them to bear witness that he met his fate like a brave man.

And that the American officers who stood round that hill, the officers of the army he'd come to destroy, had wept like children. Every one of them.

His servant came back to the city at the end of the month, under a flag, with his master's things. His uniform. His watch, which had been taken by the men who stopped him on the road, and which André's friends had bought back from them for forty guineas.

I saw the servant come up from the slip, carrying them. A thin young Englishman with red eyes, with the scarlet coat folded over his arm.

It was his own coat. It came home. He didn't.

[now]

Nobody said anything for a very long time.

The sun had gone round. The porch was in shade now, all of it, and the field had turned the color it turns in the late afternoon in July, a deep tired gold, with the shadows of the trees reaching right across it.

"I have his face," he said at last. Quietly. "From the night in his office, with the candle. Saying he'd come back in his own coat. I have it exactly. I'll have it as long as I have anything. And I find I don't know if I'm glad."

He stood up, with his hand on the rail.

"There's one more thing," he said. "And then I'll stop, and you can drive home." He looked at me. "The man in the city stopped writing, that October. After Arnold came over. I'll tell you how, next week. He was frightened, and he had every reason to be. But before he stopped, he wrote one more letter. And in it, he wrote this. I've read it so often I don't need to look at it." He closed his eyes. "*I never felt more sensibly for the death of a person whom I knew only by sight, and had heard converse, than I did for Major André.*"

He opened his eyes.

"That's the man I'd been hunting all year," he said. "The man whose road I'd shut, and whose carter I'd frightened, and whose boat I'd had shot up in a creek. And he sat in his room, in that city, and grieved for André, the same week I did." He was quiet for a moment. "That was a war, Ms. Barnett. Freda. That's what a war is. We were on opposite sides of it, he and I, and we were the only two men in that city who'd have understood each other completely, and he never knew I existed, and I never knew his name."

* * *

Freda typed it on the Sunday.

I offered to do it myself. She wouldn't let me. She took the laptop into her room and shut the door, and I heard her through it all afternoon, stopping and starting the tape, and once, about four o'clock, I heard her crying, quite hard, for a minute or two, and then the typing again. I didn't go in. She wouldn't have wanted me to.

At six she came out and put the laptop on the kitchen table and said, "It's done," and went and stood at the back door looking out at the yard for a while.

"He was thirty," she said, without turning round. "That's not that much older than Dad was when he left."

I didn't say anything.

"He's been dead nearly two hundred and forty-seven years," Freda said. "Why does it feel like somebody died this week?"

"Because somebody told you about him who loved him," I said. "That's how it works. That's all it takes."

She thought about that, looking out at the yard. Then she turned round, and wiped her face with both hands, and said, "I'm going to make Mr. Hale another pie," and went to the cupboard to see if we had any flour.
