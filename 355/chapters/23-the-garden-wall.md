## Chapter 23: The Garden Wall

He was at the bottom of the steps when we drove up. Under the maple, with his hands in his pockets, watching the car come up the gravel road.

And I understood, all at once, with my hands on the wheel, what he'd meant. *It's a good thing to see, a car coming up a road. You'll understand later.* He'd been waiting at the bottom of those steps every Thursday since July for a car to come up a road. Not ours. Somebody else's. A dusty white pickup with a vet's box on the back, turning into a side street in a valley town, with a man in a garage looking up from his workbench.

He'd known. Freda was right. He hadn't known the town, or the garage, or the grilled cheese. But he'd known there'd be a car, and a road, and somebody looking up. And he'd stood at the bottom of his own steps for a month so that when it came, I'd remember he'd been waiting for it.

I stopped the car. Freda got out first. She didn't say anything. She went straight across the gravel and stood in front of him, and he took his hands out of his pockets, and she put her arms round him, which she'd never done, and he stood very still for a moment, and then put one hand on the back of her head, the way you do with a child, though she was taller than he was.

"He's alive," she said into his shoulder. "He makes grilled cheese. He's got a cat with white feet."

"Has he," he said. "Has he, now."

* * *

I told it all on the porch. Every word, from the phone call to the drive home over the mountains in the dark. He didn't interrupt once. Freda sat on the top step against the post and corrected me twice, about the soup, and about the color of the cat. When I got to the receipt, and what I'd said in the driveway, he closed his eyes. When I got to Pearl, and the pay phone in Front Royal, and the family with two little girls, he took off his glasses, which he almost never wore, and cleaned them on his shirt for a long time.

At the end he sat with his hands folded on his knee and looked out at the field.

"You won," he said.

"We won."

"No." He turned his head and looked at me. "You won. I sat on a porch and talked about ice. You drove to a barn with nothing in your hands and asked one question and went away, and waited a month without knowing, and didn't tell anybody, and didn't give up." He looked back at the field. "Six years, Ms. Barnett. You've held it six years by yourself. And you got there."

I didn't trust myself to answer. I said the date into the recorders instead. *Session forty. Thursday, August fifth.* My voice wasn't very steady, and I've left it on the tape.

"And now," he said, "since it's a day for it, I'll tell you about the best thing I did that whole year. For the worst man I ever met."

* * *

*October 1780*

General Arnold was in New York, and Washington wanted him back.

Not to forgive. To hang. Washington had been more hurt by Arnold than by anything else in that war, I think. He'd trusted Arnold above almost anybody, and given him West Point, and Arnold had sold it, and run, and left André to hang in his place. Washington couldn't have André back. But he thought he could have Arnold. In a sack, if necessary. Across the river. Alive.

I didn't know any of that, at the time. Nobody in New York did. I learned it afterward, like the rest, from the letters. Washington wrote to one of his cavalry officers, a young major called Henry Lee, on the twentieth of October, that he agreed to the plan, and that the essential point was that Arnold be *brought to me alive*. He wanted to make a public example of him.

And that same night, a sergeant of Lee's dragoons, a big, steady Virginian called Champe, deserted.

* * *

I saw him come in.

I was at Paulus Hook again, by chance, the morning of the twenty-first. There were two British galleys lying off the Jersey shore, and in the gray light we saw a man come pelting down the beach at Bergen with a troop of rebel dragoons galloping behind him, shouting, close enough that we heard them, and he ran straight into the water in his boots and swam for the nearest galley, with a valise strapped on his back, and the sailors pulled him out by his collar just as the dragoons reached the shore and pulled up their horses at the edge of the surf and shook their fists.

Everybody cheered. The sailors, the garrison, me. It was the best desertion anybody had seen all war. A sergeant-major of the famous Lee's Legion, chased to the very water's edge by his own comrades, and saved by a boat's length.

They took him to the city that afternoon. He was questioned by the Adjutant General's office, as every deserter was, and then by Sir Henry Clinton himself, because a sergeant-major of Lee's was worth asking. He answered everything readily and well. He passed all the forms of the garrison. And a few days later he enlisted in the new corps that General Arnold was raising, of Americans who'd come over to the King, and was made a sergeant in it, and went about the town in his new coat.

I watched him do all of it. Because I watched everybody. And because something about that desertion had stayed with me, the way things did, that morning on the beach.

He'd run for his life, by his own account, with his whole regiment behind him, from the middle of his own camp, in the dark.

And he'd found the time to pack a valise. With his orderly book in it.

"He was a spy," Freda said. "Wasn't he. The sergeant."

"He was the bravest man I saw that whole war," he said. "On either side. He deserted his own army, with his own friends chasing him, who didn't know, some of them, and would have shot him if they'd caught him. They'd told only the major. He swam the river in October with his valise on his back, and walked into the enemy's city, and stood in front of the enemy's commander-in-chief and lied to his face for an hour, and joined the traitor's own regiment, to get near enough to him to take him." He shook his head. "If I'd been caught doing half that, they'd have hanged me from the nearest tree. He did all of it, and they made him a sergeant."

"And you knew."

"I didn't know. I wondered." He held up one finger, the way he does. "That's not the same, and I'm going to be honest with you about the difference. I wondered, because of the valise. A man can be chased so hard by his own side that he has to swim for it, and get away by a boat's length, and it's very lucky. Or it's very well arranged. I'd arranged things myself, that year. I knew what arranged looked like."

"Did you tell anybody?"

"No," he said.

* * *

*December 1780*

Arnold lived on the Broadway that winter, near the bottom, next door to the General's own headquarters, in a big brick house with a garden behind it that went down toward the river, and an alley along one side of the garden, with a fence of tall wooden palings between the alley and the garden. His wife came to him from Philadelphia in November, with their baby. He kept an orderly, and two servants, and he walked in his garden every night before he went to bed, alone, for a quarter of an hour, with his hands behind his back and his bad leg dragging a little. Everybody knew it. It was a thing people said about him, the way people said things about Arnold, that he walked in his garden at midnight like a man with something on his conscience.

I knew the alley. It went down to the river between the backs of the houses, and at the bottom of it, among the slips, boats tied up at night: oyster boats, wood boats, small craft from the Jersey side. I'd walked it a hundred times. I'd stood in it holding horses.

And on a gray afternoon in early December, walking down it, I saw that somebody had been at the fence.

* * *

I'd never have seen it myself. I want to be honest about that, too. It was Prince who saw it, really. He was with me that afternoon. We'd been hired to carry a load of planks down to a boat at the bottom of the alley, and he was ahead of me with his end, and he stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into him.

"Look at that," he said. "Somebody's drawn those nails."

I looked. It was a stretch of the paling fence along Arnold's garden, five or six boards, just where the alley was darkest, under the overhang of a stable roof. To me it looked like any other stretch of fence. To Prince, who'd built more fences than I'd walked past, it looked like a door. The nails at the top and bottom of each paling had been drawn, and then pushed back into their holes, so the heads showed and the boards stood straight. But they weren't holding anything. You could have taken those palings out with your fingers, one by one, in the dark, without a sound, and stepped through into Arnold's garden, and put them back.

"That's careful work," Prince said, with professional respect. "Somebody knew what they were about."

I stood in the alley and looked at those palings, and then at the bottom of the alley, at the boats tied up among the slips. And I thought of a sergeant who'd swum the river with a packed valise on his back. And of a man who walked in his garden at midnight, alone, every night, for a quarter of an hour. And of February, and five rockets going up out of the dark country to the west, and Beckwith's finger on his map going back and back to Morristown.

I'd planned this. That's what I thought, standing there. I'd sat in a cold office ten months before and planned exactly this, for the other side. Take a general out of his own headquarters at night, in the middle of his own army, and carry him off across the water before anybody knew he was gone.

They'd only done it better.

* * *

I went to DeLancey that evening. I didn't tell him about the sergeant. I told him about the fence.

I told him that somebody had drawn the nails in the palings along General Arnold's garden, at the darkest place in the alley, and pushed them back so nobody would see, and that the alley went down to the boats, and that the General walked in that garden alone every night at midnight, and that if I were Washington, and I wanted him alive, that was where I'd take him.

DeLancey looked at me for a long time. He didn't like me. He never did. But he wasn't a fool.

"Who drew the nails?" he said.

"I don't know, sir."

That was true. I didn't. I didn't know it was the sergeant. I'd wondered. That's all. And a man can be hanged on a wonder, in that city, as easily as on a fact. I'd seen it done.

"I'll have the alley watched," DeLancey said.

"If you watch the alley, sir, they'll see you watching, and wait. And try again somewhere else, some other night, and you won't know where." I'd thought it through on the way. "Don't watch the alley. Move the General. Tomorrow. Quietly. To other quarters, without saying why. Have the fence mended. Let whoever drew those nails come to that garden on their night and find it empty, and the fence nailed shut, and the General gone, and nobody waiting for them, and nothing to show that anybody ever knew." I paused. "They'll think it was chance. They'll think the General just moved. And they'll be frightened for the rest of the war that it wasn't."

DeLancey said nothing at all for a moment.

"The Major would have liked that," he said finally, in a strange voice.

"Yes, sir," I said. "I think he would."

* * *

General Arnold changed his quarters the next day. Nobody told him why, I believe, or if they did, they told him something else. The fence was mended by a carpenter from the Engineers, who complained about the state of it.

And a night or two later, the story goes, a sergeant of Arnold's Legion and a man from Newark came down that alley in the dark to the place where the palings were loose, with a boat waiting at the bottom among the slips and a gag and a rope in their coats, and found the fence nailed shut, and the garden dark, and the house empty.

Nobody was waiting for them. Nobody took them. They went away. The sergeant sailed with Arnold's Legion for Virginia a week or two later, on the twentieth of December, with the rest of the corps, in his British coat. And a long time after that, in Virginia, he walked away one night and found his way back to his own side, and to his major, and was given his discharge, so he'd never be taken and hanged as a deserter. I've always been glad of that. I'd like to have shaken his hand.

Arnold lived to be an old man too. In London, rich and despised, with nobody on either side of the ocean who'd sit at a table with him if they could help it. He died in his bed, twenty-one years later. They say he asked to be buried in his old American uniform. I don't know if that's true. I'd like it to be. It would be the only thing about him I ever understood.

He stopped. Freda was looking at him from the top step with an expression I couldn't read.

"Why?" she said. "You hated him. You said he was the worst man you ever met. You could have just... not said anything. Let them take him. Nobody would ever have known."

He thought about it, honestly, the way he thinks about everything she asks him. He'd always been honest with Freda. More honest, sometimes, than he was with me.

"Because of what Clinton said," he said. "At Paulus Hook. *A deserter was never given up.* I'd stood behind that chair for two hours, Freda, and hated that sentence more than anything I'd ever heard. And then I'd understood it. If they could take Arnold, out of his own garden, in the middle of the King's own city, then they could take anybody who'd come over. Any deserter. Any Loyalist. Any man who'd run from his master on a proclamation." He looked at her. "Arnold was a traitor and a coward and he got a better man killed. I'd have watched them hang him with pleasure. But he'd come in on the same promise I had. If the promise didn't hold for him, it didn't hold for me. Or for Prince. Or for any of us."

"So you saved him for you," Freda said.

"I saved him for all of us," he said. "And for André, I think. Because André would have." He picked up his glass. "And I did it without giving them the sergeant. So nobody hanged. On either side. That's the part I'm proud of." He drank. "The best work I did that whole year, Freda. For the worst man I ever met. That's how it goes, sometimes. You don't get to pick who you're good for."

Freda thought about that for a long time, with her chin on her knees.

"That's really annoying," she said at last.

He laughed so hard he had to hold on to the rail.

* * *

When he'd stopped laughing, and wiped his eyes, he looked at the two of us. At me in my chair, with my notebook. At Freda on the step.

"Next week," he said, "is December. The end of the year." He folded his hands on his knee. "The eggs."

Freda went very still.

"I'm going to tell you how I found her," he said. "The way I found her. In the order I found her in. And I'd like you both to be here." He looked out at the field. "I've never told anybody. In all this time. I'd like to tell it to the two of you."
