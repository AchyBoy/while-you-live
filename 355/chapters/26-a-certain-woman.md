## Chapter 26: A Certain Woman

On the Thursday, I brought him the paper.

Not a copy off the internet. The real thing, the Sunday paper, the whole heavy fat front section of it, folded under my arm. I'd bought six at the gas station on the Sunday morning, at five, when the bundles came off the truck, and the man behind the counter had looked at me as if I'd lost my mind, and I'd told him I had a story in it, and he'd said, "Well, congratulations," and given me a free coffee.

He was at the bottom of the steps. Of course he was. Freda didn't wait for me. She was out of the car before I'd turned off the engine, with a copy of her own, the one she'd kept back from Mrs. Dunleavy's, and she held it out to him across the gravel with both hands, the way you'd hand somebody a baby.

He took it. He didn't open it. He held it, and looked at the front page, above the fold, at the headline and the two names under it, for a very long time. Then he looked at me.

"They spelled his right?" he said.

"Three times," I said. "I checked."

"Of course you did," he said, and folded the paper carefully under his arm, the way I'd been carrying mine, and went up the steps with it, one hand on the rail.

* * *

He read the whole thing on the porch, every word, slowly, with his glasses on, while Freda and I sat and watched him and the recorders sat on the little table, not running yet. It took him half an hour. When he'd finished he turned to page two, and read the box, the eleven lines, twice. Then he took his glasses off and folded the paper and put it on the bench beside him, on top of the cardigan, and laid his hand flat on it.

"Eleven," he said.

"I know."

He looked at me, and then he laughed, and it was the laugh that made him hold the rail. Freda started laughing too, without knowing why, and then I did, and the three of us sat on that porch in the August heat laughing at the number eleven until Freda had to put her head down on her knees.

"Mrs. Dunleavy says you've got a good ear for stairs," Freda said, when she could.

He wiped his eyes.

"Tell her," he said, "that I learned it from her."

* * *

I said the date into the recorders. *Session forty-three. Thursday, August nineteenth.* And then, because I'd promised myself, before we started, I told him about the money.

The deposit on the Monday, with the wrong account on it. *Trustees of the Halloran Fund, established 1938, Hartford.* The single scanned page from the 1950s. *The support of historical inquiry and the preservation of testimony.* Eighty-nine years.

He'd been looking at the field while I talked. When I said *1938*, he didn't move. Nothing in his face moved at all. I'd learned what that meant, that year. I'd watched it at the Carpathia card, and at Damon's letter. It's what his face does when it's decided to do nothing.

"1938," he said, after a while. Quietly. To the field.

"Does it mean something?"

"It was a good year to start waiting," he said. "If you knew what was coming." He turned and looked at me, and his face had come back. "Write it down, Ms. Barnett. You were right to. It's true, and it's the first true thing, and he didn't mean you to have it." He picked up his glass of water. "Which means somebody in a bank in Hartford is having a very bad week. Leave it there for now. It'll keep. It's kept eighty-nine years."

And then he wouldn't say anything else about it. I didn't ask twice. I'd learned that, too.

* * *

"And I found it," I said. "The line. The three Black men."

He went very still.

"In the guide to the Clinton Papers," I said. "Volume eighty-seven. The last days of February and the first of March, 1780. *British deserter caught and turned in by three African Americans.* That's how the archivist wrote it. It took me four hours."

I opened my notebook to the last page, the one I keep, the one with nothing crossed out. And I read it to him. All three entries. Ned Hurly in the Point Lookout register, from the winter. Lowell Hatch in the *Tribune*, from the spring. And the new one.

When I'd finished, he didn't say anything for a long time. He was looking at the field, and his hands were folded on his knee, and they weren't quite steady.

"Isaac would be pleased," he said at last. "He'd want to know if they'd spelled him right." He smiled, a little. "They didn't spell any of us. That's all right. That's how it was. We knew who we were."

* * *

"You said next week," I said. "Last week. You said this would be the last one."

"I did."

"Did you tell DeLancey? About her?"

He didn't answer straight away. He looked at Freda, on the top step, and she looked back at him, and something passed between them that I wasn't part of, and didn't mind not being part of.

"I'll tell you exactly what I told him," he said. "And then I'll tell you why. And then you can decide what you think, the way you always do."

He folded his hands.

"Yes," he said.

* * *

*January 1781*

DeLancey sent for me at the beginning of January, as the year turned, the way a man calls in his accounts.

He wanted to know what I'd found. All of it. The whole year. He'd had my reports, in bits, as they came, through Beckwith and Robinson and André, and now André was dead and he wanted it all in one place, laid out on André's long table, so he could see it. So I gave it to him. The paper, and the ink that answered only to its own key. The carter, without his face. The road along the north shore, fifty-five miles. Setauket. The boat, and the man who came over in it, whose name everybody knew. The officer in Connecticut who took the letters at the other end. The man in the city, a gentleman of business, without his name, because I didn't know it, and without his shop, because I did.

And then DeLancey asked the question I'd known he'd ask. Because he wasn't a fool.

"And in Setauket?" he said. "Where does the boat take them up? From whom?"

I'd lain awake three nights over that question, Ms. Barnett. Freda. In the shed by the North River, with my eyes shut and her face on every page.

"At or near a certain woman's, sir," I said.

He looked up.

"Which woman?"

"I don't know, sir," I said.

I had to give him something, or he'd have sent a man who'd look harder than I had. So I gave him Setauket, which he had already, and a woman, and there are a great many women in Setauket. No face. No cart. I sent him looking fifty-five miles from the ferry stairs.

* * *

[now]

"You lied," Freda said.

"I lied," he said. "To his face. Across André's table. The only time in that whole two years I ever lied to any of them about anything that mattered." He looked at her. "And I'd do it again. I'd do it every time."

"Why?"

He was quiet for a long time. A long time. The bees went in the clover. Somewhere down the gravel road a car went by, slowly, and the dust drifted across the field, and settled.

"Because I knew what they'd do," he said finally. "DeLancey would have had her taken on the Island, and brought to the city, and put in the Provost, and asked. And she wouldn't have told them anything, any more than my ferryman did. And then he'd have had to decide what to do with her. And he'd have done what they did with André." He turned his cup on the table, a quarter turn. "I'd have put her in a coat that hanged her. I'd promised myself, on the White Plains road, that I'd never do that to anybody again. Not even them. Not even her."

"That's not all," I said.

He looked at me.

"No," he said. "That's not all. You're right." He looked back at the field. "She'd beaten me, Ms. Barnett. Fairly. Every morning for a year and a half, at the top of the only road, in front of everybody, in plain sight, with a basket of eggs. She'd beaten the best I had, and I'd had the best eyes that ever lived, and she'd done it by standing where I stood. At the edge of the room. Where nobody looks." He was quiet. "If I'd given them her face, I'd have taught them to look there. At the edge. At the woman with the cart and the man holding the horse and the carpenter mending the fence. They'd never once looked at us, that whole war. That was the only safety any of us had." He looked at Freda. "If I'd taught them to look at her, the next people they looked at would have been Prince. And Isaac. And me."

Freda had her arms round her knees again.

"So you let her go," she said. "For you. Again."

"For all of us," he said. "Again." And then, gently, "And for her. Because she was the best I ever saw. And I didn't want to be the man who caught her." He picked up his glass and found it empty and set it down. "I've never once been sorry. Not once, in all the years since. I've been sorry about nearly everything else."

* * *

"The line," I said. "In my notebook. From the research I did, for the Facts. There's a British agent's letter, from February of 1781, to DeLancey. It says the letters come *by the way of Setauket, where a certain Brewster receives them at, or near, a certain woman's.*"

He smiled. Slowly.

"That was a man in Connecticut," he said. "A double agent of sorts, called Heron, who sold the British news and sold the rebels news and was never quite sure himself which side he was on. DeLancey sent him what I'd said, and asked him to find out more, from the other side. And Heron wrote back in February and told him exactly what I'd told him, in my own words, as if he'd found it out himself." He shook his head. "A certain woman's. I recognized it the moment DeLancey read it to me. That was the closest the British ever came, in the whole war, to the woman with the eggs. Two words. Mine."

"And nobody ever found her."

"Nobody ever found her," he said. "I looked, myself. Afterward. In every record there is. There's no Widow Ames. Not in New York, not in Setauket, not anywhere. She was never caught, and never named, and never written down, except once as a number in a letter, and once as two words of mine in somebody else's." He looked at me. "Three hundred and fifty-five. Lady. That's all anybody will ever know about her. That's all she ever wanted anybody to know."

* * *

He told the rest quickly. He said it was his story, not the war's, and that the war's ending was in every book.

The war went on another year. In October of 1781, at Yorktown, in Virginia, Cornwallis surrendered his whole army to Washington and the French, and that was the end, though nobody in New York would believe it for a long time. The British held the city for two more years while the diplomats talked in Paris. And in the treaty they signed, there was a line that said the British would leave without *carrying away any Negroes or other property of the American inhabitants*.

"Property," he said. "That was us. In the treaty. Between the cattle and the furniture."

In May of 1783, Washington came to meet the new British commander, Sir Guy Carleton, at a village near the Hudson, to arrange the handing over of the city. And to ask for his property back. Including the people who had run from Mount Vernon, his own household, who were in New York.

"It was Tappan," he said. "Orangetown, the letters call it, after the township it sits in. The same village. I've always thought that was something. The same village where they hanged André, three years before." He looked at the field. "Carleton said no. He said the men and women who'd come in on the King's proclamations were free before the treaty was ever signed, and that handing them back would be, I'll give you his words, *a dishonourable violation of the public faith*. He said the King's promise would be kept. And he wrote down all our names in a book, every one of us, with our ages and where we'd come from and who'd owned us, so that if Britain was ever found to be wrong, the owners could be paid for us instead."

"The Book of Negroes," I said. I'd read about it, for the Facts.

"Three thousand names," he said. "Mine's in it. Not Caesar Freeman. The clerk wrote something else, and I let him, by then. I won't tell you what. You'd find it in an afternoon, and I'd like one thing in that whole life left that you didn't."

And each of them was given a certificate, he said, signed on behalf of the new Commandant of the city, General Birch. The same Birch who'd led the cavalry over the crusted snow toward Morristown, that February, and sent up the five rockets, and turned round. *This is to certify,* the certificates said, *that the Bearer hereof, a Negro, resorted to the British Lines, in consequence of the Proclamations of Sir William Howe, and Sir Henry Clinton.* And that he had permission to go to Nova Scotia, or wherever else he might think proper.

"Any occupation which he shall think proper," he said. "Wherever else he may think proper. They never could bring themselves to say the word. But they kept the promise." He rubbed his thumb along the folded edge of the newspaper beside him. "I've never decided how I feel about that either."

* * *

"Where did you go?" Freda said.

"Nova Scotia," he said. "In the summer of '83. On a ship full of people with certificates in their pockets. With Prince, and Isaac, and Isaac's hat." He smiled. "It was colder than the Hard Winter. The land they gave us was rock and spruce and fog, and they gave it late, and less of it than they'd promised, and some of us never got any at all. We built a town there and named it after Birch, because his name was on our papers. Prince built half of it. He finally got to build something."

"And then?"

"And then, nine years later, a man called Thomas Peters, who'd been a sergeant in the Black Pioneers, went to London and talked his way into the government and came back with ships. And twelve hundred of us went with him, in fifteen ships, out of Halifax harbor in January, across the whole Atlantic, to Africa. To Sierra Leone. To build a town of our own, where nobody owned anybody." He looked at the field. "We called it Freetown. Caesar Freeman went to Freetown. I thought that was rather good, at the time."

"Is that where you..."

"The second year," he said. "Of fever. A great many of us did. I was forty-three, and free, and I'd built my own house with Prince, with a door that shut." He looked down at his hands. "And Caesar's eyes went with him. Everything I saw after that, in the lives since, I've had to remember the ordinary way. Badly. With the edges worn off." He looked at Freda, and then at me. "Except the two of you. I'll remember the two of you the ordinary way. And I find that I don't mind at all."

* * *

The card came on the Wednesday after. The twenty-fifth.

By courier, at a quarter to nine at night, the same young man in the same reflective jacket. "Evening, Ms. Barnett." The tablet for my finger. The envelope. "Have a good one."

Freda was at the kitchen table, with her school supplies spread out in front of her, labeling folders, because school started on the Monday and she'd decided, this year, to be organized, which I didn't expect to last. She looked up when I came back with the envelope. She put the label maker down.

One card. Heavy cream paper.

I could barely read it. The hand was worse. Much worse. The letters were very large, as if somebody had been holding the pen close to the paper and close to his eyes, and they leaned and wandered and came apart, and in one place the pen had gone right through the card.

It took me a long time.

*Please ask him about the war. The second one. All of it.*

Freda read it upside down. Then she looked at me.

"All of it," she said.

* * *

I read it to him on the porch, on the Thursday. The last Thursday of August. The light was already different, the way it gets at the end of summer, lower and more golden, with the long shadows coming early across the field. I said the date into the recorders before anything else, because I'd promised myself I would, this time. *Session forty-four. Thursday, August twenty-sixth.*

I gave him the card first, before the words, the way I always do now. He held it in the sun for a long time and looked at the hand. He touched the place where the pen had gone through.

"He's very near the end," he said quietly. "He's asking for all of it because he knows there isn't time to ask for it a piece at a time." He looked up at me. "He's right. There isn't."

Then he read the words.

I watched his face. Eleven seconds, the first card. Forty, at the Carpathia. A slow smile, at New York. This time it was none of those. This time his face did something I'd never seen it do before, in a whole year of watching it. It got older. All at once, in the sun, in front of me. As if every one of the years he'd lived had come and sat down on him together.

"All of it," he said.

"Were you there?" I said. "In the war. The second one."

He laughed. It wasn't much of a laugh. It was mostly breath.

"Several times," he said.

I didn't understand. And then I did, and I felt it go down my arms.

"Several..."

"It was a long war, Ms. Barnett. Six years. Seventy million people died in it. Some of them were me." He set the card down on the bench beside him, face up, very gently, the way he'd set down the New York card in the spring. "And not always on the same side."

Freda, on the top step, had gone completely still.

He looked out at the field. The swallows were going over it low, in long curves, the way they do at the end of August, feeding before they leave.

"I'd like to ask you for two small things," he said, still watching them. "And I'd like you not to ask me why. Not today."

"All right."

"Don't ever change your email. Or your telephone number. People do, these days, every few years, for no reason at all. Don't."

I almost laughed. It was such an old man's thing to ask. "I've had the same number for eighteen years."

"Keep it for eighteen more." He turned his head and looked at me, and he wasn't smiling. "And the second thing. If you ever get a letter, or one of those emails, from somebody you don't know, from anywhere, however young they sound, and it starts with the number of a session, read it to the end."

"Which session?"

"The last one we had," he said. "Whichever that turns out to be. You'll know it. You write them all down."

Freda had turned round on the step and was looking at him. He didn't look at her. He looked at me until I nodded. Then he looked back out at the field, and the swallows, and the long gold light going across it toward the trees.

"Bring a bigger notebook," he said.
