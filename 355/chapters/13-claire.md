## Chapter 13: Claire

I went on the Tuesday, at the end of June, in the morning, because he'd said people were braver at their work, and vets work mornings.

It was an hour and a quarter west of my kitchen, out past Leesburg, where the suburbs give up and the country starts all at once, between one traffic light and the next. Rolling fields, white board fences, stone walls, big trees standing alone in pastures with horses in their shade. The kind of country where the mailboxes have names on them instead of numbers. The road to the practice was gravel for the last half mile. The sign at the gate had a jumping horse on it, the same horse as the patch on the fleece, and under it, *Catoctin Equine, C. Whitcombe, DVM*, and under that, smaller, *Emergencies call anytime*.

I sat in the car in the gravel lot for ten minutes with the engine off.

I'd decided, driving out, what I'd say, and I'd decided it again at every traffic light, and every time it came out differently. In the end I'd decided on the thing he'd told me. Not a reporter. A woman with one question. I'd left my notebook in the car. I'd left both recorders in the car. I had my phone in my pocket, switched off, and nothing in my hands at all, which was the strangest part. I couldn't remember the last time I'd gone to ask somebody a question with nothing in my hands.

* * *

The barn was long and low, with green trim and a wide center aisle open at both ends so the air could go through, and it smelled the way barns smell, of hay and horse and something medicinal and clean. A radio somewhere was playing country music very quietly. A young woman in breeches came out of an office by the door, with a coffee mug, and asked if she could help me, and I said I was hoping to see Dr. Whitcombe, for a few minutes, about something personal.

"She's with a horse," the young woman said. "She'll be a while. You're welcome to wait." She looked at my shoes, which were the wrong shoes. "There's a bench."

There was a bench, by the office door, and I sat on it, and waited.

Halfway down the aisle, in a stall with the door open, there was a big brown horse standing very still, with his head low, and a woman crouched at his front feet. She had her back to me. I could see the gray coming into short dark hair, and a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbows, and her hands on the horse's leg, running down it, slowly, from the knee to the hoof, the way you'd run your hands down a banister in the dark, feeling for a splinter. She had a tool beside her on the straw, a big steel thing like a pair of tongs, and every now and then she picked it up and pressed it, very gently, against a part of the horse's hoof, and watched his face. When he flinched, she said something to him, too low to hear. When he didn't, she moved the tool a little and tried again.

She didn't hurry. Not once, the whole time. The horse knew it. You could see him know it. He stood for her like a statue.

I watched her for twenty minutes. I knew already, watching her hands on that horse's leg, that I was going to get this wrong.

* * *

When she came out of the stall, she was wiping her hands on a towel, and she saw me on the bench and came down the aisle, unhurried, and the young woman with the coffee said, "Claire, this lady's been waiting to see you," and went back into the office.

"Hi," Claire Whitcombe said. "What can I do for you?"

She was taller than she'd looked in the video. Her face was her mother's, from the obituary photograph, the same wide mouth, but not laughing. A plain, tired, intelligent face, that had been up since five and expected to be up till nine, and wasn't going to waste any of the time in between. She had a smear of something on her cheek. She didn't know it was there.

"My name is Iola Barnett," I said.

I'd thought about that, too, in the car. Whether to give her my name. Whether she'd know it. Six years ago my name had been on the front page of a newspaper above a story about her father's employer, and then on a correction underneath it, and then on a great many angry blogs. People who'd never heard of Carrow House knew my name for about a month. People who had, didn't forget it.

She knew it. I watched it arrive. It didn't arrive in her face. It arrived in her hands, which had been folding the towel, and stopped.

"I know who you are," she said.

"I thought you might."

"What do you want?"

And I could feel it, the way you feel a tide turn under a boat. Everything I'd planned in the car, about being a woman with one question, and being gentle, and going slowly. I could feel her waiting for me to say it, so that she could say no to it and be done. She was already halfway through saying no. She'd been ready for this for six years.

So I said the question. The only one. The one he'd told me to ask, because nobody had ever asked it.

"How long did you wait?"

* * *

I'll tell you exactly what happened, because it's the only part I got right.

She didn't say anything. She didn't move. She stood in the barn aisle with the towel in her hands and the country music playing very quietly somewhere behind her, and she looked at me, and the color went out of her face from the top down, slowly, like water going out of a basin. I've seen that happen to people perhaps three times in my life. The last time had been my editor, the morning the correction ran.

The horse in the stall behind her shifted his weight and blew out through his nose, a long, soft, rattling breath, and she turned her head toward the sound, the way you'd turn toward a child who'd called you, and I saw her see that he was all right. And then she turned back to me, and her face had closed.

"I'd like you to leave," she said.

"Dr. Whitcombe..."

"I don't know what you're talking about." Her voice was very even. "I don't know what you think you know, or who's been telling you things, but I'm working, and I have a mare to see in Hamilton in forty minutes, and I'd like you to leave my barn. Now, please."

"I'm not going to write anything," I said. "I don't write anything anymore. I write about gutters. I just want..."

"Now," she said. "Please."

It was the *please* that did it. It wasn't rude. It was the opposite. It was a woman holding on to the last thing she had, which was her manners, with both hands.

I took a card out of my pocket. A plain one, with my name and my phone number and nothing else. I'd had them made the week before, for this, because my old cards had the paper's name on them. I held it out. She didn't take it. So I put it down on the bench where I'd been sitting, and stood up, and said, "I'm sorry for coming to your work," and meant it, and walked out of the barn into the sun.

She didn't call after me. I didn't look back. I got into the car and sat there, and through the open end of the barn I could see her, still standing in the aisle, exactly where I'd left her, with the towel in her hands. She stood there all the time it took me to start the car and turn it round in the gravel. When I pulled out of the gate, she was still there.

* * *

I drove a mile and then pulled into the gravel at the side of the road, under a big oak in a pasture with three horses standing in its shade, and turned the engine off, and got my notebook out of the glove box, and wrote it all down. Every word she'd said. The towel. The *please*. The color going out of her face from the top down. The horse breathing, and her turning to it. I wrote for half an hour, the way I used to in the car after an interview, before I forgot anything. When I'd finished, I read it back.

She hadn't asked what I meant.

That was the thing I saw, reading it back. I'd come into her barn, a stranger, a woman she knew only from a newspaper six years ago, and asked her how long she'd waited. Not waited for what. Not waited where. And she hadn't asked. She'd said *I don't know what you're talking about*, which is what everybody says. But she hadn't asked me what I was talking about. She hadn't needed to.

And I understood, finally, sitting under the oak, what he'd meant the week before. *Let her have one more week of not knowing anybody's looking. You'll need her to have had it.*

She'd had six years of thinking it was over. Of driving her father to his talks, and putting her hand under his elbow, and parking in loading zones, and not thinking about one Wednesday morning in an alley in Washington more than she could help, at night, every night. And then one more week. One more ordinary week, of horses and mornings and coffee in the office, while a woman she'd never met sat at a kitchen table an hour away, waiting.

I'd taken that week away from her this morning in a barn aisle with five words. And she would know, now, for the rest of her life, the exact difference between the week before and the week after. Every time she heard an engine idle.

He'd wanted her to have it, so that she'd know what she'd lost.

I sat under the oak for a long time after that. The three horses in the pasture came over, slowly, one at a time, and put their heads over the fence by the car, and looked at me through the window, and I looked back at them, and none of us said anything.

* * *

Freda was waiting at the kitchen table when I got home, with the laptop shut and her hands flat on it, which meant she'd been waiting a while.

"Well?"

"She asked me to leave."

"Oh." Freda's face fell. "That's... okay. That's bad."

"It's not bad," I said. I put my bag down. "She didn't ask what I meant."

Freda looked at me. I watched her think about it. It took her about four seconds.

"Oh," she said again, differently.

"Yes."

"So it's her."

"It's her. She was in the car." I sat down across from her. "And she's not going to tell me anything. Not now. Maybe not ever." I put my notebook on the table between us. "And by tonight, or tomorrow, she's going to have told her father that I came. And he's going to call Carrow House, and Carrow House is going to call their lawyers. And in about a week I'm going to get a letter."

Freda put her hand on top of the notebook.

"Maybe she won't tell him," she said.

"Why wouldn't she?"

"Because she didn't ask what you meant," Freda said slowly, working it out as she went. "Because she's been not telling anybody anything for six years. Not even him, maybe. People who don't tell anybody anything don't suddenly start." She looked at me. "I didn't tell you about Dad for seven months. And I didn't tell Dad I hadn't told you. I just didn't tell anybody anything, about any of it. It's easier." She took her hand off the notebook. "It's like a habit."

I looked at my daughter for a long time across the kitchen table.

"We'll see," I said. "In a week."

* * *

We saw.

I checked the mail every day that week, and my email every hour, and my phone, which I left switched on, on the kitchen table, face up, all day and all night. I didn't get a letter from Carrow House's lawyers. I didn't get a call. Nobody wrote anything about me on any blog. Nobody came to my door.

Nothing happened at all. And by the end of the week, I'd understood that nothing happening was the loudest thing that had happened to me all summer.
