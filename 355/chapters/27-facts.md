## Extra 27: Facts

The British took New York in September 1776 and held it for seven years, until November 25, 1783. Days after they marched in, a fire burned something like a quarter of the town, Trinity Church with it. People lived in the cellars of the burned houses under old sails. They called it Canvas Town.

On June 7, 1779, the British commandant of New York, Major General Daniel Jones, ordered that "All Negroes that fly from the Enemy's Country are Free." Three weeks later Sir Henry Clinton's Philipsburg Proclamation promised any runaway from a rebel master "full Security to follow within these Lines, any Occupation which he shall think proper." It never uses the word free.

The winter of 1779 to 1780 was the hardest anyone on that coast had recorded. New York harbor froze. Men, sleighs, cavalry and, by one account, the heaviest cannon crossed the ice to Staten Island and New Jersey. The city needed about six hundred cords of firewood a week and at times had seventy. The Chief Justice wrote that "We often hear of the Deaths of the Poor, frozen in their Houses."

On the night of January 14, 1780, some three thousand Americans crossed the ice in sleighs to attack Staten Island. Deserters had warned the British commander, Brigadier Thomas Stirling, and his men were inside their forts. The Americans spent two freezing nights in the open, and about five hundred were frostbitten. On February 10, three hundred British cavalry set out across New Jersey to seize George Washington at Morristown. A crust of frozen sleet cut the horses' legs, and after five or six miles their commander, Lieutenant Colonel Samuel Birch, sent up five rockets and turned back.

The William L. Clements Library's guide to the Clinton Papers describes an item from the end of February 1780 as a "British deserter caught and turned in by three African Americans." The words are the catalog's. No names are given.

Washington's spies in New York, the Culper Ring, wrote in an invisible "sympathetic stain" made by Dr. James Jay, which showed only when brushed with a second liquid. They hid their letters among blank sheets of writing paper and used a codebook of numbers. 711 was Washington. 727 was New York. 355 was the word "lady." It appears once, in a letter of August 15, 1779, from Abraham Woodhull of Setauket: "by the assistance of a 355 of my acquaintance, shall be able to out wit them all." The day before, the Royal Gazette had announced that John Hill, inspector at the Brooklyn ferry, would examine everyone crossing. The lady in that line has never been conclusively identified. Some historians have suggested Anna Strong, a Setauket neighbor of Woodhull's; others think the evidence too thin to name anyone.

The ring's man in the city was Robert Townsend, a merchant. In April 1780 he refused to keep writing, and on May 19 Washington wrote that "the intercourse may be dropped." It started again in July, when the French landed at Newport. On July 20, 1780, Woodhull, sick in bed, sent the city's news to the boat with a note: "let not an hour pass: for this day must not be lost." It was not the first warning to reach Newport. Townsend's part in the ring was proved only in 1930, when the historian Morton Pennypacker had his handwriting matched to the letters.

Benedict Arnold, commanding West Point, wrote to the British for more than a year in a cipher of page, line and word, and asked twenty thousand pounds for the fort. Major John André, Clinton's adjutant general, went up the Hudson on the sloop Vulture to meet him on the night of September 21, 1780. At dawn American guns drove the Vulture downriver. Arnold sent André back by land, in a civilian coat, with papers in his stockings. On the morning of September 23, near Tarrytown, three militiamen stopped him. He told them he was a British officer.

The last canto of André's comic poem "The Cow-Chace," about General Wayne's failed attack on a Loyalist blockhouse, was printed in New York on September 23, the day he was taken.

On September 30, an American officer at a supper at Paulus Hook hinted that André would be freed if Arnold were given up. Clinton's answer, as it was remembered, was that "a deserter was never given up." André was hanged at Tappan at noon on October 2, 1780. He asked to be shot. He tied the handkerchief over his own eyes and asked those present to bear witness that he met his fate like a brave man. In New York every British officer wore crêpe. Two weeks later Townsend wrote: "I never felt more sensibly for the death of a person whom I knew only by sight, and had heard converse, than I did for Major André."

On October 20, 1780, Washington approved a plan to kidnap Arnold from New York and bring him back "alive." Sergeant Major John Champe of Lee's Legion deserted that night, swam to a British galley and enlisted in Arnold's own regiment. The plan, according to his commander's memoir, failed because Arnold changed his quarters the day before. Champe sailed with Arnold for Virginia in December and slipped back to his own side there.

In May 1783, at Orangetown, the village where André was hanged, Washington met Sir Guy Carleton to arrange the British departure. Carleton refused to return the Black men and women who had come in under the proclamations, calling it "a dishonourable violation of the public faith." He recorded about three thousand of them in a ledger now called the Book of Negroes, which also lists people still enslaved to departing Loyalists. A joint board heard claims from American owners, and a few people were handed back. Most of those who had come in under the proclamations were given a certificate "By Order of Brigadier General Birch," the same Birch who had sent up the rockets.

Most of them went to Nova Scotia, where they founded Birchtown. In January 1792 about twelve hundred sailed from Halifax in fifteen ships for Sierra Leone, led in part by Thomas Peters, once a sergeant in the Black Pioneers, and founded Freetown.

Britain passed the act ending slavery in its empire in 1833. The United States ended it in 1865.
