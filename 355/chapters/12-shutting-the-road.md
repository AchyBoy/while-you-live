## Chapter 12: Shutting the Road

The week he'd told me to wait was the longest week of the summer.

I didn't go to Purcellville. I didn't drive past the practice, which I'd found on the map, a long low barn with green trim on a road outside town, with a gravel lot and a sign with a jumping horse on it. I didn't call. I didn't look at the horse rescue's website more than twice a day. I wrote nine hundred words about a dentist's new chair and a thousand about seamless gutters, and I took Freda to buy a dress for her friend's sister's wedding, and I didn't sleep very well.

On the Thursday it was ninety-one degrees by eleven, and the porch was in full sun, and he'd moved us to the kitchen with the fan going and the shades half down. I said the date into the recorders, *session thirty-five, Thursday, June twenty-fourth*, and he said, before I could ask, "Next week. Go next week," and I let out a breath I hadn't known I was holding.

"Now," he said. "Frightening Setauket."

* * *

*April 1780*

It's easy to frighten people in a war. Anybody can do it. You burn a barn, you hang a man at a crossroads, you send a troop of horse galloping through a village at midnight. People are frightened. And then they get used to it, the way people get used to anything, and in a month they're doing exactly what they were doing before, only angrier.

That wasn't the kind of frightened I meant.

I wanted them frightened the way you're frightened when you realize somebody's been reading your letters. Not by noise. By quiet. By the sense, slowly, at every step of the road, that somebody had been there first, and was being very polite about it.

So we did small things. Beckwith did most of them. He had the authority and the men, and he was cleverer at this than he looked, once he understood what I meant. I just told him where.

* * *

The first thing was the paper.

An officer from the Commandant's office, a polite young lieutenant with a writing board and a cold, went round the shops in the city that sold good writing paper. All of them. Stationers, printers, the dry goods men who kept a few quires among the cloth. He told each of them the same thing, pleasantly. That because of the war, and the shortage of paper, and certain irregularities that had come to the notice of headquarters, the Commandant would be grateful to know, from now on, the name of anybody who bought writing paper by the quire or the ream to carry out of the city. Purely for the record. Nothing more. He wrote the shopkeeper's name on his writing board, thanked him, and left.

That was all. He didn't search anything. He didn't threaten anybody. He went to fourteen shops in two days, and in every one of them, he left behind a man who now knew that the army had begun to wonder about paper.

In one of them, I knew, he'd left behind a man who'd gone very white indeed after the door closed. I didn't know which one. I've worked it out since.

* * *

The second thing was the road.

Fifty-five miles from the ferry to Setauket, along the north shore of the Island, through Jamaica and Flushing and Hempstead, through a dozen villages, past a hundred farms. Most of that country was quartered with the King's troops that spring, one regiment or another, Loyalists mostly, in winter quarters. And the men who rode that road with anything to hide knew every post on it. Which taverns the officers drank in. Which bridges had a guard. Where you could ride through a village at dusk and nobody would look up.

Beckwith changed it. Not much. A sentry where there hadn't been one, at a bridge near Huntington. A picket moved half a mile down the road from where everybody knew it was. A troop of the Seventeenth Dragoons riding out along the north shore on an exercise, through Setauket and out the other side, and back again three days later, for no reason anybody could see. A deserter's story, let slip on purpose in a Jamaica tavern by one of Beckwith's men, that headquarters had a list of names on the north shore. There was no list. There didn't need to be.

And a rumor. That was the best of them, and half of it was mine. It had started on its own, in the winter. A quartermaster's clerk on the north shore had been asked to look into the forage at Setauket, and how many cords of wood the village could furnish, and he'd asked about it, loudly, at every farm, and by the middle of March the farmers were telling each other that two whole regiments were to be put into winter quarters in Setauket itself, next season. Two regiments, in a village of a few hundred people. Every house with soldiers in the kitchen. Every barn with an officer's horse in it. Every cove on the harbor with a sentry. It wasn't decided. Nobody had decided it. All I did was make sure it didn't die. A word in a Jamaica tavern. A question from a dragoon at a Setauket well. The same clerk, sent back in April to count the barns again.

I read the letter, a long time afterward, that their man in Setauket wrote in the middle of March, when the rumor was still new. Two regiments, he told Tallmadge, were to be stationed there. And then: *If it should take place it will I fear entirely ruin our correspondence.*

I can't tell you how I felt when I read that. Two hundred years later, in a library, in a reading room, with my gloves on. I hadn't started that fear. But I'd sat in a cold office with my finger on a map and made sure it lasted all spring.

* * *

The third thing was the carter.

I never gave him to anybody. I told you that. But I didn't leave him alone either.

On a morning in April I was at the Brooklyn landing, on the far side of the ferry, by Hill's table, when his wagon came off the boat from the city with his good gray horse in the shafts. Hill's men turned his load over, as they did everybody's: sugar, a cask of rum, a bolt of blue cloth, the usual. No paper, that morning. And while they did it, I went and stood at the gray horse's head.

I didn't say anything to the carter. I didn't look at him. I looked at the horse. I put my hand on its neck and stroked it, slowly, the way Caesar had stroked horses all his life, and the horse leaned into my hand the way horses always leaned into Caesar's. And I said, to the horse, not loudly, "Good horse. You go to Setauket a great deal, don't you? Every week or two. Good long road, that."

Then I took my hand away and went back to Hill's table and stood there with my arms folded, watching the boat go back across the river.

The carter didn't move for a long moment. Then he got up on his wagon and drove off up the Brooklyn road, not fast, not slow, whistling, like any man on any morning. But I'd watched his hands on the reins while I stroked his horse, and they'd gone white at the knuckles. And he wasn't whistling a tune. He was whistling one note, over and over, the way a man does when he's forgotten what tune he meant to whistle.

He came back over the ferry eleven days later. No paper. And the next time, three weeks after that, no paper. And the next.

* * *

The paper stopped in the middle of April.

I didn't know why. I didn't know anything then except what I could see from the ferry: that the carter still came, with his rum and his sugar and his nutmeg, but never again that spring with a parcel the shape of a quire. That the rumors about Setauket went round the north shore and came back to us bigger. That the dragoons rode out and rode back. That the polite lieutenant's writing board had fourteen names on it and not one purchase of paper by the quire.

And that the widow at the top of the ferry stairs on the city side had a full dozen eggs in her two rows every morning I passed that May. Every one of them. I noticed it, as I noticed everything. I remember thinking her business must be very bad, and buying an extra two, once or twice, because I felt sorry for her.

That's what I could see. Here is what I couldn't, which I learned a long time afterward, reading their own letters, in that library, with my gloves on.

The man in the city stopped writing. Their man in Setauket went into New York in the middle of April, as he'd planned, to see him, and found him frightened, and begged him to go on, and got nothing but what he called *his utter denial*. In May he wrote to Tallmadge that the man in the city *declines serving any longer*. He'd still talk, if somebody came, he said. But he wouldn't put another word on paper.

And on the nineteenth of May, at his headquarters in Jersey, George Washington sat down and wrote to Tallmadge that since the man in the city had *totally declined*, and the man in Setauket *seems to wish to do it*, he thought *the intercourse may be dropped*.

Dropped. Washington's own word. His best spies in New York, his only real eyes inside the city, dropped. For two months, from the middle of April to the middle of July, nothing came out of New York to Washington by that road. Not a word. In the spring of 1780, while Clinton was taking Charleston and the war was going worse for the rebels than it ever had or would again.

Nobody was hanged. Nobody was arrested. Nobody was searched to the skin or held to a flame. Nobody on the other side ever knew who'd done it. A polite lieutenant had gone round some shops with a writing board, and a troop of horse had ridden through a village and back, and a clerk had asked about firewood, and a Black man had stroked a horse at a ferry landing. And Washington's best spies had stopped writing to him.

That was the best work I did in that war, Ms. Barnett. Better than the ice. Better than any of it. I've never been prouder of anything than of the things in that war that nobody ever knew happened.

[now]

He stopped, and got up, and went to the window, and lifted the shade an inch with one finger and looked out at the glare.

I didn't say anything for a while. The fan ticked round on the counter. I had written *the intercourse may be dropped* in my notebook and drawn a box round it, and I sat looking at the box.

"Washington got other news," I said finally. "Didn't he? From somewhere."

"He did." He let the shade fall. "He opened another road, through Staten Island, with other people. It was slower, and it was worse, and it didn't see what the man in the city saw." He came back to the table. "I'm not going to tell you I won the war that spring. I won a road. That's all. For two months." He sat down. "But I won it without a single person in that whole chain coming to harm. That's rare enough, in any life, that I count them."

"And the eggs," I said.

He looked at me.

"Twelve," I said. "Every time you passed, in May. You said she always had eleven, the morning in January, and the morning in March, and now in May it was twelve. A full dozen." I tapped my notebook, where I'd written it, each time, because he'd told me to. "You told me to write down eleven. You said I'd want it later."

He was quiet for a long time. The fan went round.

"Did I," he said at last. And then, very gently, "Keep writing them down, Ms. Barnett. Every time I tell you. That's all I'll say."

I kept looking at him. He kept looking back. And then he smiled, and got up to make more tea, and wouldn't say another word about eggs for the rest of the afternoon, though I gave him four separate chances.
