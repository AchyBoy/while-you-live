## Chapter 5: The Hard Winter

I brought the coat.

It was eighty-eight degrees the next Thursday, the last one in May, and I drove up with the air conditioning on full and the green wool coat folded on the back seat like a passenger. When I came up the porch steps I had it over my arm. He was on the bench in the shade with a glass of water, and he looked at the coat, and then at me, and then he laughed, properly, the laugh that made him hold the rail.

"I was told to," I said.

"You were." He wiped his eyes. "Put it on the chair. It can listen."

I put it over the back of the other chair, and set the recorders on the little table, and said the date into them, *session thirty-one, Thursday, May twenty-seventh*, and he looked at the coat for a while, hanging there in the heat, as if it really were going to listen.

"Good," he said. "Now. Cold."

* * *

*January 1780*

You won't believe me about the cold. Nobody does, now. You've never had a winter like it, and nobody alive has. It was the worst winter anyone on this coast had ever written down.

It began before Christmas, with the ice in the slips. By the third week of December the floes in the North River were so thick the boats from the Jersey shore couldn't get through them, and the farmers who brought wood and food across stopped coming. Then, in the first days of January, the snow came. It snowed for three days. In the hills of Jersey, where Washington's army was in its huts, it lay four and five and six feet deep, and drifted over the huts so the men had to dig their way out in the morning. In the city it was less, but it didn't matter. It didn't melt. It didn't melt for two months.

And then the rivers froze.

Not the edges. All of it. The North River, which is a mile wide at the city and has the whole tide of the ocean pushing in and out of it twice a day, froze from shore to shore, solid. The bay froze. The water between the city and Staten Island, which is five miles of open harbor, froze hard enough to walk on, and then hard enough to ride on, and then hard enough to drive a sleigh full of firewood across, and then, about the middle of January, hard enough to drag the army's heaviest cannon across it, which nobody in the history of the place had ever seen or imagined.

New York was an island. That's all it had been for three years, a fortress on an island, safe behind its water, with the King's navy all round it. In the second week of January it stopped being an island. You could walk to it from New Jersey. You could walk to it from anywhere.

* * *

The city ran out of wood.

That's the thing I remember most, of that winter, and I remember all of it. Not the snow. Not the ice, though the ice was a wonder. The wood. A town of thirty thousand people needs a great deal of firewood to get through a winter, and in an ordinary year it came in by water, from Long Island and Jersey and up the river, every day, boatload after boatload. That year nothing came. The water was stone.

They burned everything. The army cut down every tree left standing on the island, the old orchards and the shade trees on the Bowery lanes, the trees in the gentlemen's gardens with the gentlemen watching from their windows. They pulled down empty houses and burned them. They bought up old ships and broken hulks lying in the slips and broke them up for firewood, and the bits of old ships burned blue and green in the grates from the copper and the salt. The soldiers' fuel was cut, and cut again. Gentlemen burned their furniture. I saw a man on Queen Street burning a harpsichord.

The officers at headquarters said the city needed six hundred cords of wood a week. I know because I heard Beckwith say it, more than once, in the way a man repeats a number that's frightening him. Some weeks there were seventy cords in all the army's yards.

And in Canvas Town, under the sails, over the burnt cellars, people froze to death.

* * *

I'm going to tell you about one of them, because I have to tell somebody. I'll be short.

There was a family in a cellar on the west side of Broadway, near where Trinity had been. A man and his wife and three children, sailors' people, from somewhere in the West Country of England. I passed them most days, that winter, on my way down to the river. The man had been pressed off a merchant ship and then dismissed from the navy sick, and he had no work and no way home. They'd made a sort of room down in their cellar, with a canvas roof and an old door for a wall, and a little fire in the corner of what had been somebody's kitchen hearth. The youngest child was about four. A girl, with very fair hair and a red woolen scarf that went round her neck three times. She used to sit at the top of the cellar steps in the mornings when it was sunny, with the scarf up to her eyes, and watch the people go by.

On the eleventh of January there was no smoke coming out of the hole in their canvas. I noticed it on my way down to the river. On my way back, at dusk, there was still none, and two men from the Barrack Master's office were carrying them up the cellar steps, one at a time, wrapped in their own blankets.

The red scarf was round the smallest one. They'd left it on her.

That's all. It happened in a hundred cellars that month. The Chief Justice of the province wrote in his diary, that winter, that they often heard of the deaths of the poor, frozen in their houses. He was a decent man, and I believe it upset him, and he was writing in a warm room.

I've told you about Caesar's eyes, how anything they fell on stayed. I've told you it was a gift. This is the other side of it. I have that cellar, Ms. Barnett. I have the steps, and the old door, and the color of the scarf, and the way the second man held the child, under the knees and the shoulders, as if she might still mind being carried badly. I've had it for two hundred and forty-seven years. I lost Caesar's eyes when I lost him, in the end, and everything I saw after that I've remembered the way anyone remembers, which is to say badly, with the edges worn off. But the things I saw with his eyes I still have the way he saw them. As if I were holding the page.

I wouldn't give them back. I want you to understand that. Even the cellar. Somebody ought to keep it.

He stopped. Across the field, at the far edge, the line of trees had gone blue in the heat haze, and a tractor was turning the hay in long slow rows, very small and far away, and the sound of it came to us a little after we saw it move.

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't have been for my sake rather than his. He drank some of his water. The green coat hung over the chair beside me, in the heat, absurd and patient.

"All right," he said, after a while. "That's that one."

* * *

*January 1780*

Beckwith sent for me in the second week of January.

I'd never spoken to him. I knew him by sight, as I knew everybody: a young man, younger than André, quick and thin and pale, with a sharp nose and sharp eyes, the kind of officer who reads every paper put in front of him twice and trusts none of them. With André and the General gone south, the whole secret business of the army in New York had landed on his desk. The agents. The letters. The deserters coming in over the ice, more every day, with stories, some true. And the rebels, sitting out there in the Jersey hills four and five miles off, looking at a city that wasn't an island anymore.

He saw me in a cold little office at the back of the Commandant's house, with his greatcoat on and a candle on the table and his breath showing. He had a paper in front of him, Robinson's paper about me. He didn't ask me to sit down either. But he looked up at me when I came in, straight up, the way Robinson had looked at me the second time, and not the first.

"Colonel Robinson says you see things," he said.

"I try to, sir."

"He says you notice what people carry, and where they go, and who they talk to, and that you never forget a face." He tapped the paper. "He says that's why the rebels' letters stopped coming over the Brooklyn ferry in the summer. Because you noticed them."

"They didn't stop, sir. They went another way."

He looked at me a moment longer. Then he smiled, which surprised me. It was a thin, quick smile, and it went as fast as it came.

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly what I'd have said. Good." He leaned back. "Here is my difficulty. You may have noticed that the harbor has frozen."

"Yes, sir."

"We've been sitting behind our water for three years. Now we have no water. Washington has an army in the hills at Morristown, thirty miles off, starving and freezing and very angry, and every day the ice gets thicker, and every night I lie awake thinking about what an angry general does when somebody builds him a road." He put both hands flat on the table. "I have deserters coming in who tell me he's coming. I have agents who tell me he isn't. I have Loyalists on Staten Island who see rebels behind every snowdrift. What I don't have is anybody who can stand on that ice in the dark and tell me what's really out there."

"The ice talks, sir," I said.

He raised his eyebrows.

"At night," I said. "When it's very cold and there's no wind. A sound will carry across it for miles. You can hear a dog bark on the Jersey shore from the middle of the bay, as if it were on the next street. You can hear men talking at Elizabethtown Point from the Staten Island side, if they're careless. You can hear sleighs." I paused. "Caesar learned it on the ponds up in Westchester, as a boy. Sent out to cut ice. Any man who's worked on ice knows it. The officers don't, because they've never had to."

He was very still.

"Sleighs," he said.

"If anybody's coming across from Jersey to you, sir, they'll come by sleigh. There's nothing else that'll carry men and guns over that much ice at night. And you can hear a sleigh on the ice, on a still night, a long way off. The runners sing. And the horses' bells, if they're fools enough to leave them on."

Beckwith picked up his pen and wrote something on Robinson's paper, very small, in the margin. I read it, of course, upside down. *Staten Island. Nights.*

"You'll go over tomorrow," he said. "There's a sleigh going across with the wood party at first light. Report to General Stirling's people at the watering place. Not the American Stirling," he added, dryly. "Ours. We have one each."

* * *

I want to tell you one more thing about that week, and then we'll go out on the ice.

The ferry to Brooklyn wasn't running. The East River was jammed with ice, great broken slabs of it heaved up against each other the full width of the water, grinding and groaning with every tide, too rough to walk across and too thick for any boat. But people still came down to the ferry stairs every morning out of habit, the way you go and look at a shop you know is shut. And the carts still came. There was nowhere better to sell to a crowd of people with nothing to do.

I went down there the morning before I went across to Staten Island, to look at the ice on the East River before I trusted myself to the bay. It was very early, and so cold that the air hurt the inside of your nose. The sun was just up, red, over the Brooklyn shore. The ice in the river was pink with it.

The widow was at the top of the stairs with her cart. She had a little iron pot of coals on a tray underneath it, to keep the butter from freezing hard, and she'd wrapped her hands in strips of old blanket so that only the tips of her fingers showed. She was looking at the ice too. And she was humming, very quietly, to herself, under her breath, the way people do on a cold morning to keep themselves company.

I couldn't hear what. The ice was too loud. Every time the tide moved, the whole river creaked and cracked and boomed, like a great house settling. I caught a turn of something, under it. Up, and a little turn, and down. And then a slab of ice somewhere out in the stream gave way with a noise like a cannon, and everybody on the stairs jumped, and when I looked back, she had stopped, and was rubbing her hands over the little pot of coals, and the tune was gone.

I bought two eggs from her. I remember them still, the weight of them, warm from the coals, in my hand. I put them inside my shirt for later. She didn't look at me any more than I looked at her.

I've wondered since what she was humming. I've wondered it for two hundred and forty-seven years.

He stopped there and looked over at the coat on the chair, and so did I.

"Why does that matter?" I said. "What she was humming."

He didn't answer for a moment. On the far side of the field the tractor had reached the end of its row and was turning, slowly, to come back.

"Because I remember everything else about that morning," he said. "Every face on those stairs. The color of the ice. The number of eggs in her basket, which was eleven, before I bought two." He turned his glass of water slowly on the little table, a quarter turn, and another. "And the one thing I didn't catch is the one thing I'd give the rest of it for. That's how it goes, I find. You notice everything, and then the river makes a noise." He looked at me. "Write down that it was eleven. You'll want it later."

I wrote it down. *Eleven eggs.* I didn't ask why. I'd learned by then that he'd tell me, and that he'd tell me when it would do the most good, and that asking only made him wait longer.

Then he picked up his glass and stood, with his hand on the rail, and said we'd go out onto the ice after lunch, and went in to see about the lunch. And I sat on the porch in the heat with the green coat beside me, and found, when I looked down, that I had my hand on its sleeve.
