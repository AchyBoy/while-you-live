## Chapter 16: Glover and Hoyt

Nine days, and nothing from Purcellville.

I'd stopped checking the mail every hour. I checked it every three. I'd stopped leaving my phone face up on the kitchen table all night and started leaving it face down, which Freda said was worse, because it meant I was pretending. She was right. She was right about most things that summer, and insufferable about all of them, and I'd never liked her more.

On the Thursday he was waiting for me at the bottom of the porch steps, standing in the shade of the maple by the gravel with his hands in his pockets.

"I wanted to see the car come up the road," he said, when I asked. "It's a good thing to see, a car coming up a road. You'll understand later." He turned and went up the steps, slowly, one hand on the rail. "Nothing from her?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

I said the date, *session thirty-seven, Thursday, July eighth*, and he settled himself on the bench in the shade with the cardigan beside him and his glass of water on the little table, and looked out at the field.

"August," he said. "The ring was back. And this time I knew where the boat came in."

* * *

*August 1780*

The road was open again. I'd known it since the twentieth of July, from a carter on a thwart with no cart. And at the beginning of August he had a parcel the shape of a quire again, under his sugar, for the first time since April. He'd been over the ferry and back, and I'd watched him go through Hill's table at the Brooklyn landing, and seen the parcel, and seen Hill's men riffle it and hand it back.

I didn't stroke his horse that time. I let him go. I'd frightened that road once, and it had worked, and now it was open again, because Washington needed it more than they were frightened. You can't frighten people twice the same way. The second time, they're ready for it. So I didn't try to close the road again. I went to the other end of it.

The boat.

* * *

I've told you about Brewster. The rebels' whaleboat officer, born in Setauket, who came over the Sound from Connecticut in the dark with armed crews to raid the Loyalist farms on the north shore. Everybody knew Brewster. He didn't hide. He used his own name, and he fought, and he took Loyalist boats in the Sound and Loyalist cattle off the shore, and the Loyalists on the Island hated him the way you hate weather.

What nobody at headquarters had worked out was that Brewster wasn't only raiding. He was collecting the mail.

I'd worked it out in the spring, with my finger on Beckwith's map, as I told you. The carter went to Setauket. Somebody in Setauket put it in a boat. There was only one rebel boat that came over to Setauket regularly, and everybody knew its captain's name. So it was Brewster. It had to be. And if it was Brewster, then on the nights the letters went across, Brewster had to come over to the Island and wait. Not raid. Wait. In a cove somewhere near Setauket, in the dark, with his boat drawn up in the reeds, for somebody to come down to the shore with a packet.

A raider moves. A man waiting for a letter has to sit still.

* * *

There were Loyalist boatmen on the Island too, of course. Refugees, mostly, men who'd been run out of Connecticut by the rebels, who'd armed boats of their own and fought Brewster's men up and down the Sound for years, a little private war on the water, raid for raid, boat for boat. The two best of them were called Glover and Hoyt. They worked out of Lloyd's Neck, a big hook of land on the north shore with a Loyalist fort on it, halfway between the city and Setauket.

I went to see them at the beginning of August, with a pass from Beckwith, on a borrowed horse.

They were not men who expected to take advice from anybody, and certainly not from a Black man on a borrowed horse with a paper they couldn't read very well. Glover was small and dark and quick, Hoyt was big and slow and red, and they both looked at me the way Captain Rudd had looked at me in the redoubt on Staten Island. But they listened. They listened because I didn't tell them anything about spies, or letters, or Washington. I told them about Brewster.

"He waits," I said. "Near Setauket. Not every week. Every two or three weeks. He comes over on a dark night and lies up in a cove all next day and goes back the night after, without raiding anything. Have you never wondered what he's doing?"

Hoyt said he'd supposed Brewster was visiting his mother.

"He's waiting for something to be brought down to him," I said. "And while he's waiting, he can't move. He's got to stay where he said he'd be. He's got to be there when they come. That's the only time Brewster's ever in one place for twenty-four hours, with his boat drawn up on the shore and his men asleep in the grass."

Glover was looking at me differently by then.

"Which cove?" he said.

"I don't know. One of the ones east of the harbor. Behind the Strong farm, perhaps, on the Neck. There's good cover there." I'd never been there. I'd heard a hundred carters and boatmen and farmers at the ferry talk about that shore, for a year, and I'd looked at Beckwith's map till I could see every cove on it. "And I don't know which night. But it'll be a dark one, near the new moon, when the carter's just come back from the city with a parcel. Watch the carter. When the carter's been to the city, watch the coves. And when you find him, take the boat while they sleep. Push it off. Without a boat he can't carry a letter, and nobody needs to be shot."

* * *

They watched.

It took them two weeks. And on the seventeenth or eighteenth of August, I don't know the exact night, I wasn't there, they found Brewster lying up near Setauket with his boat, waiting, exactly as I'd said. Waiting for dispatches from the man in the city that hadn't come down to the shore yet.

They went at him in the dark. It was a hard little fight, in the reeds and the shallows, the way all those fights on the Sound were. Brewster lost one man taken and one wounded. His men killed one of Glover's on the spot. And Brewster got back into his boat and got away across the Sound to Connecticut in the night, the way he always did, with half his crew and none of his letters.

The letters stayed on the Island. The man in the city's dispatches, the first he'd written since he'd started again, were sitting in Setauket waiting for a boat that had just been driven off. Tallmadge wrote to Washington a couple of days later, I read it afterward, that his boatman had been *drove from Long Island so suddenly* that the dispatches had been left behind. Brewster wrote, in his own letter, which was a very angry letter, that Glover and Hoyt *never stayed to bury their dead man*.

That's true. They didn't. And I never went to look for him, either.

He stopped. The bees were in the clover at the edge of the porch again. Somewhere down the road a dog was barking at nothing, on and off, the way they do in the heat.

"A man died," I said.

"A man died. One of ours. One of Glover's crew, a boatman from Stamford whose name I never knew." He was quiet. "I'd told them where to go and when. I hadn't told them to fight. But you don't send armed men at Caleb Brewster in the dark and expect a conversation." He looked at his water glass. "I said I'd tell you my part exactly, no more and no less. That's my part. A man I never met died in a creek on Long Island because I'd worked out that a raider who's waiting for a letter has to sit still."

"And the letters got through anyway."

"They got through anyway. A week late." He smiled a little, without much in it. "Brewster came back over at the end of the month and lay up behind the Strong farm again, the same cove, because he was a stubborn man and it was a good cove, and a lieutenant of dragoons went out on foot looking for the Strong family's hounds and walked within gunshot of him. Brewster wrote that the lieutenant *begged so hard* not to be taken, when they jumped him, that he let him go, because he was too near the house." He shook his head. "That's the Sound, in that war. Everybody knew everybody. Half the time they let each other go."

"So you lost that round too."

"No," he said. "No, that one I won. A week is a long time, in a war. A week late is a week in which Washington didn't know something. And those were the first real dispatches since April, and the boat that should have carried it had been shot up in the night by men who knew where it would be." He looked at me. "They knew, after that, that somebody knew. It wasn't a road anymore. It was a road with somebody standing on it. You can feel the difference, from inside. I've been on both sides of it."

* * *

*August 1780*

The last week of August I was out in Westchester again, with a troop of DeLancey's refugee horse, the Westchester men, hunting the rebels' dispatch riders.

Washington's army was up the Hudson by then, around the forts in the Highlands, and his letters to his officers in Connecticut went across the Neutral Ground by express, a dragoon on a good horse, riding hard, alone or in pairs. There was a bridge over the Croton River called Pine's Bridge that every one of them used, because it was the only good crossing for miles and the fords were deep in summer. Caesar had known it since he was a boy, long before I was in him. The body knew the bridge. It had driven cattle over it.

We lay up in the woods above Pine's Bridge for two days. On the second evening, a rebel dragoon came down the road from the north, alone, on a tired horse, with a leather satchel on his back. DeLancey's men took him on the bridge without a shot. He'd been riding since dawn. He looked very young, and very tired, and he didn't even try to run.

We brought him into New York with his satchel. I never saw what was in it. Letters to and from officers in Connecticut about horses and forage and pay. Nothing very much, Beckwith said afterward. But nothing very much is still something, if it's the enemy's, and it was in our hands and not theirs.

I remember the bridge, Ms. Barnett. That's why I'm telling you. Pine's Bridge. Rough planks, on stone piers, over brown water, with the road going up through the trees on the far side toward Tarrytown. I lay above it for two days and learned every board of it, without trying, because that's what Caesar's eyes did.

A month later, almost to the day, a gentleman in a civilian coat would cross that bridge on a borrowed horse, heading south for the British lines, and I would be on the wrong road.

But not yet.

* * *

*September 1780*

On the twelfth of September André sent me to Oyster Bay with a letter.

It was the first time he'd ever sent me with one of his own. Sealed, with his own seal, which was a little classical head, very fine. To Colonel Simcoe, whose Rangers were in quarters at Oyster Bay again, on the north shore, in the house of a merchant family called Townsend.

I took the letter out by the ferry and along the north shore road on a good horse of André's, and gave it to Simcoe in the front parlor of the Townsend house, a plain wooden house with a big tree in front and a hall that smelled of beeswax. Simcoe was a tall, cold, clever man with a long face, and his Rangers were the best soldiers the King had in that whole country, and the most feared. He broke the seal standing up, and read it at the window, and then he laughed, a short laugh, not unkind, and read a line of it aloud, to himself, in a tone of great amusement.

"*Apparent arrangements*," he said, "*are not always real ones.*" He folded the letter and put it in his coat. "Tell the Major I understand him perfectly. And tell him my men are ready."

I didn't know what it meant. I didn't know what his men were ready for. Every messenger was to remain ignorant. I rode back to the city with the answer and gave it to André word for word, as I gave everything, and André smiled and said, "Good," and went back to his papers.

I stood in the front hall of that house, Ms. Barnett, with my hat in my hands, while Simcoe read his letter. And while I stood there, one of the sons of the house came down the stairs. Home from the city for a day or two, a servant said afterward. A pale young man a few years younger than me, with a long nose and a careful mouth, in a good plain coat. He stood aside on the bottom step to let a Ranger officer go by, and glanced at me, the way a man glances at another man's servant, and went out by the back. I looked at him for perhaps three seconds. I have him still, every line.

It was another hundred and fifty years before anybody in the world knew who he was. I'd stood in his own father's hall, with André's letter in my hand, and watched him go by, and thought of nothing at all.

"Who was he?" I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he picked up his glass and drank the rest of the water in it.

"Look it up," he said. "Oyster Bay. Townsend. You're quicker than I was." He set the glass down. "That's as far as I'll go today, Ms. Barnett. Next week is the rest of September. I'd like you to bring Freda, if she'll come." He looked out at the pale field, and the heat lying on it, and the line of trees. "I don't think I want to tell September twice."

I looked it up that night, at the kitchen table. It took me about four minutes.

Robert Townsend. Of Oyster Bay. Merchant. Proven in 1930, by a historian with a handwriting expert, to have been the man in the city. The man who wrote the letters. The one who stopped writing in April, and started again in July, and whose dispatches were left sitting on the shore in August when Brewster's boat was driven off.

I sat and looked at the screen for a long time. Then I wrote in my notebook, under the date: *Tom stood in his father's hall. 1780. Watched him go by for three seconds.* And then, because I couldn't help it: *Didn't know.*

And then, because I really couldn't help it: *Neither did anyone. For 150 years.*
