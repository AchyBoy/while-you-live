## Chapter 19: The Wrong Road

He came back from the end of the drive after a quarter of an hour, slowly, and washed his hands at the kitchen sink for a long time, and then made sandwiches, because it was lunchtime, and he made lunch for us every Thursday, and I think he needed to do something that he always did.

Freda helped. He let her. She buttered the bread and he cut the cheese, and neither of them said anything, and I sat at the table and watched them, the tall girl and the old man, side by side at the counter, moving around each other in the small kitchen as if they'd done it for years.

We ate on the porch. Nobody was very hungry.

Afterward he sat back on the bench, in the shade, and looked out at the field for a while. Then he said, "All right," to nobody in particular, and I started the recorders again.

* * *

*September 1780*

A cannon is a very simple sound, at thirty miles. It isn't a bang. It's a push, in the air and in the ground, that you feel in your chest more than hear. Then another. Then a long pause. Then another.

I stood on the heights above the lines in the gray light with the officer of DeLancey's party beside me, and we counted. Fourteen shots, in perhaps half an hour. Then nothing.

"Our people?" the officer said. "Or theirs?"

I didn't know. Nobody could have known, at that distance. But I knew where it came from. Up the river, on the east side, about where Teller's Point would be. Where the *Vulture* was anchored. Where there was no reason for anybody to be firing a cannon at dawn unless they were firing at a ship.

"Theirs," I said. "From the shore. At the *Vulture*."

He looked at me. "How can you know that?"

"Because the *Vulture*'s been sitting off a rebel shore for six days doing nothing, sir, and the rebels have guns, and sooner or later somebody was going to get bored." I was already thinking past it. "If they've hit her, or frightened her, she'll drop down the river. Out of range. And if she drops down the river..."

I didn't finish. I didn't have to. If the ship had dropped down the river, then wherever André was, he wasn't on it. He'd gone ashore in the night, to meet Arnold, and he'd come back to the shore at dawn to find his ship gone, or going.

And then the only way home was the land road.

* * *

A fishing boat came down that afternoon with the news. The *Vulture* had been hit, more than once, and had dropped a few miles down the river, out of range, and anchored again off Sing Sing. She was in no hurry. Nobody aboard her was signaling anybody.

I didn't need to see. I knew. I knew the way you know a thing about a person you've watched for a long time, as I'd known Robinson was nervous, and the carter was frightened, and Beckwith was angry. André was ashore. On the rebel side of the river. And Arnold, who was frightened, every minute, from the first letter to the last, had his visitor on his hands in broad daylight with a rebel shore battery wide awake and every militiaman for miles round looking up the river at the British warship.

He'd want him gone. The fastest way he could find.

* * *

I'd spent all summer thinking about the land road, and I'd thought it through to the end long before that morning. I want you to know that, so you'll understand what I did next. It wasn't a guess.

If André came down by land, he'd start on the west side of the river, where Arnold would have taken him ashore, away from his own headquarters and the people who knew his face. And he'd have to cross to our side somewhere. The nearest crossing to Teller's Point was King's Ferry, a few miles up, and Arnold controlled King's Ferry. He'd send André over there, with a pass. And a pass from Arnold would be good on the roads Arnold commanded, which were the roads through his own posts. Down from the ferry, east, through the country behind the American lines, to their outposts. To North Castle, where a lieutenant colonel called Jameson kept a post of dragoons. And then through the outposts, on Arnold's pass, to White Plains. And from White Plains, into the Neutral Ground and down to us.

The White Plains road. A man with a pass from the commanding general would take the road his pass was written for. He'd go through the posts openly, because his pass was good at the posts. He'd ride in daylight. He'd look like what he was pretending to be, a gentleman on business, with a paper from the general. That was the sensible road. The safe road. The only road where the paper in his pocket meant anything.

And André was a soldier. He'd never been in the Neutral Ground on his own. He didn't know the back roads, and there'd be nobody to show him. He'd do what soldiers do in a strange country. He'd follow his orders, and his orders would be his pass.

So I went up the White Plains road to meet him.

* * *

My orders were to wait at the lines and on no account to go forward. I want you to know that too. I disobeyed them. I'd do it again. I'd do it every time.

I took a horse from DeLancey's party, the best one, a tall bay mare that belonged to a captain who was asleep in a farmhouse with his boots on. The trooper holding the horses watched me untie her and didn't say a word. I think he thought I'd been sent. I'd been sent by nobody. I took Robinson's paper, which was no use to me at all where I was going, and a knife, and a loaf of bread, and I rode north out of the British lines a little before sunset on the twenty-second, alone, into the Neutral Ground.

I rode by the back ways. The farm tracks, the wood roads, the fords. Every road Caesar's body had known since it was a boy. I didn't have to think about them. My hands knew when to turn the mare before my head did.

It was a clear night. Very still. A half moon. You could see a long way on the roads, and I watched them all. Every rider. Every horse tied at a tavern door. Every lantern on every porch. I passed farmhouses I'd guided Loyalist horse to, and farmhouses I'd helped burn, and farmhouses where I knew the dogs, and the dogs knew the horse I was on and didn't like it. I passed the crossroads at Young's, where the house had been, and didn't look at it.

* * *

At the ford over the Bronx River, a little after ten, I heard them before I saw them.

Horses. Three, maybe four. Coming down the bank on the far side, walking, not hurrying. Men's voices, low.

I got off the mare and took her down into the willows below the ford, into the black under the trees, and stood at her head with my hand over her nose. She was a good horse. She didn't like it, but she stood.

You have to understand what that road was, at night, for a man like me. There were two kinds of men on it. DeLancey's Cowboys, who were ours, more or less, and would have taken one look at a Black man alone on a captain's mare in the Neutral Ground and put me in irons for a deserter and a horse thief, and been right on both counts. And the Skinners, who were theirs, more or less, and would have taken the mare, and then decided whether I was worth more sold south to Virginia or hanged off a tree as a British spy. I didn't know which these were. It didn't much matter.

They came down into the ford and stopped in the middle of it to let the horses drink. Twenty yards from me. I could hear the water running round the horses' legs, and the horses sucking at it, and the men.

"The dragoons have got the whole road from North Castle watched," one of them said. A young voice, bored. "Dragoons at every crossroads. Nobody's getting down that road tonight without a pass."

"Then they won't come by that road," another said. Older. "The ones that want to get through. They'll go by the river. Tarrytown way."

"There's men out on the river road too."

"There's men out on every road," the older one said. "Cattle thieves and the men who catch cattle thieves, and you can't tell the one from the other in the dark." He spat. "Come on."

The mare blew against my palm. Very softly. I felt it more than heard it, a long warm breath in the cup of my hand.

The young one turned his head toward the willows.

I didn't breathe. The mare didn't move. In the water, his horse lifted its head, with the river running off its muzzle, and looked at the willows too, for a long moment, and then put its head down and drank again.

"Come on," the older one said again, and they went up the bank on my side and away along the road toward White Plains, and their voices went with them, and after a while there was only the water.

I stood in the willows a long time after. Not because I was afraid they'd come back. Because of what the older one had said.

*They won't come by that road. They'll go by the river.*

I thought about it. I want you to know that I thought about it properly, there in the dark with my hand still on the mare's nose. And I reasoned it away. A man running from the dragoons would go by the river, yes. A man with no pass. A cattle thief, a deserter, a spy without papers. But André wasn't running from the dragoons. André had a pass from their own commanding general. Jameson's dragoons would salute him and wish him a good night. The White Plains road was the one road in all that country where a pass in Arnold's hand would carry him through. The river road was the one place where it was no use at all.

So I got back on the mare, and rode on toward White Plains. I was right about the pass. Every word of it was right. I've gone over it a thousand times. It was the best reasoning I did all that year.

* * *

Below White Plains, the road forks. The left fork goes on to White Plains. The right goes north and east, up toward Bedford, past a farm with good bottom land and a stone wall along the road. Reynolds's country.

I stopped the mare at the fork.

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for her to start cropping the grass at the side of the road. I'd disobeyed my orders. I'd taken a captain's horse. When I got back to the lines, if I got back, there'd be men waiting who'd want to know why, and I wasn't going to be able to tell them, because the honest answer was that I'd ridden out alone into the Neutral Ground in the middle of the night to look for the Adjutant General of the British army on the strength of a feeling. I could turn round. Nobody had seen me go. I could be back at the lines by dawn and say I'd ridden the picket line all night, and they'd believe me, because nobody ever wondered where a guide had been.

The mare lifted her head and looked down the left fork, toward White Plains, the way horses do when they've decided for you.

"Yes," I said to her. "All right."

* * *

By midnight I was on the high ground above White Plains, looking north up the long road that came down from North Castle, from the American posts. The road he'd come down, if he came.

I waited there all night. In a stand of cedars above the road, with the mare tied behind me and the bread in my coat, watching that road come down out of the dark.

The cold came up out of the ground the way it does in September, after a hot day. I couldn't light a fire. I walked up and down behind the cedars to keep my feet. Every hour or so something came along the road, and every time my heart went up into my mouth. A dog, trotting, on its own business. A cart, with a lantern swinging under it and a man asleep on the seat. At three in the morning, a single rider, coming south at a walk. I watched him the whole length of that road, a quarter of a mile, in the moonlight. A man alone. A good horse. A cloak.

He came under the cedars and I saw him. A farmer, fat, half asleep in the saddle, with a sack of something tied behind him. He went by twenty feet below me, snoring, I think. I stood in the cedars with my hand on my knife and my heart going like a drum, and watched him out of sight, and then I sat down in the needles with my back against a tree and shook, the way you do after.

Nobody else came.

* * *

At first light I went further north. I couldn't help it. Up the road toward North Castle, as far as I dared, which was further than I should have. I thought he might have been held up, at a post, by a careful sentry. I thought he might have lodged somewhere for the night, the way travelers did. I thought a great many things. I asked a boy driving cows, keeping my head down like a hired man, whether he'd seen a gentleman ride by, and he said there'd been gentlemen riding by all week with the armies the way they were, and asked me if I was a runaway, and I said no and gave him half the bread.

The sun came up. It got warm. Nine o'clock came, and went, on that empty road. I can tell you the exact moment, because I've worked it out since, a thousand times, from the records. At about nine o'clock on the morning of the twenty-third of September, while I sat on a borrowed horse behind a wall above the North Castle road, looking north, with half a loaf of bread in my coat, Major John André was sitting on a borrowed horse on a different road, perhaps eight miles to the west of me, being asked to get down.

On the river road. Tarrytown way.

* * *

Not the road his pass was for. The other road. The river road, that ran down along the Hudson through the Neutral Ground, through country that was nobody's, where his pass from Arnold was no use to him at all. Where every band of men on the road was either the King's Cowboys or the rebels' militia, or nobody's, and a gentleman alone in a good coat was a gift from heaven to any of them.

Why? I've asked that every year since. The man who crossed with him at King's Ferry left him near Pine's Bridge that morning, after breakfast, and turned back. The story goes that somebody had told André, the night before or that morning, that the river road was safer. That it was in the Cowboys' country, and that the Cowboys were friends to the King. Which they were, mostly, if you were the King. Perhaps somebody told him that. Perhaps he worked it out for himself, and was too clever, the way he'd been afraid he'd be. He took the road toward people he thought were his own side.

At a little stream above Tarrytown, three men stepped out of the trees with muskets. One of them was wearing a faded Hessian coat that he'd taken off somebody somewhere. And André looked at the coat, and thought he was among friends, and said the thing you never say.

He told them he was a British officer.

* * *

There were seven or eight of them, in the end, rebel militia, out on the road that morning on their own account, looking for cattle thieves and anything else worth taking. Three stopped him, and searched him. They made him take off his boots. And in his stockings, under his feet, they found papers in General Arnold's own hand: the state of the works at West Point, the strength of the garrison, the placing of the guns, the minutes of a council of war.

He offered them his horse. Any sum of money they liked to name. They took his watch, and took him to the American post at North Castle.

He was wearing somebody else's coat.

He stopped.

He didn't stop the way he'd stopped before, to drink his water or to look at the field. He stopped the way a clock stops. His hands were on his knees, and he was looking at nothing, and he didn't move.

Freda didn't move either. She was sitting on the top step of the porch, where she'd gone after lunch, with her back against the post and her arms round her knees, and she was looking at him, and her face was wet. She wasn't making any sound at all.

I reached over, after a while, and turned off the recorders. Both of them. I didn't ask. It was the first time I'd ever done that, in all those Thursdays, without being asked. He didn't seem to notice.

We sat there. The three of us, on the porch, in the afternoon, in the heat. Somewhere a long way off a tractor was working, and the sound of it came and went on the hot air. A car passed, on the paved road at the end of the gravel, a mile off, and he turned his head very slightly toward the sound, and then away.

"He took the coat," he said at last. Quietly. Not to us. "He promised me, and he took the coat."

Nobody said anything.

"Arnold made him," he said. "I know that now. I've read all of it, everything anybody ever wrote down. Arnold was frightened, and in a hurry, and the ship was gone, and he talked André into it, into the coat and the pass and the papers in his boots, all three, every one of the three things that hanged him. And André let him. Because he was a soldier who'd been told to do a thing by a general. Because he was clever, and he could see that the coat was quicker. Because he didn't know the roads, and he trusted the man who did." He turned his head and looked at me, and I'd never seen his face like that. "He trusted the wrong man who knew the roads, Ms. Barnett. That's all. He had two of us. He trusted the wrong one."

* * *

After a long time he said I could turn the recorders back on.

"Not because I want to go on," he said. "Because I want it written down. Freda will type it. I want the rest of it on the tape, the way it was, so it's said once, and done."

I turned them on. He said the rest of it in a level voice, looking at the field.

He'd ridden back to the British lines on the afternoon of the twenty-third, with no news, and been put under arrest by DeLancey's officer for taking a horse and disobeying orders, and released the next morning when Beckwith sent for him. On the twenty-fourth, nothing. The *Vulture* lay in the river, waiting. Robinson sent a letter down to Clinton that day saying they'd heard nothing of André since he left the ship. On the twenty-fifth, a rumor, from the river, that something had happened at West Point, that Arnold had come down to the *Vulture* in his barge in a great hurry, under a flag, and gone aboard her and not come off.

And on the twenty-sixth, General Benedict Arnold came into New York.

"The people exult much," the Chief Justice wrote in his diary that night. *But it is not known yet that André was catched.*

"That's the only time I ever saw him," he said. "Arnold. On the twenty-seventh, on the Broadway, coming out of headquarters with two of Clinton's aides, in his new British coat, with his limp. He walked straight past me. I was standing at the gate, holding a horse. He didn't look at me, and I didn't look at him." He was quiet a moment. "I told you I never met him, and then I didn't. That's what I meant. I stood six feet from the man who'd talked André into that coat, and I didn't do anything at all. I held the horse."

He looked at Freda, on the step.

"That's the end of the road part," he said, gently. "You can stop now. Both of you. The rest is a week of waiting. I'll tell it next Thursday."

Freda wiped her face on the knees of her jeans, first one side, then the other.

"No," she said. "I want to hear it now. All of it. I don't want to wait a week knowing."

He looked at her for a long time.

"You're very like your mother," he said.

"Everybody says that," Freda said, and her voice cracked in the middle, and she didn't care. "Tell it anyway."
