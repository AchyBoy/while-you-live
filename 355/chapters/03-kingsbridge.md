## Chapter 3: Kingsbridge

He'd made the tea before I got there, and it was already on the kitchen table in the brown pot. I set out the recorders and said the date into them. *Session thirty, Thursday, May twentieth.*

"Kingsbridge," he said. "June. The middle of the night."

* * *

*June 1779*

The Hessian who found me that night was about nineteen, and frightened, and trying very hard not to look it, and I liked him at once.

He was standing in the road at the top of the Kingsbridge lines with his musket across his body and a lantern on a pole stuck in the ditch beside him, which is the worst place a picket can put his lantern, because it shows the enemy exactly where he is and blinds him to everything outside its circle. I had watched him from the trees for half an hour. He had not looked into the dark once. He had looked at the lantern, mostly, the way you look at a fire.

So I came out of the trees slowly, into the edge of the light, with my hands open in front of me so he could see they were empty, and I said, in English, clearly, as I'd practiced it on the walk down, "I am come in on the General's proclamation."

He pointed his musket at my stomach and shouted something in German. Then he shouted it again, louder, as men do with foreigners, and backed up two steps into the lantern pole and nearly knocked it into the ditch.

I didn't move. I said it again, the same words, the same tone. *I am come in on the General's proclamation.* Then I said it in German, as well as I could, which wasn't very well. I hadn't spoken German in that life, or for a long time before it, and the body's mouth had never made those sounds. It came out like a man talking with stones in his cheeks. But he understood the word *General*, and he understood that I wasn't running, and after a while he stopped shouting and shouted instead, over his shoulder, for somebody called Friedrich.

Friedrich was older and had a lantern of his own and a pipe in his mouth, and he looked at me for a long time without saying anything. Then he took the pipe out and said, in English nearly as bad as my German, "Run from rebel?"

"Yes."

"Which rebel?"

"Captain Joseph Reynolds," I said. "Of the Westchester militia."

That mattered more than anything else I'd say that night, and I knew it. The proclamation covered a man who ran from a rebel. It didn't cover a man who ran from a Loyalist. If my master had been a friend to the King, the King's soldiers would have tied my hands and walked me back up the road to him in the morning, and been paid for it. Everything turned on Reynolds being what he was. I was ready to prove it six ways if they asked: his company, his lieutenant's name, the house where they mustered, the tavern where they drank afterward. They didn't ask. Friedrich only nodded, as if he'd heard all of it before, which I suppose he had, many times that month, and jerked his head toward the dark behind the lines.

"Come," he said.

* * *

They kept me that night in a barn at the back of the post, with two other men who'd come in the same week. One was from New Jersey, a big quiet man who had come across the Hudson in a stolen boat and didn't want to talk about it. The other was a boy of fifteen or so from somewhere near Tarrytown, who wanted to talk about nothing else and couldn't stop. He'd been there four days. He had a blanket, and he shared it, and he told me everything he knew about what happened next, most of which was wrong.

In the morning an English officer came out with a book under his arm.

It was the book I'd been waiting for, though I didn't know that's what it would look like. A long ledger, bound in brown calf, half full already, with columns ruled down each page in red ink. The officer was a lieutenant, young and sunburned, with a cold, and he sat on an upturned bucket in the barn door and opened the ledger on his knee and dipped his pen and asked us, one at a time, our names.

I've told you about that already. *Caesar.* And what else? *Freeman.* He wrote it, and spelled it right, and I read it upside down from four feet away while he blotted it, and in that moment I had it, the whole page, every name above mine. Twenty-two of them. Where each man came from. Whom he had belonged to. Which ones could drive a team or swing an axe or handle a boat. And a note in the margin by one name from the week before, in a different hand: *Returned to owner, a loyal subject.*

It was the first time I understood what that body could do.

* * *

I should tell you about it properly, because it's the reason for everything that happened to me over the next two years, and I didn't understand it myself for a long time.

Caesar's body had a memory such as I've never had before or since, in any life. I don't mean a good memory. I've had good memories. I mean that anything his eyes fell on stayed, as it was, as long as I lived in him. A page, a face, a list of numbers, the pattern of a quilt on a bed I walked past once. I didn't have to try. It went in like light into a room. And afterward, if I wanted it, I could go back and stand in front of it and read it off again, as if I were holding the paper.

Caesar himself had never known. How could he? He couldn't read. Nobody had ever let him. All his life he'd had the most remarkable eyes in the county and nothing to point them at but fields and cattle and the roads he was sent down. He'd known every road, and every farm, and every horse he'd ever handled, better than any man alive, and everybody thought him slow, because he didn't say much, and because he was owned, and people find it very easy to think slowly of anyone they own.

I could read. I'd been reading for two thousand years. Put my reading behind his eyes, and the two of us together could take in a page at a glance and keep it.

It was a gift, Ms. Barnett. It was also the most dangerous thing about me, and I knew that by the second day.

* * *

Because I couldn't let a soul know I could read.

You have to understand how a Black man reading looked in 1779, in British New York, to the people who held the power over him. The British officers didn't much care, one way or the other, as long as you were useful. But the British officers weren't the city. The city was full of Loyalists, men who'd lost their farms and their businesses to the rebels and run to New York with nothing, and a good many of them had brought their own enslaved people with them, or meant to get them back. To them a reading Black man was a man who could forge a pass. He could read a newspaper and learn the war was going badly. He could write a letter to a rebel. He was the worst thing they could imagine.

And a Black man suspected of anything, in that city, had nobody. No court. The city was under the army, and the army's justice ran up a chain of officers who might hear him or might not, depending on their dinner. I watched a man taken off the street outside a tavern in Queen Street, that first summer, because a Loyalist merchant said he'd seen him reading a handbill on a wall. He was a free man, born free on Long Island. He had his papers in his coat. It took him six weeks to get out of the Provost, and he came out with his papers gone.

So I learned to look at a page without looking at it. A glance, while I was doing something else. Holding a horse beside a man reading a letter. Carrying a candle past a desk. Standing behind a chair. One look, and I'd have it, and then I'd look away and never look back. The glance was all I ever got, all those two years. It was all I ever needed.

* * *

They took me to Colonel Robinson on the third day.

Beverley Robinson was a Virginian by birth, which nobody ever let you forget, and a New Yorker by marriage. He'd married a Philipse, one of the great land families of the Hudson, and before the war he had owned a great part of the Highlands, up the river, and lived in a fine house there among his tenants like a duke. The rebels took the house and the land when he wouldn't swear to them. He came down to New York, raised a regiment of Loyalists at his own expense, and spent the rest of the war trying to get his country back, and, I think, his house.

He was nearly sixty then, heavy and gray and courteous, with a gentleman's soft voice and the patience of a man who has spent his life collecting rents. And he collected information the way he'd collected rents: steadily, from everybody, without ever seeming to ask. Every man who came in through the lines from Westchester was sent to him sooner or later, because Westchester was his country, and nobody knew it better.

Until I came.

He saw me in a farmhouse behind the lines that his officers used, in a room with a table and two chairs and a map of the county on the wall, painted, very fine, with the farms marked in tiny red squares and the names written beside them in a careful copperplate hand. He sat in one chair. He didn't offer me the other, and I didn't expect it. A clerk sat in the corner with a writing board on his knee.

"You're Reynolds's man," he said. "From Bedford."

"I was, sir."

"I knew Reynolds's father." He looked at me for a while, pleasantly. "Tell me how you came down."

So I told him. The mill on the Croton, the wagon left with the grain still in it. The two nights. The hollow by the creek where I slept in the day. And because I could see the map on the wall behind him, and because I could see, as I talked, that it was wrong, I told him that too, without seeming to.

"I didn't come by the Post Road, sir," I said. "There's a picket at the Hammond place now, since the spring. They moved it from the crossroads." His map had it at the crossroads. "And I didn't come by the Saw Mill road, because the bridge over the river at Odell's is down. It went in the April flood."

His clerk stopped writing. Robinson didn't turn round to look at his map. He didn't have to. He knew perfectly well what was on it.

"The bridge at Odell's," he said.

"Yes, sir. Half of it. You can still get a man across. Not a horse."

He looked at me with a different sort of attention then. Not suspicion, exactly. More the look a man gives a field he has driven past for years, when he notices for the first time that it's better land than he thought.

"Tell me the rest," he said. "Everything you passed. Every house, and who's in it now."

It took me two hours. I gave him every farm between Bedford and Kingsbridge, all of them, the way Caesar had known them from twenty years of being sent down those roads, and everything I had added to it myself in the six months I'd been in him. Who'd gone over to the rebels and who was only pretending, for safety. Which houses had men in them who'd been out with the militia and which had men hiding in the cellar from it. Where the rebel guard boats lay up on the river, and which widow on the Sawmill River took in their wounded. The clerk filled six sheets. Robinson listened to all of it without interrupting, and at the end he sat for a moment, quite still, and then stood up and took a quill and went to his own map on the wall, and moved the picket from the crossroads to the Hammond place, and drew a small neat line through the bridge at Odell's.

"You'll guide for us," he said, with his back to me. "There's a fee. The Quartermaster General keeps the guides on his books. You'll be paid by the day when we use you and not when we don't, and you'll come when you're sent for, day or night." He turned round. "Can you ride?"

"Yes, sir."

"Can you read?"

He said it in exactly the same voice. Pleasantly. As if it were the next question on a list. I suppose it was. Behind him on the table, under his hand, there was a letter he'd been reading when I came in, face up, and I had read it, all of it, at a glance, through the door, before I'd taken my second step into the room. A report from one of his agents in the Highlands. Eight lines about the rebels' boats at Peekskill.

"No, sir," I said.

He looked at me a moment longer. Then he nodded, and sat down, and folded the letter in half, without hurrying, and put it in his coat.

* * *

That was how it started. A guide, on the books, at a fee by the day.

They gave me a paper with Robinson's name on it, to show any sentry who stopped me, and a place to sleep in a long wooden shed by the North River, near the Commons, with other men who'd come in the same way and worked for the army: wagoners, axemen, men who drove cattle and men who dug. The army fed us, some days, and paid us, most weeks. The shed leaked. The man in the next place along was a carpenter from Virginia who'd come all the way up with Dunmore's people in '76 and lived through the smallpox camp, and had the marks of it all over his face, and he could build anything, and nobody would let him, because they only wanted him to dig. His name was Prince. I've thought about him a great deal, since.

And in July, Robinson sent for me in the middle of the night, and I went, the way he'd said, and he put me on a horse with a troop of Loyalist horse going up into the Neutral Ground after a rebel who'd been seen at a farmhouse near Eastchester. I took them round by the back of the farm, by the creek, and we came in quietly and the man was asleep in his bed and his boots by the fire, and he came back with us to the lines with his hands tied, swearing. He wasn't a spy. He was a rebel militia lieutenant who'd come home to see his wife. They exchanged him a month later for a Loyalist captain.

It was a small thing. The officer in charge thanked me, at the end, and gave me a shilling of his own, and said Robinson had told him I knew the country, and that Robinson was right. I don't think he thought of me again.

But Robinson did. And so, before very long, did somebody else.

He stopped and looked down at his hands. We were in the kitchen that day, because the porch had been in full sun since eleven, and he'd brought the tea in from the stove and forgotten it, and it had gone cold in the pot.

"Did he know?" I said. "Robinson. That you'd read his letter."

"I've wondered that for two hundred and forty-eight years." He reached for the pot, found it cold, and poured himself a cup anyway, and drank it. "I think he knew there was something. He couldn't have said what. He was the kind of man who notices that his dog is cleverer than other people's dogs, and doesn't like to think too hard about why." He set the cup down. "He folded that letter very carefully, after I said no. And he never once left another one on the table when I was in the room. Not in two years."

"Did anyone ever find out?" I said. "That you could read."

He looked at me over the cup for a long moment.

"Once," he said.

Then he looked at the clock on the wall over the stove, which said a quarter to two, and got up to put the kettle on again, and didn't say who. I wrote it in my notebook, under the date, the way I always do: *Once.* And the recorders ran on in the warm kitchen with the window open and the hay smell coming in, and the kettle began, very quietly, to tick.
