## Chapter 22: Bookbinder

She wouldn't talk on the phone. I hadn't expected her to.

"Not like this," she said, after the twenty-seven minutes, after a long silence with the radio and the horse in it. "Saturday. Not the barn. Somewhere else." She named a diner on Route 7 outside Leesburg, halfway between us. "Noon."

"Noon," I said.

There was another silence. Then, "You didn't tell him," she said. "My father. You could have. You didn't."

"No."

"Why not?"

I thought about how to say it.

"Because I asked you a question," I said. "Not him."

She hung up without saying goodbye. I sat at the kitchen table with the phone in my hand for a long time. Then I went and woke Freda, which I never do in summer, and told her, and she sat bolt upright in bed with her hair over her face and said, "I'm coming," and I said she wasn't coming into the diner, and she said she'd sit in the car, and I said fine, because I wanted her there, and we both knew it.

* * *

The next day was Thursday, so I went up. Freda came. She'd had her learner's permit since March and she drove the first hour, both hands on the wheel, while I sat beside her and tried not to hold the door handle.

I told him on the porch, about the call. *It was twenty-seven minutes.* He listened, and then he sat back and closed his eyes for a moment in the sun.

"Saturday," he said. "Good."

And then he wouldn't do any 1780 at all. "Not this week," he said. "You wouldn't hear a word of it. Neither of you." So I said the date into the recorders anyway, *session forty, Thursday, July twenty-ninth*, and they ran for an hour and ten minutes on the three of us sitting on his porch talking about pie crust, and a hawk over the field, and whether a cat with white feet could be called anything but Socks. Freda typed it on the Sunday and said it was her favorite tape of the whole year, and I've never asked her why, because I know.

* * *

It was the kind of diner I'd done half my reporting in, years ago. Chrome and red vinyl, a long counter with stools, pies under plastic domes, a waitress with a coffee pot who called everybody hon. I got there at a quarter to twelve and sat in a booth by the window where I could see the lot. Freda was in the car, at the far end of it, with the windows down and her headphones round her neck, pretending to look at her phone.

At five to twelve a dusty white pickup with a vet's box on the back pulled in and parked, and Claire Whitcombe sat in it with the engine running for a minute, looking at the diner. Then she turned off the engine and got out.

She'd come straight from a call. She was in the same kind of jeans and flannel and boots, with her sleeves rolled, and she came in and looked round once and saw me and came over and sat down across the booth without saying anything. The waitress came. Claire ordered coffee. I ordered coffee. When the waitress had gone, she put both hands flat on the table, the way he does, and looked at them.

"I've been sick about you for six years," she said.

* * *

I'm going to tell it the way she told it. As near as I can. I didn't have a recorder. I didn't take a note, the whole time, not one, and I'd only done that once before, in a barn. I wrote it all down that night at the kitchen table, for an hour, while Freda sat across from me and didn't say a word.

Her father had called her on the Wednesday morning at seven. She was at home, in Purcellville, getting ready for the day. He'd never called her that early in her life. He said he needed her to drive him into the city, right away, and not to ask him why, and could she please come.

She'd come. Of course she had. He was seventy-three and he didn't drive in the city anymore, and he'd never once asked her for anything like that.

On the way in he told her a little. That a young man who worked for him at Carrow, a man she'd known since she was a teenager, who'd been to their house at Christmas, who'd been her father's apprentice for fifteen years, had done something brave and foolish. That he'd given a newspaper a great deal of Carrow's private paperwork. That the paper had run a story on the Sunday. And that on Tuesday night, very late, someone at Carrow had telephoned her father at home and told him they knew who it was.

"He didn't tell me who called," Claire said. "He's never told me. Somebody high up. Somebody he'd known for thirty years." She turned her coffee cup round on the table. "They told him they knew. They told him they'd be at the apartment with the police by the end of the week, with a warrant, for the papers. Stolen company documents. They said they'd prosecute. They said they'd make sure he never worked with a piece of paper again in his life, and that he'd go to prison if they could manage it. And they said..." She stopped. "They said my father should think very carefully about whose side he was on."

"So he went to get the papers."

"He went to get the papers. Before they could. Because he said the papers were the only proof that your story was true, and if Carrow got them, they'd disappear." She looked up at me for the first time. "That's what he said. I've thought about it for six years. I believe he meant it. I think he also meant that if Carrow got the papers they'd find out what was in them, and some of what was in them was letters he'd been asked to repair, himself, over the years. And had." She looked back down. "Both of those. I don't know which was more."

* * *

She told me about the morning. The building over the dry cleaner's, with the steam coming up through the grates. The alley behind it. Her father going in by the back door, which stuck, and which he had to fight. Her father laughing on the stairs, once, too loud, the way he always laughs when he's frightened. She'd heard it from the car. Her sitting in the car with the engine running because she was afraid to turn it off, in case they needed to leave in a hurry, in case somebody came. Every car that passed the end of the alley.

"Twenty-seven minutes," she said. "I watched the clock on the dash the whole time. I've never been so frightened in my life. I've had a horse go down on me in a trailer on the interstate, and I wasn't as frightened as that." She almost smiled. "Nobody's ever asked me how long. You're the only person who's ever asked."

"Somebody told me to."

"Who?"

"A man who knows what it's like to wait in the wrong place for somebody who doesn't come," I said.

She looked at me oddly, but she didn't ask.

They'd brought down two boxes and his computer, she said. And him. He'd come down the stairs white as paper and got into the back seat with the boxes and not said a word all the way out of the city. He thought he was going for a day or two. To the country, to her father's house, until things calmed down. He'd shut his cat in the bedroom, because her father was at the door, and he'd left food, and he'd meant to go back for her that night, or ask a neighbor.

"And that afternoon," Claire said, "at the house, my father told him the rest. About the warrant, and the prosecution, and what they'd said. And he said he couldn't go back. Not that night. Maybe not ever. He said if he went back to that apartment he'd walk straight into them." She closed her eyes. "And he said, *Who'll feed Pearl?* That was the first thing he said. Not his job. Not you. *Who'll feed Pearl?*"

I couldn't say anything.

"My father said he'd see to it. He said he'd call the landlord." She opened her eyes. "He didn't. He forgot. Or he didn't want to call anyone who might ask where the tenant was. I don't know which. I found out on the Saturday. I've never forgiven him for that, either."

* * *

The waitress came back and filled our cups, and asked if we were ready to order, and we both said no, and she went away.

"Why are you telling me this?" I said. "Now."

She was quiet for a long time.

"Because I'm angry," she said. "Because I've been angry for six years and there's nobody I can tell. My father says it was the right thing. He says the papers are safe and the young man's safe and the lawsuit went away and everything worked out. He says you'd be fine. He said that, at the time. *Reporters always land on their feet.*" She looked at me. "And then I read about you. The correction. You losing your job. People on the internet calling you a liar, for years. And I thought, that's us. We did that. We took the one thing that would have shown she was right, and put it in our barn, and let her..."

She stopped.

"Let me hold it," I said. "By myself."

"Yes."

"For six years. While everybody decided I'd made him up."

"Yes," Claire said, and her voice broke, and she put her hand over her mouth.

And I understood that the thing I'd been carrying for six years, she'd been carrying too. From the other end. And that she'd been carrying it with nobody to put it down in front of, because the only other people who knew were the two men she'd driven out of that alley, and one of them thought it had all worked out, and the other one was hiding.

"He's alive," I said. "Isn't he."

She took her hand away from her mouth.

"He wants to see you," she said.

* * *

She drove. I want that on the record. Claire Whitcombe drove, and I sat in the passenger seat of her pickup, with her vet's kit rattling in the back, and Freda in the narrow back seat behind us, two hours west, over the Blue Ridge, into the Shenandoah Valley, to a small town with a courthouse and a college and a main street of old brick buildings with mountains standing up at both ends of it. She drove the way she'd watched that horse: carefully, not fast, all the way.

He lived in a little white frame house on a side street, near the college, with a porch and a big garage behind it with the door rolled up. He was in the garage when we pulled up. I saw him before he saw me.

He was at a workbench, under a lamp, with a book open in front of him. An old book, big and heavy, with the spine coming away. He had a brush in one hand and a little pot of glue at his elbow, and he was laying a strip of cloth along the inside of the spine, very slowly, very carefully, smoothing it with the side of his thumb. He was thinner. His hair had gone gray at the temples. He had glasses on that he hadn't had before. He looked exactly the same.

He'd always worked like that. I'd forgotten. When we met in diners, he used to fold the paper napkin into smaller and smaller squares while he talked, exactly matching the corners, and then unfold it and smooth it out flat with the side of his thumb.

Claire turned off the engine. He looked up.

He saw her first. Then me.

He put the brush down in the glue pot, carefully, so it wouldn't drip. He took off his glasses and folded them and put them in his shirt pocket. And then he stood there behind his workbench, in his garage, with his hands at his sides, and looked at me across six years, and I saw that he was more frightened than I'd ever seen him, more frightened than in any parking lot or diner, more frightened than the first night in the diner, when he slid into the booth across from me with a folder under his arm and asked if I was Iola Barnett.

I got out of the truck.

I'd thought about what I'd say, for six years. On bad nights, when I was furious with him, I'd had whole speeches. On worse nights, I'd thought he was dead, and there'd been nothing to say at all. I stood in his driveway in the sun and every word of every speech went out of my head.

"I kept the receipt," I said. "From the diner. The grilled cheese. You never ate it."

He looked at me for a long moment. And then something happened to his face that I'd never seen happen to it before, in eight months of meetings, and he came round the end of the workbench and across the garage, and I don't remember which of us got there first.

* * *

We sat in his kitchen, after. Claire and him and me. Freda had climbed out of the truck and stood in the garage door, uncertain, and he'd looked at her and said, "You're the daughter. You were ten," and she'd said yes, and he'd said, "I'm sorry," and she'd said, "It's okay," in a voice that meant it mostly was, and gone to sit on the porch, where she could hear.

He made grilled cheese. In a black iron pan, on an old gas stove, with tomato soup from a can heated in a little saucepan beside it. He didn't ask if we wanted any. He made four, and put them on four plates, and sent one out to Freda on the porch, and sat down with us at his kitchen table.

And he ate it. All of it. The whole sandwich, and the soup, with a spoon, slowly, while I watched him.

"Six years," he said, when he'd finished. "I couldn't eat in front of you. Every time. I used to go home after and eat two." He looked at the empty plate. "I was so frightened, all those months. All the time. I was frightened walking into that diner and frightened walking out. And you were the only person I'd ever met who didn't think I was mad."

"I didn't think you were mad."

"I know." He looked up. "That's why I chose you."

* * *

I asked him about Pearl. He asked me first, really. Before the papers, before the lawsuit, before anything else. The first thing he asked me, sitting at his kitchen table with his hands round his coffee cup, was whether I knew what had happened to his cat.

I told him what I knew. That Petrakis had found her on the Friday, shut in the bedroom, half starved, and taken her to the shelter himself, and complained about the carpet. That I'd never asked, after.

He nodded slowly.

"I called the shelter," he said. "A month later. From a pay phone, in a gas station in Front Royal. I said I was a neighbor. They told me she'd been adopted. A family with two little girls." He was quiet for a moment. "I've thought about those girls a great deal. I hope they let her sleep on the bed."

He had another cat now. A big gray one with white feet, asleep in a basket on top of the refrigerator, that he'd taken from the same kind of shelter in the valley, three years ago. He didn't say why he'd picked that one. He didn't need to.

* * *

The boxes were in Gerald Whitcombe's barn, in Middleburg, Claire said, in the loft, under a tarp, where they'd been for six years, with the computer. Her father went up and looked at them once a year, she thought. She'd seen him do it.

"They're yours," Bookbinder said to me. "They always were. I'd have given them to you that week, if..." He stopped. "I want you to have them. All of it. I want it printed. I want it in the paper with your name on it, the way it should have been, and I want..." He stopped again, and took off his glasses, and put them back on. "I want my name on it too. If you'll have it. I've spent six years being nobody in a garage in a valley. I'd like to be somebody again before I'm too old to enjoy it."

"Your name," I said.

"My real one." He told it to me. Across his kitchen table, at last. He'd never needed to before. He'd always just been the man in the booth.

I didn't write it down. I've never written it down, and I'm not going to now. He says I may. He says I should. He says it doesn't matter anymore, and that he'd like it in the paper, and it was, on the fifteenth of August, in black and white, under mine.

But in my notebook, and here, he's still what he was the first night I came home from the diner and opened a fresh page and needed something to call him. A man who mended old books, and kept copies of everything, and folded his napkin into smaller and smaller squares while he talked, and smoothed it flat again with the side of his thumb.

Bookbinder.

* * *

Claire drove us back over the mountains in the dark, to the diner on Route 7, where our car had sat all day. Freda drove us home from there. She drove carefully, not fast, the whole way, with both hands on the wheel, and I sat beside her with the window down and the night air coming in, and didn't say anything for a long time.

"Mom," Freda said, somewhere past Leesburg.

"Mm."

"You have to tell Mr. Hale."

"I know."

"He said the dogs had gone quiet," Freda said. "He knew. He knew it was this."

"He didn't know," I said. "He thought it."

"He knew," Freda said, with great certainty, watching the road. "He always knows. He just waits for you to get there, so you'll have had it yourself." She was quiet for a mile. "That's what he does with the eggs, too."

I turned my head and looked at her in the dashboard light.

"Don't say it," I said. "He asked us not to."

"I'm not saying anything," Freda said. "I'm driving."
