## Chapter 8: Over the Ice

After the eggs, he wanted the porch again. The rain had stopped by then, and the boards were steaming a little where the sun came through. He sat on the bench with his tea and looked at the wet field for a while, and when he spoke, it wasn't about Gerald Whitcombe at all.

* * *

*January 1780*

I said I'd give you one clean one first, and I have. The next two aren't clean. I'm going to tell them anyway, and I'm going to tell you exactly what my part in them was, no more and no less, and you can decide what you think. That's the bargain.

After the night of the five deserters, Beckwith sent for me again. He'd had General Stirling's report by then. He didn't say anything about it. He didn't need to. He looked at me across his cold little table for a while, the way he'd looked at me the first time, and then he unrolled a map.

"They came to us across the ice," he said. "Now we go to them."

* * *

You have to understand the mood in the city that week. It wasn't relief. It was rage.

The Loyalists on Staten Island had watched three thousand rebels, their own neighbors from across the water, sit on the hills above their farms for two days, and then strip those farms on the way home: the cattle, the grain, the blankets off the beds. Jersey farmers had followed the soldiers across the ice with empty sleighs, on purpose, to fill them. Men had come back to their houses on the island and found the doors off and the furniture gone. And every one of those Loyalists knew some of the faces that had done it.

The army wanted to answer. The Loyalist regiments wanted to answer more. And for once there was a road to answer on. Elizabethtown was just across the water from the island, on the Jersey shore, where Lord Stirling's sleighs had gathered. There was a rebel guard in the town, and stores, and officers in billets. And for a few more weeks, maybe days, there was a road of ice straight to it.

The question was how to get there without being heard. Lord Stirling had come across the ice like a brass band. Everybody in the city knew it by then, from the deserters and from me.

"You heard them," Beckwith said. "Could they hear us?"

"If you go the way they came, sir, yes. Straight across from the watering place to the Point. It's the shortest way and the thickest ice and they'll be listening for it every night now."

"Then how?"

I showed him. South, along the island's own shore, close in, where the snow lay thick on the ice and swallowed the sound. Then across the narrow water at the south end of the bay, not to the Point but below it, to a place called Trembly's Point, where the marsh came down to the shore and nobody kept a guard because nobody could imagine an army landing in a salt marsh. No sleighs. Men on foot, and the cavalry walking their horses, with sacking on the hooves. No bells. No torches. No talking. Then up through the marsh to the town in the last hour before light, and take the guard in their beds, and the officers with them, and come away by the same road before the country woke.

Beckwith asked me what it was for. It was a fair question. There were men in the city who wanted it for nothing but revenge.

"Prisoners, sir," I said. "Alive. Every man you take out of his bed is a man you can trade for one of ours in a rebel jail, and half the Loyalists on that island have a brother in one. A man asleep in his bed can't be shot, and he can't shoot anybody either. If they hear us coming, it's a fight in the streets, with women at the windows. If they don't, it's a theft." I'd thought about it a long time. "That's the whole of the plan, sir. Quiet enough that nobody has to fire."

Beckwith looked at me for a moment, and then he nodded, slowly. I think it was the first time I ever saw him like anything.

It was a good plan. I want to say that plainly. It was the best plan anybody made that winter, on either side. It went right to the end. It was what happened at the end that wasn't mine.

* * *

They went on the night of the twenty-fifth. About four hundred men, mostly Jersey Volunteers, under their lieutenant colonel, Abraham Van Buskirk, a Dutch doctor from Bergen County who'd raised a battalion of his own neighbors for the King, and a troop of the Seventeenth Light Dragoons. I went with them as the guide.

We went down the island's shore in the dark, close in, on the soft snow. It worked. It was like walking on wool. Four hundred men, and the loudest thing on the bay was the ice singing its long note under us now and then, and the breath of the horses. We crossed at the narrows below Trembly's Point and came up through the frozen marsh with the reeds crackling round our knees, and nobody heard us. At four in the morning we were in the streets of Elizabethtown, and the guard was in its beds, and the officers were in theirs.

They took about fifty prisoners. Some officers, some militiamen, and a few men who were only in the wrong house. Almost nobody fired a shot. The rebel guard came out of the houses with their hands up and their breeches in their hands. It was over in twenty minutes. It was everything Beckwith had wanted.

And then somebody set fire to the church.

* * *

I'll tell you what I saw. I've thought about it for two hundred and forty-seven years and I can't make it more than what it was.

There was a Presbyterian meeting house in the middle of the town. A big wooden building with a tall steeple, the finest building in Elizabethtown. The rebels had used it as a barracks, and before that, the Loyalists said, its minister had preached rebellion from the pulpit every Sunday since '75 and rung its bell to call out the militia. The Loyalists hated it more than any building in Jersey. Some of them had sat in its pews as boys.

When the prisoners were being collected in the street, a party of men came down the street with torches. They weren't Van Buskirk's regulars, the Volunteers. They were Loyalist militia from the island who'd come along on their own account, the way the Jersey farmers had followed Lord Stirling, men whose farms had been stripped the week before. And they went into the meeting house, and a few minutes later there was light in the windows, and then there was light coming out of the windows, and then the steeple was burning like a candle against the dark.

Van Buskirk came running. I heard him. He was shouting at them to put it out, in Dutch and English both, that he hadn't ordered it, that they were to put it out at once. Nobody did. Nobody could have, by then, with buckets and snow. And while he was shouting at them, somebody else set fire to the courthouse across the green.

I stood in the street and watched them burn. There was nothing I could do. I was a Black man with a guide's paper in a town full of armed white men with their blood up. If I'd so much as touched one of those torches they'd have thrown me in after it.

We went back across the ice in the gray light with fifty prisoners and both buildings still burning behind us, and you could see the glow of them from the middle of the bay, from Staten Island, from the Battery in the city, the people told me afterward. The rebels said it all over Jersey for the rest of the war: that the King's men had come across the ice in the night and burned a house of God. They were right. They had.

He stopped. The boards were drying in patches now, and the steam had gone off them. He'd brought his tea out with him, and he set it down on the rail.

"You said it wasn't yours," I said. "The end."

"It wasn't. I didn't light it. I didn't order it. The man who did order the raid tried to stop it, and couldn't." He looked at me. "And the road those men walked to the church that night, across the ice, through the marsh, in the dark, so quietly that nobody heard them coming, was mine. I made it. I was very proud of it." He was quiet. "I'm not asking you to forgive anybody. I'm telling you what happened. You can do something useful with a road, Ms. Barnett, and somebody else will always be waiting at the end of it with a torch. I learned that in Elizabethtown, and I've never once been able to unlearn it."

"Was there another one?" I said. "That night."

"There was. Not mine." He picked up the tea again. "The same night, another column went over the ice from the city itself, across the North River in sleighs to Paulus Hook, and then by road up to Newark. They burned the academy there. And they pulled a magistrate called Hedden out of his bed in his nightshirt and marched him off barefoot in the snow, and his wife tried to stop them, and one of them ran her through with a bayonet." He said it plainly, the way he'd said everything about that night. "Nobody I knew. Nobody I'd have trusted with a road. I only mention it because if I left it out, you'd find it, and you'd think I'd hidden it."

"I would have found it."

"I know," he said. "That's why I like you."

* * *

*February 1780*

The other one was Young's House, a week later, and that one was mine from beginning to end.

I'll tell it quickly. There was a farmhouse up in Westchester, in the middle of the Neutral Ground, at a crossroads near the Saw Mill River, called Young's, after the family. The rebels had made it an outpost, the most forward post they had on that side of the river, with a few hundred men, Continentals and militia, in and around the house. It was a thorn. Every foraging party the King's army sent up into the county that winter got news of it sent ahead by Young's, and every Loyalist farm within ten miles got visited.

I knew the house. I knew every house up there. It was eleven miles from Captain Reynolds's farm.

On the second of February, in the evening, a strong column left the old fort at the top of the island, the one the British had renamed Fort Knyphausen: a battalion of the Guards, Hessians, and the Westchester refugee horse, DeLancey's men, who knew that country nearly as well as I did and hated everybody in it, under a lieutenant colonel of the Guards called Norton. And sleighs, to carry the foot, and two small field guns. The snow in Westchester was two feet deep on the level and more in the drifts. The roads were gone. You couldn't see where they'd been.

But I knew where they'd been. Caesar's body knew. It had driven those roads under snow every winter of its life.

I sat on the front of the first sleigh, next to the driver, all that night, and I told him where the road was. Left here, where the stone wall comes down to the drift. Now straight, between the two big oaks; the road runs between them. Mind the brook; it's under there; keep to the right of the willow. Twenty miles, in the dark, in two feet of snow, with a column of men behind me who'd have been lost in the first hour without somebody who knew where the ground was under all that white.

We came out of the woods above Young's crossroads a little before nine in the morning, with the sun on the snow so bright it hurt your eyes, and the rebels at the house were cooking their breakfast.

* * *

It was over in about a quarter of an hour.

They fought. I want you to know that. They came out of the house and the barns and stood in the snow and fought, and some of them fought very well, but they had no guns that could answer ours, and DeLancey's horse came round behind them through the orchard. In a quarter of an hour fourteen of them were dead and thirty-seven wounded and more than seventy were standing in the yard with their hands up.

I have the fourteen. All of them. Where each one lay. The colors of their coats against the snow, and the snow under them. I didn't kill any of them. I'd never fired a shot. I'd only told a sleigh driver where the road was under the drifts.

And I have the seventy-six in the yard. I went down to the yard afterward, because Norton wanted somebody who knew the county to tell him who the prisoners were. Which were Continentals, which were militia, which were the local men who'd hang if the Loyalists got hold of them on their own. I walked along the line of them in the snow, slowly, with Norton's adjutant beside me writing down names.

And the ninth man in the line was Captain Joseph Reynolds.

* * *

He'd been at the house with a company of his militia. He was standing in the snow in his good blue coat with no hat, with his hands tied in front of him, and blood on his cheek from a cut over the eye, and he looked older than I remembered, and smaller, and very cold.

He knew me at once. I saw him know me. I watched it go across his face, all of it: surprise, and then disbelief, and then something I think was nearly fear, and then, last, the thing I'd seen on the first morning I woke up in that body, at noon, in his barnyard, with his account book under his arm. He was looking at his property. Six months gone, and turned up in the wrong army, with a British officer at its elbow.

"Caesar," he said.

The adjutant looked up from his paper. At me, and then at Reynolds.

"You know this man?"

Everything I had was in that moment, Ms. Barnett. I want you to see it. I could have said anything. Norton's adjutant would have written it down. *This man is a militia captain and a committeeman, sir, and he hanged a Loyalist neighbor in '77.* It wasn't true, but it would have been believed, and DeLancey's men were standing ten feet away, listening, and some of them had neighbors who'd been hanged. I could have ended him with a sentence. And he knew it. I watched him know that too.

"Yes, sir," I said. "His name is Joseph Reynolds. He's a captain of the Westchester militia, from Bedford. He farms there. He's a decent man to his neighbors." I looked at Reynolds. "He'll do for exchange. He's no use to anybody dead."

The adjutant wrote it down. *Reynolds, Jos., Capt., militia, Bedford. Exchange.*

And Reynolds looked at me, there in the snow, as if he'd never seen me before in his life. Which, I suppose, he hadn't.

"Caesar," he said again. Quieter.

"Freeman," I said. "It's Freeman now, Captain."

I walked on down the line. I didn't look back. I heard later that he was exchanged in the spring, for a Loyalist lieutenant, and went home to Bedford, to his bottom land and his account book, and lived a long time. I don't know if he ever told anyone about the man in the yard at Young's. I don't know if he ever thought of it again. I've thought of it a great deal.

It was nearly four. The sun had come right round onto the porch, and the boards had dried, and steam came off the rail where it was still wet in the shade.

I'd stopped writing a while back. I realized it when I looked down and saw that my pen had been lying across the page, on the word *Reynolds*, for I didn't know how long.

"Why?" I said. "He owned you. You could have..." I stopped.

"I could have," he said. "Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

He thought about it, honestly, the way he thinks about everything I ask him, as if it's the first time anyone's ever asked.

"Because I'd spent six months being something he'd paid for," he said. "And I'd walked thirty miles in the dark so that I'd never have to be that again. And the moment that man in the yard was in my power, I found I didn't want to be the kind of thing that does what it likes with a man in its power." He picked up his cup, which was empty, and looked into it. "It wasn't goodness. I'd like to say it was. It was pride. I wanted him to know, for the rest of his life, that I'd had him in my hand and opened it." He set the cup down. "Write down that it was pride. You'll make it something nicer if you don't."

I wrote it down. *Pride.* And then, because I'm a reporter and not a saint, and because I couldn't help it, I wrote underneath it, very small, where he couldn't read it from his side of the table:

*Not only.*
