## Chapter 18: The Vulture

*September 1780*

I worked out whose letters they were on the seventeenth of September, from a piece of paper about a house.

André hadn't told me. He'd kept his own rule to the end: every messenger ignorant. But that summer I'd carried a great many of his papers, and stood by a great many of his tables, and I couldn't help what I saw, any more than I could help breathing. And on the sixteenth Colonel Robinson went up the Hudson on a sloop of war called the *Vulture*, with a letter in his pocket under a flag of truce, to an American general. About his house.

I've told you about Robinson's house. The fine house in the Highlands, up the river, that he'd lived in like a duke before the war, among his tenants. The rebels had taken it when he wouldn't swear to them. And now an American general was living in it, because it stood right across the river from the great fortress at West Point, and the general had made it his headquarters when he took command of the fort in August.

Robinson's letter asked that general, very politely, for a meeting under a flag, to discuss his confiscated property.

Robinson didn't care about his property that week. I knew Robinson. I'd watched him for a year. He'd been nervous for days, in the way a careful man is nervous when he's doing something he's not built for. He'd been going in and out of André's office with the door shut. He'd stopped talking to me about the country. And now he was going up the river, in person, at his age, to talk about a house he'd been trying to get back for four years through lawyers, to the one man in America who happened to be living in it, and who happened to command West Point.

I knew whose letters they were. I knew what was being sold. And I knew, from the way André had been all that month, that it was nearly done.

General Benedict Arnold. Gustavus. Mr. Moore. Those were the names he signed himself. He'd been writing to André for more than a year, and André was going up the river to buy West Point from him, and the war was nearly over.

* * *

I went to André that night.

I shouldn't have. It wasn't my place. A guide doesn't knock on the Adjutant General's door at ten at night. But we had our secret, the two of us, and I think he'd have been more surprised if I hadn't come.

He was at his long table under the window, alone, with one candle and a great many papers, and he looked up when I came in and didn't seem surprised at all. He looked tired. It was the only time I ever saw him tired. He was writing the last canto of his poem about Wayne and the cows, of all things, for Rivington to print, and there was a pile of crossed-out verses at his elbow.

"Freeman," he said. "You've guessed."

"Yes, sir."

"I thought you might. I told the General you would." He put the pen down. "Sit down. No, sit. Nobody's going to come in."

I sat. In the other chair, across the table from him, in the Adjutant General's office at headquarters, at ten at night. I don't think I've told you how strange that was. In that body, in that city, in that whole year, it was the only chair anybody ever offered me.

"You want to tell me something," he said.

* * *

I told him what I knew about the Neutral Ground.

He knew some of it. Everybody at headquarters knew some of it. But he'd never been there, not the way I had, on foot, at night, on the roads. He'd ridden through it with escorts of dragoons. He'd never been alone in it, and I had, and I tried to tell him what that meant.

"There are men on every road between the river and the Sound, sir," I said. "Ours and theirs and nobody's. DeLancey's Cowboys on the south side. The rebel militia on the north. And bands of men who belong to neither, who'll stop anybody on a horse with a good coat and take the coat and the horse and whatever's in his pockets, and if they find anything worth money on him, they'll sell it, and him, to whichever side pays. They don't care whose pass you carry. Half of them can't read it." I leaned forward. "If you go up the river, sir, come back down it. On the ship. In your own coat. Whatever happens. Whatever he says to you."

He looked at me for a long moment.

"That's the arrangement," he said. "I'm to go up to the *Vulture*, and meet him under a flag, by water, and come back the same way. The General was very clear about it. So was I." He smiled. "I'm a staff officer, Freeman. Not a spy. I've no intention of creeping about Westchester in a farmer's hat."

"He'll want you to."

"Who will?"

"General Arnold, sir." It was the first time either of us had said the name aloud. It sat in the room between us like a third person. "He's selling his country for money. He'll be frightened. A man selling his country is frightened all the time, every minute, from the first letter to the last. He'll want the meeting somewhere he controls. On his side of the river. In a house. And when it's done, if anything's gone wrong, if the ship's moved, if there's a shot fired, if anybody's seen you, he won't want you anywhere near him. He'll want you gone the fastest way he can find. And the fastest way won't be the safe way. It'll be a horse, and a pass in his own hand, and a civilian coat over your uniform so nobody stops you." I stopped. "And if they do stop you, sir, in a civilian coat, behind their lines, with papers, you're not a staff officer any more. You're a spy. And they'll hang you."

He didn't say anything for a while. He picked up a crossed-out verse and looked at it and put it down again.

"You've thought about this a great deal," he said.

"Yes, sir. I've thought about very little else since I saw Colonel Robinson's letter about the house."

"And what would you have me do?"

"Take me with you," I said. "If you go ashore. If anything happens. If it has to be the land road. Take me. I know every road in that county. I know which farm has a dog and which ford is deep and which tavern keeper sells news. I can get you from the river to our lines in a night, by roads nobody watches, and nobody looks twice at a gentleman's servant. Nobody ever looks at me at all."

* * *

He thought about it. I'll always be grateful for that. He didn't wave it away. He sat there with the candle between us and thought about it honestly, the way he thought about a puzzle, turning it over.

Then he shook his head.

"No," he said, gently. "Thank you. No."

"Sir..."

"Two reasons." He held up one finger. "First, the arrangement. Arnold's been told one person. One officer, coming up under a flag. If I come ashore with a servant he doesn't know, he'll think he's been betrayed, and the whole thing will stop dead, and a year's work with it. He's frightened, as you say. A frightened man sees a stranger and runs." He held up a second finger. "And second, I'm coming back by water. On the *Vulture*. In my own coat. I've given the General my word. So there won't be a land road, and I shan't need a guide." He smiled. "You've been too long in the Neutral Ground, Freeman. Everything looks like an ambush to you."

"Everything is an ambush, sir. In the Neutral Ground."

He laughed. Then he stopped laughing and looked at me in the candlelight with his dark bright eyes, and said something I've never forgotten.

"I know what you're afraid of," he said. "You're afraid I'll be too clever. That I'll see a quicker way and take it, because I always do. You're right to be afraid of that. I'm afraid of it too." He picked up his pen. "So I'll make you a promise, since you've been honest with me. If I have to come back by land, I'll come back in my own coat. Whatever Arnold says."

He stopped. Freda had stopped eating pie some time ago. She was sitting with both arms wrapped round her knees, on the kitchen chair, with her chin on them.

"He promised," she said.

"He promised," he said. "And he meant it. He always meant it."

Freda looked at him. Then at me. Then she put her face down on her knees for a moment, and brought it up again.

"I looked it up," she said. "Last night. After Mom said it was September. I'm sorry. I couldn't not." Her voice wobbled, and she steadied it. "He wore a different coat."

"Yes," he said gently. "He did."

* * *

*September 1780*

I should go back a week. The first attempt was on the eleventh, at Dobbs Ferry, before Robinson's letter about the house. André and Robinson went up by boat to meet Arnold on the river, and Arnold came down by barge from his side, and the British gunboats on the river, who hadn't been told, saw a rebel barge on the water and fired on it, and Arnold turned round and went home. That was nearly the end of it, right there. A gunboat lieutenant doing his duty.

They tried again. Arnold wrote, under his false name, that he'd be on the river on the night of the twentieth. Robinson went up on the *Vulture* on the sixteenth with his letter about the house, and the *Vulture* anchored off a long low point of land on the east shore called Teller's Point, a few miles below King's Ferry, and waited.

And on the morning of the twentieth, André went up to join it.

I went down to the slip to see him off. I had no business there. He saw me, standing at the back of the little crowd of boatmen and porters, and lifted his hand to me, quite openly, the way he'd lift it to Robinson or to the General, and I lifted mine.

He was in his uniform. The scarlet coat, and the gold lace, and the gorget at his throat. Under his boat cloak, but in it. I looked very carefully, because I'd been afraid of exactly that. He had nothing else with him but a small valise. No other coat. I let out a breath I'd been holding since the seventeenth.

He'd said he'd come back in his own coat, and there wasn't another coat in that boat to put on.

* * *

I was sent to the lines that night, with a party of DeLancey's horse, to the advanced posts below the Neutral Ground, on the east side of the river, to wait for him in case he came back by land after all. André had arranged it, in spite of what he'd said, which tells you that he'd thought about what I'd told him more than he'd let me see. If he came down by road, we were to meet him at the edge of the Neutral Ground and bring him in. If he came by water, we'd never see him, and that would be best of all.

My orders, from Beckwith, through the officer of DeLancey's party, were to wait at the lines, and on no account go forward into the Neutral Ground on my own. Two days. Then come home.

I waited.

On the night of the twentieth, nothing. On the night of the twenty-first, nothing. The river was very still. Nothing moved on it that we could see, from the heights, but the moon on the water and a fishing boat or two.

And at first light on the twenty-second, very far up the river, nearly thirty miles off, so faint I felt it in the ground before I heard it, somebody began firing a cannon.

He stopped. He put his hands flat on the kitchen table, on either side of his cup, and sat looking at them.

Nobody said anything. The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, the hawk had gone from the field.

"That's enough for this morning," he said at last. "We'll go on after lunch. I'd like to walk down the road a little way first, if you two don't mind. I'd like to see the car again." He got up, with his hand on the edge of the table. "It's a good thing to see. A car on a road. In the sun."

He went out. Through the window I watched him go slowly down the porch steps with his hand on the rail and across the gravel and out to the end of the drive, where the car was, and stand there in the hot white sun beside it, with his hand resting on the roof, looking down the long straight gravel road toward the south.

Freda came and stood beside me at the window.

"He's waiting for somebody," she said.

"No," I said. "I think he's waiting for somebody who didn't come."
