## Chapter 7: The Person Who Wasn't Laughing

I spent that week on Gerald Whitcombe, and I did it the way I used to, in the newsroom, when I still had a desk and a phone with my name on it. Records first. People later. People last of all, if you can manage it, because a person can only be asked a question once for the first time.

It's surprising how much of a life is public, if you know which counter to stand at.

The stone house outside Middleburg was in his name, and had been for thirty-one years. Forty-two acres, not forty, the county said, with a barn and a run-in shed and a pond, assessed at a figure that made me put my pen down for a moment. He'd bought it the year he became head of conservation at Carrow House. He owned no other property in Virginia or the District or Maryland. He'd never been sued, never been arrested, never been in a newspaper for anything but his talks and his retirement and a letter to the editor of the Middleburg paper, nine years ago, about a proposal to widen a road. He was against it. The road had not been widened.

He'd been married for forty-four years to a woman called Margaret, who taught piano and died eleven years ago, of a stroke, in the spring. Her obituary was still up on the funeral home's website, with a photograph of a gray-haired woman at a piano laughing at somebody off to one side. It said that she was survived by her husband, Gerald, *her partner in all things,* and by their daughter, Claire, of Loudoun County, and two grandsons. It said that in lieu of flowers the family asked for donations to a horse rescue in Purcellville.

I read it twice. I made a note of the daughter, because I make a note of everybody. Then I moved on to the board of the historical society and the library foundation, and his talk, every spring, on the enemies of paper. Light. Damp. Acid. The human hand.

By Wednesday night I had six pages on Gerald Whitcombe and I still had exactly what I'd had on Monday, which was an old woman's memory of a laugh.

* * *

He made eggs, that Thursday.

It was cooler, the first cool morning in two weeks, with rain in the night and the yard still dripping, and he'd lit the stove more for comfort than for need. He made the eggs the way he'd made them in the spring, when the bakery in town was shut and we'd had nothing else: scrambled very slowly in a pan with a lot of butter, stirred with the back of a fork, and toast from the bakery bread, and the tea in the brown pot. I said the date into the recorders. *Session thirty-two, Thursday, June third.* Then he put a plate in front of me and sat down across the kitchen table with his own, and looked at me, and waited.

He always knew when I had something. I've never worked out how. I've watched my own face in the car mirror afterward and it doesn't look like anything.

"I've got a laugh," I said. "And nothing else."

"Tell me."

So I told him. The six pages. The house, the acres, the road he didn't want widened. The talk every spring. The way the audience laughed at his jokes. And Mrs. Dunleavy, in her son's front room in Frederick, with her eyes closed and her hands on the composition book, saying *That's him* before the laugh was even finished.

"I believe her," I said. "I think she's right. But I can't print a laugh. I can't take a laugh to a lawyer. I can't even take it to Whitcombe, because if I drive out to Middleburg and knock on his door and say, *Mr. Whitcombe, a woman heard you laugh on a staircase six years ago,* he's going to laugh at me, the same laugh, and close the door, and call Carrow House's lawyers, who I already know, all of them, by their first names. And then the one thing I've got that he doesn't know I've got, I won't have anymore."

He ate some of his eggs. He took his time over it.

"You're right not to go," he said.

"I know I'm right. That's not the problem."

"No." He put his fork down. "Tell me about the laugh."

"I just did."

"No. You told me about Mrs. Dunleavy. Tell me about the laugh. The one in the video."

I thought about it. I'd watched that video eleven times. I'd watched the laugh at thirty-one minutes more often than that.

"It's big," I said. "Too big for the room. He puts his head back. It's the kind of laugh that tells everybody else in the room that they can laugh now too. And they do." I hesitated. "It's a laugh that's used to people laughing with it."

"Yes," he said. "A man who laughs like that is used to being believed." He picked up his tea. "Don't give him the chance to be."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning you're right. Leave him alone. He's the strongest thing in your whole story. He's been standing at lecterns for forty years with people laughing in the right places. He'll be very good at your questions, because he's very good at everybody's questions. You'll come home with nothing but the sound of him being charming, and so will anybody you send." He set the cup down. "So don't go to the man who was laughing, Ms. Barnett. Find the one who wasn't."

* * *

I didn't understand him for a moment. Then I did, and I felt it go down my arms, the way it used to in the newsroom.

"The car," I said.

"The car."

"Somebody waited in the car."

"Somebody sat in an alley behind a dry cleaner's for twenty-seven minutes," he said, "with the engine running, on a Wednesday morning, while two men went up and down the stairs of a building with a woman on the second floor who wrote down every footstep. They didn't get out. They didn't help carry anything. They sat there." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "Think about those twenty-seven minutes from inside the car. It's the longest half hour of that person's life. They can hear nothing. They can see the back door and the alley mouth and nothing else. Every car that goes past the end of the alley is the police. Every minute the door doesn't open is a minute something's gone wrong upstairs." He looked at me. "The man upstairs was laughing. The person in the car wasn't. I'd bet you your coat on it."

"You'd lose the coat. I'd keep you on the bet just to win it."

"That's a yes," he said, and went back to his eggs.

I sat there with my fork in my hand.

"Whoever it was," I said slowly, "is the weak place. Not him. He's done this sort of thing all his life, probably, a version of it. Talking. Making people comfortable. Whoever was in that car isn't like that, or they'd have been upstairs carrying boxes. They were the one he trusted not to talk, and not to ask, and to sit still." I thought about it. "And they've been carrying those twenty-seven minutes ever since."

"Six years," he said. "Every time they hear an engine idle." He spread butter on a piece of toast, right to the edges, the way he did everything. "And when you find them, don't ask them what they did. They've told themselves what they did a thousand times, at night, and they've got it smooth. Ask them how long they waited."

"Why?"

"Because nobody's ever asked them that. And they know the answer to the minute."

* * *

I wrote it all down. Then I sat and looked at the page.

"I don't know who it was," I said. "That's the trouble. I've got six pages and there's nobody in them. A dead wife. A historical society. The man's never even had a parking ticket."

"Who drives a man into the city?" he said.

I looked at him.

"I'm not going to do it for you," he said, mildly. "I've had my turn this year. Just ask yourself the question properly. Not who would help him. Lots of people would help him. Who'd drive him? Who'd sit in a car for him, in an alley, and not ask?" He finished the toast. "How old was he? Six years ago?"

"Seventy-three."

"And he lives an hour and a half out, in the country, on forty acres. Does a man of seventy-three who lives on forty acres in the hunt country drive himself into the middle of Washington on a weekday morning?"

I didn't answer. He didn't need me to.

"Eat your eggs," he said. "They're getting cold, and I don't make them for everybody."
