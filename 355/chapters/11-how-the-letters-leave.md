## Chapter 11: How the Letters Leave

He had Freda's letter on the kitchen table when I came in, under the sugar bowl, folded in thirds the way she folds them. He didn't mention it. He made the tea and put the cups down and sat, and I said the date into the recorders, *session thirty-four, Thursday, June seventeenth*, and then he looked at me over the top of his cup with an expression I'd never seen on him before, and it took me a moment to understand that it was pride.

"I had a letter," he said.

"I know."

"She says it was her."

"It was her."

"She says," he said, and unfolded it, and read off it, though I'm certain he knew it by heart, "*It was at the end of the video from three years ago, because that is when the camera person forgets, which I said. Mom says to tell you it is not proof. I know it is not proof. It is a fleece.*" He folded it up again, carefully, and put it back under the sugar bowl. "She'll do," he said. "She'll more than do."

"She's been unbearable all week."

"Good. She's earned a week." He drank some tea. "And you haven't gone."

"No. I wanted to ask you how."

He thought about it. Outside it was the first real summer day, clear and hot and very still, and the bees were loud in the clover at the edge of the yard.

"Go where she works," he said. "Not where she lives. People are braver at their work. Their hands know what they're doing, even when the rest of them doesn't. And don't go as a reporter. Go as a woman who wants to ask her one question." He set his cup down. "And not this week. Let her have one more week of not knowing anybody's looking. You'll need her to have had it."

I didn't understand that. I wrote it down anyway.

"Now," he said. "Spring. Beckwith had lost his ice, and he wanted something to show for it."

* * *

*March 1780*

When the ice went out of the harbor, the letters started moving again. We knew because the deserters told us.

Not in so many words. But every deserter who came in from Jersey that spring was questioned at headquarters, and some of them had been servants to rebel officers, and grooms and cooks, and they'd heard things at their officers' tables. And what they'd heard, more than once, was that General Washington knew what was happening in New York. Which regiments had gone south with Clinton and which were still in the city. How many cords of wood were left in the yards. When a fleet was fitting out. Not quickly. Weeks late, sometimes. But he knew.

Beckwith had me in his office in the second week of March, with the window open for the first time since December and the noise of the slips coming in with the smell of the river, and he was angry. Not with me. He was angry the way a man is when he's done everything right and it hasn't worked.

"Hill finds nothing," he said. "Eight months at that table. He's opened four thousand letters. He's turned out every basket on Long Island. He's made half the farmers in Kings County stand in their stockings. Nothing." He pushed a paper across the table at me, face down; I suppose he'd forgotten I couldn't read. "We've had the letters held to the fire. We've had them washed with every acid the surgeons have got. Nothing comes up. They're letters about butter. About cousins. About the price of salt. And Washington is still getting his news."

"Then it isn't in the letters, sir."

"I've thought of that," he said, sharply.

"Yes, sir. I meant something else." I thought about how to say it. "Mr. Hill looks at what a man's carrying. He opens it and reads it. If it says nothing, he gives it back. So whatever's carrying the news has got to be something a man can carry openly, that Mr. Hill will look at and give straight back. Something that says nothing, on its face." I paused. "There's nothing that says less than a blank sheet of paper."

Beckwith looked at me.

"You've had letters held to the fire, sir," I said, "and nothing came up. So it's an ink that doesn't answer to fire. Or to the surgeons' acids. Only to its own key. A man would need to carry a little bottle of the key with him, wherever the letter was going. And he'd need to write on paper that didn't look written on." I stopped. "If I were doing it, I'd write on a clean sheet, and put the sheet in the middle of a quire of clean sheets, and carry the quire to the country as if I'd bought it for somebody, in a basket, with the tea and sugar. And Mr. Hill would riffle through it and see twenty-four sheets of good paper, and put it back in the basket."

There was a long silence. Somewhere down at the slips a man was shouting at a horse.

"And how," Beckwith said, slowly, "would the man at the other end know which of the twenty-four?"

"He'd know because they'd agreed it. The fourth sheet. The one with a pinprick in the corner. The one folded a certain way." I shrugged. "Or he wouldn't, and he'd have to try every sheet with his bottle. Which would waste a great deal of his bottle."

I didn't know then, of course, how right that was. I've read their letters since. A good many of them, Ms. Barnett, are two frightened men complaining to each other that they've run out of the key, and can't read what's been sent, and can't get more. One of them wrote, once, that he'd sent three blank sheets instead of one, for fear of sending the wrong one. I laughed out loud in a library when I read that. A librarian asked me to leave.

* * *

So I went looking for paper.

It's a strange thing to hunt, a sheet of blank paper. But paper wasn't cheap in New York in 1780. Most of it came from England, across the sea, in war time, and it cost a good deal, and people who bought it were people who wrote. Merchants. Clerks. Officers. Lawyers. Not farmers. Not carters. A Long Island farmer might buy a few sheets in a year, to write to a cousin or settle a debt. He didn't buy it by the quire.

There were perhaps a dozen shops in the city that sold good writing paper. Stationers. Printers. A few dry goods merchants who kept it among the cloth and the tea. I couldn't go into any of them and ask who'd been buying paper, of course. A Black man asking a merchant about his customers would have been thrown out at best, and reported at worst, and the man I was looking for would have heard about it by supper. But I didn't need to ask. I only needed to stand outside, at the right hour, holding somebody's horse, or carrying somebody's parcel, or sitting on the step of the next house with my hat over my eyes. Nobody looks at a Black man sitting on a step in New York. I'd learned that much.

I watched what came out of those shops, and where it went, for three weeks.

And I watched the ferry. That was the end of every road east. Everything that went to Long Island went over the ferry, and everything that went over the ferry went past me, sooner or later, if I stood at the top of the stairs long enough. I stood there a great deal, that March. The widow had her cart back at the top of the stairs as soon as the ice was gone, with her eggs and her butter and her little iron pot of coals for the cold mornings. I bought eggs from her twice a week, as I had before, and put them inside my shirt, and stood and watched the people waiting for the boat. She had eleven, one of those mornings. I remember thinking she'd had a slow week. I didn't buy any that day. I had enough.

* * *

The carter came on a Tuesday.

He wasn't anybody. That was what caught me. A Long Island man, about thirty, broad and quiet, with a good wagon and a good gray horse, who carted goods between the city and the north shore of the Island for anybody who'd pay him, every week or two. Butter and cheese and firewood in. Rum and sugar and nutmeg and cloth out. There were twenty men doing the same thing. He was a carpenter by trade, the boatmen said, a joiner, and cart work paid better in a war. I'd seen him a dozen times and never once thought about him.

But that Tuesday I'd seen him come out of a dry goods shop, the week before, with his hands full, and put a parcel in his wagon. And the parcel was the right size and the right shape and the right weight for a quire of good paper, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. And now here he was at the ferry with his wagon, and the parcel was in among the sugar, and when Hill's men turned his load over at the Brooklyn landing, they found the parcel and opened it, and riffled through twenty-four sheets of good clean writing paper, and asked him what a carter wanted with all that, and he said it was for a gentleman in Setauket who'd asked him to get it, and had paid for it, and would want it.

And they gave it back to him. Of course they did. It was paper.

Setauket. A village of a few hundred people on the north shore of Long Island, fifty-five miles from the city, a long day's ride, with a harbor that faced straight across the Sound at Connecticut. Where, as it happened, the rebels had a whaleboat officer called Brewster who came over in the dark with armed crews to raid the Loyalist farms and carry off whatever he could, and who'd been born there, and was known to every soul in the place.

It wasn't proof. It was a parcel. But I'd watched the ferry for eight months by then, and I knew every carter on that road, and none of the others had ever carried paper out to the country. Not once.

* * *

I went to Beckwith with it. Not with the carter's face. With the road.

I drew it for him on his own map with my finger, since I couldn't be seen to write. A man in the city, a man of business, who could buy good paper without anyone wondering, writes his news in an ink that only answers to its own key, on one sheet of a quire. A carter takes the quire out with his load, over the ferry, past Mr. Hill, along the north shore, fifty-five miles, to Setauket. Somebody in Setauket opens it, and finds the right sheet, and reads it, and adds his own news of the Island. And on an agreed night a whaleboat comes over from Connecticut, twenty miles, in the dark, and takes it back across the Sound, and a dragoon rides it to Washington.

"Five men," I said. "Perhaps six. The man in the city. The carter. The man in Setauket. The boat. The officer in Connecticut who gets it at the other end. And the man in the city has never once set foot outside it, so far as anyone knows. He's not on the road. He's never searched. He's a gentleman. He goes about his business. He keeps a shop, or a counting house, or a desk, and he listens, and he writes it down at night."

Beckwith was silent for a long time, looking at my finger on his map.

"And his name?" he said, at last.

"I don't know it, sir."

"The carter's?"

I'd known the question was coming. I'd thought about it, walking up from the slips, all the way to his door. If I gave him the carter's face, he'd have the carter taken at the ferry the next time he came over, and searched to the skin, and the quire held to every flame in the city, and nothing would come up, because the ink only answered to its own key. And the carter would say the paper was for a gentleman in Setauket, which was true. And they'd let him go, eventually, or hang him on suspicion, and the man in the city would hear of it before the day was out, and cut the road, and start another one I didn't know about.

You don't pull up the first root you find, Ms. Barnett. You find out where it goes.

"I'd know his horse anywhere, sir," I said. "I don't know his name. And I don't think you want him yet. If you take him, they'll only find another carter. I think you want the road to feel watched. Every mile of it." I took my finger off the map. "I think you want them frightened."

He looked up at me then, the long cool look, and I saw him think about all the reasons a man in my position might not want to give him a name. Then I saw him decide that the one I'd given was the true one, which it was. Mostly.

"Frightened," Beckwith said. He picked up his pen. "Yes. I think I can frighten Setauket."

He stopped and looked out of the window. The bees were still going in the clover. A car went by on the gravel road, slowly, raising a long plume of white dust that hung in the still air and drifted across the field and settled.

"Mostly?" I said.

He turned back.

"You said it was the true reason. Mostly."

He smiled a little.

"I didn't want to give anybody a face," he said. "Not yet. I'd seen what happened to faces in that city. I'd given Robinson one at Young's House, the year's only face, and it had been the man who owned me, and I'd spent it on getting him exchanged." He turned his cup on the table, a quarter turn. "The carter whistled when he drove, and he gave his horse half his bread. I wanted to be sure. That's all. I wanted to be quite sure, before I gave anybody a face."

"And were you? Sure?"

"Oh, yes," he said. "He was one of them. He rode that road for them for two more years. Nobody ever took him." He looked at me. "And I never gave anybody his face, Ms. Barnett. Not that spring and not ever. You can write that down too. Next week I'll tell you what I did instead."

I wrote it. *Never gave the carter's face.* And then I sat looking at what I'd written, and at the word *frightened* a few lines above it, and I understood, or thought I did, that the hunt he was about to tell me about wasn't going to be the kind where somebody gets caught.
