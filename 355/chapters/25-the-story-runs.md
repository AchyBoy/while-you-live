## Chapter 25: The Story Runs

I called my old editor on the Monday after the valley. The first of August.

Her name is Ruth Okafor. She was the one who'd hired me, and the one who'd stood in the doorway of the conference room six years ago while the lawyers talked, with her arms folded, and said nothing, and the one who'd handed me the box at the end. She was managing editor now. I'd seen her name on the masthead every Sunday for six years and never once called the number underneath it.

She picked up on the second ring.

"Iola," she said. Not surprised. Wary. The way you'd say the name of somebody who'd been dead and was now apparently on the phone.

"He's alive," I said. "Bookbinder. My source. I found him. I've sat in his kitchen. He's got the papers, all of them, the originals he copied, in two boxes, and he wants to go on the record. With his name. Under mine."

There was a long silence on the line. I could hear the newsroom behind her, faintly, the way you hear the sea in a shell. Phones. Somebody laughing.

"Come in," Ruth said. "Today. Bring nothing. We'll talk in my office with the door shut." She paused. "And Iola. Don't tell anybody else. Not one person."

"I've told one person," I said. "He's eighty and he lives in Pennsylvania and he's never going to tell anyone anything."

"Well," Ruth said, after a moment. "That's a start."

* * *

It took two weeks. That's very fast, for a story like that. It should have taken two months. It would have, except for one thing Claire found, on the second day.

Carrow House had a fall sale coming. *Autograph Letters, Manuscripts and Historical Documents*, the same heavy cream catalog with the brass doors on the cover. It was already printed. It was on their website. And in it, on page forty-one, there was a letter, signed by a general from the Revolutionary War, estimated at forty to sixty thousand dollars, that was described, word for word, as a letter my source had been asked to repair, eight years ago, with damage that was new. It was in his copies. It was in the boxes.

They were selling it again.

"Then we haven't got two months," Ruth said, when I showed her. "We've got until the sale."

* * *

The boxes came out of the barn in Middleburg on the third of August.

Claire went to her father. She went alone, and she told me afterward that she'd sat at his kitchen table, in the stone house, the way he'd sat with her at the kitchen table six years before, and told him that a woman had come to her barn and asked how long she'd waited, and that she'd answered, and that she'd driven the woman over the mountains to the valley, and that it was done.

"What did he say?" I asked her.

"Nothing, for a long time," Claire said. "Then he laughed." She was quiet. "Not the big one. A small one. And then he cried. I've never seen him cry. Not when Mom died. Not ever." She looked at her hands. "And then he got up and went out to the barn and climbed up the ladder to the loft himself. At seventy-nine. I couldn't stop him. He brought the first box down himself, on his shoulder, one rung at a time. He said he'd carried it up, and he'd carry it down."

I met him once. Only once. On the fourth, at the stone house, because he'd asked to see me, and Claire said I didn't have to, and I went anyway.

He was smaller than in the video. He sat in a wing chair by a cold fireplace in a room full of books, in a cardigan, with a rug over his knees in August, and he didn't get up when I came in, and he didn't laugh. He looked at me for a long time, and then he said the thing I'd known since the porch that he'd say, and that I'd been afraid of, because I didn't know what I'd do with it.

"I told my daughter," he said, "that reporters always land on their feet." He looked at the cold fireplace. "I've been wrong about a great many things in my life, Ms. Barnett. I've been wrong about paper. I've been wrong about people. I was very wrong about that." He looked back at me. "And I repaired some of those letters. Myself. Before he ever did. Over many years. I'd like you to know that from me, and not from a box."

I didn't say anything. I didn't trust myself to.

"Print it," Gerald Whitcombe said. "Print all of it. My name too. I'm seventy-nine. I'd rather it was true than kind."

* * *

The paper sent the letters to two independent document examiners, one in New York and one in London, and they came back in eight days, both of them, and said what my source had said seven years before. New damage. Old paper. Modern ink, very cleverly aged, in places that had been made to look like repairs. The lawyers read everything three times. Carrow House was given forty-eight hours to respond, by email and by courier, and didn't, and then, at five minutes to the deadline, sent a statement saying they were reviewing their consignment procedures and had withdrawn three lots from the fall sale *out of an abundance of caution*.

The story ran on Sunday, the fifteenth of August, on the front page, above the fold.

Two bylines. Mine first. His second. His real name, which I'd never written in eight months and six years, in black type on newsprint on every doorstep in the city, under mine.

And on page two, in a box, a short note from the editors. It said that six years before, the paper had published a correction to a story by Iola Barnett about forged documents at Carrow House. It said that the correction had been wrong. It said that the paper regretted it, and apologized to Ms. Barnett and to her source.

It was eleven lines long. The correction, six years ago, had been longer than some of my stories. I counted the lines in the apology twice, at the kitchen table, at six in the morning, in my bathrobe, with the paper spread out in front of me and a cup of coffee going cold at my elbow.

Eleven.

I didn't know whether to laugh or cry, so I did both, for a while, by myself, before Freda woke up.

* * *

Freda took a copy to Mrs. Dunleavy. She asked to. She took the bus to Frederick on the Monday, with the paper in a plastic sleeve so it wouldn't get creased, and sat in Kevin Dunleavy's front room while his mother read the whole thing, every word, with a magnifying glass, sitting very upright in her chair.

"She read it twice," Freda reported that night. "Then she said, *I told Petrakis that man upstairs was up to something. He never listened.* Then she made me take a cookie." Freda paused. "Then she asked if Mr. Hale was the one in the story who figured out the car. Because she'd liked him on the tape. I said yes. She said, *Tell him from me, he's got a good ear for stairs.*"

Petrakis called on the Tuesday, from his daughter's house in Silver Spring, with the television on. He said he'd read it. He said he'd always known that tenant was a good tenant, except the rent. He said, "And the cat? The cat is all right?" and I told him about the family with two little girls, and he was quiet for a moment, and then he said, "Good. Good. Stupid cat. Nice cat," and hung up.

I got eleven hundred emails that week. Most of them were kind. Some of them were from people who'd written very unkind things about me six years before, and wanted me to know they'd been wrong, which was more than most people do. Three of them were offers of work. Real work. I didn't answer any of them yet.

Bookbinder called on the Sunday night, from the valley, after it ran. He didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, "They spelled it right," and I said I'd checked, three times, and he laughed, a small, startled laugh, as if he'd forgotten he could.

* * *

And on the Monday, the regular money came. The six thousand, for the Thursday before, as it had come every Monday since January.

But this time something was wrong with it.

I wouldn't have noticed, a year ago. I'd have looked at the amount and closed the app. But I'd spent a year with a man who noticed eleven eggs instead of twelve, and I'd learned to look at the edges of things. And at the bottom of the deposit, where the bank shows you who sent it, where for nine months it had said only the name of the law firm, *Aldine, Mercer and Voss*, and once, in May, the name of a bank in another city, there was a longer line than usual. Somebody, somewhere, in some office, had made a mistake, and sent it from the wrong account, and the bank had printed the whole thing.

*ORIG: TRUSTEES OF THE HALLORAN FUND (EST. 1938) / HARTFORD CT / FBO A M & V CLIENT ACCT.*

I sat and looked at it for a long time.

Then I did what I do. I looked it up. And it was real. The Halloran Fund was a charitable trust, registered in Connecticut, established in 1938. Its purpose, according to the one filing I could find, a single page from the 1950s scanned into the state's archive, was *the support of historical inquiry and the preservation of testimony*. Its trustees were a bank in Hartford. It had no website. It had never, as far as I could find, given a single grant to anybody.

Except me. For ten months.

1938. Eighty-nine years ago. Before the war.

I didn't know what it meant. I still don't. But it was real, and it held up, and it was the first true thing I'd ever learned about the man who sent me the cards, apart from the shaking in his hand. Somebody, eighty-nine years ago, had set up a fund to preserve testimony. And the testimony it had been waiting for, all that time, was being told on a porch in Adams County, Pennsylvania, every Thursday, to me.

I wrote it in my notebook. *Halloran Fund. Est. 1938. Hartford. "Preservation of testimony."* And underneath, because it was the only honest thing I could think of: *Waiting since before the war. For what?*

* * *

And one more thing, that week, because I'd promised myself.

On the Wednesday, the eighteenth, I spent the afternoon looking for a line in a box in a library. The way he'd told me to. *Find it.*

The Clinton Papers are at the William L. Clements Library, at the University of Michigan. Hundreds of volumes of them. Every letter, every report, every scrap of paper that crossed Sir Henry Clinton's desk in the whole war, bound into big books and numbered. I couldn't go to Michigan. But the library has a guide, a long, dry, wonderful list, volume by volume, of what's in each one, written by archivists over many years. I read it for four hours. Volume after volume. Spies, deserters, returns of troops, complaints about firewood.

And there, in volume eighty-seven, among the papers for the last days of February and the first days of March 1780, in the plain flat language of an archivist cataloguing a box, there was a line.

*British deserter caught and turned in by three African Americans.*

That's all. Three of them, no names, in a line in a guide to a box in a library in Michigan. The archivist had used a modern word, the polite word of our own time, for whatever word the officer had written by his lantern at Kingsbridge on a cold night in 1780. But it was there.

He'd told me in June. I'd found it in August. It had taken me four hours, and I'd known exactly what I was looking for, and where.

I turned to the back of my notebook, to the last page. The one I write on only when he tells me something before I find it. And I wrote it down, and dated it, the way I do.

Then I closed the notebook, and sat at the kitchen table in the late afternoon with my hand flat on the cover. And thought about a big good-tempered wagoner called Isaac, who'd bought a hat with his shilling, and worn it till he died.
