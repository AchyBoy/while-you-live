## Chapter 6: Five Deserters

*January 1780*

You've never walked on the sea. I had, by then, once or twice, in other lives, on the Baltic in a bad year. It doesn't get easier. Every part of you knows that there's forty feet of black water under your boots, moving, and that the only thing between you and it is something that was water itself last week. The body doesn't believe the ice. It never does. It walks on it the way you'd walk on the back of a sleeping animal.

The wood sleigh took me across from the city at first light, behind two horses with sacking tied over their hooves for grip. Five miles of white, flat as a table, with the sun coming up red on the left and the Jersey hills on the right, and the little black shape of Staten Island ahead of us getting bigger very slowly. The driver was a Loyalist from Long Island who talked the whole way about his cousin, who'd fallen through near Bedloe's Island the week before and been pulled out by his hair. Every so often the ice under us made a noise. Not a crack. A long, low, singing note, like somebody running a wet finger round the rim of a glass the size of the harbor, that started somewhere far off and ran toward us and under us and away. The horses flattened their ears every time. So did I.

"Tide," the driver said, the first time. "Just the tide, lifting it." He said it every time after that too. I think he said it for himself.

* * *

The British held Staten Island under a brigadier called Thomas Stirling, a Scotsman, a good plain soldier who'd been in America since the beginning and didn't trust anything he hadn't seen. Most of his men were Loyalists: New Jersey Volunteers, Skinner's men, who'd been run off their own farms across the water by the rebels and who could look across the ice, on a clear day, at the steeples of the towns they came from. They hated the rebels the way only neighbors can. They had built a line of small forts, redoubts, along the heights above the watering place on the north shore, with log walls and earth and ditches full of snow.

I spent four nights on that ice.

I'd go out after dark, alone, a few hundred yards from the shore, where the snow on the ice had been blown thin, and lie down flat on a horse blanket with another one over me, and listen. If you've never done it, you can't imagine how loud the world is on a still night on the ice. The cold makes the air heavy and holds the sound down low, the way a lid holds steam, and it carries along the flat for miles. I could hear dogs on the Jersey shore. I could hear a door bang at Elizabethtown Point, two miles off, and a man swear at it. I could hear the ice itself, every few minutes, singing its long note across the bay. And under all of it, I could hear the rebels.

Not much, the first two nights. Sentries calling. A cart. Somebody chopping wood. The ordinary noise of a cold, hungry army's outposts trying to stay alive till morning.

On the third night, the thirteenth, it changed.

There were sleighs. Many sleighs, on the roads behind the Point, coming down out of the hills from the direction of Morristown, one after another, and stopping, and more coming behind. The runners sang on the packed snow of the roads the way they sing on ice, a high hissing whine, and the horses blew, and men talked to the horses, low. Some of the sleighs still had their bells on. Somebody in charge of them didn't know about ice, or didn't think it mattered. I lay there on my blanket with my cheek on the cold and counted. I counted until two in the morning. I stopped counting at three hundred, because after that it was one long noise and I couldn't separate the sleighs in it anymore.

Three hundred sleighs. More. Gathering behind Elizabethtown Point, in the night, in the coldest winter in living memory, where there was nothing on earth for a sleigh to do except cross the ice to Staten Island.

* * *

I went back to the redoubts before dawn and asked for the officer of the guard, and he was a captain of the Jersey Volunteers, a big red-faced man called Rudd with frost in his eyebrows, and he didn't believe a word I said.

I don't blame him, now. Look at it from where he stood. It was four in the morning. He'd been on his feet all night in a log hut that was colder inside than out. And here was a Black man he'd never seen, in a horse blanket, with frost on his face, telling him that he'd lain on the ice and listened and counted three hundred sleighs in the dark, on the other side of two miles of frozen sea. He laughed at me. Then he got angry, because I didn't go away. Then he told me that if I'd been listening to the rebels all night, it was a wonder I hadn't gone over and joined them, and that if I didn't take myself off, he'd have me in the guardhouse for a spy.

I showed him Robinson's paper, with Beckwith's note on it. He held it to the fire to read it. Then he handed it back and said that Captain Beckwith was in New York, and he was here, and he'd send my report up in the morning with the others. And I knew he would. I knew exactly where it would go: into a pile on a table, under a stone, with all the other reports from all the other people who'd seen rebels behind snowdrifts that week. Rudd wasn't a fool. He'd just been told the rebels were coming every night for a fortnight, by every frightened Loyalist on the island, and none of them had come.

I went out of the redoubt and stood in the snow, and thought.

A report from me wasn't going to move anybody. I was nobody. A pair of ears on a horse blanket. What would move them was something they could see.

* * *

So I went back out on the ice, in the gray before sunrise, and I walked toward Jersey.

Not all the way. A mile, or a little more, until I could see the smoke of the outposts on the far shore against the brightening sky. And then I lay down again on my blanket and waited, because I was fairly sure, by then, of what I was waiting for.

You have to think about that army. Washington's men at Morristown. I knew a good deal about them, because deserters had been coming in all winter, and I'd seen them at the lines. They were starving. That isn't a figure of speech. That January the army at Morristown went days at a time without bread or meat, and the snow had cut the roads, and the men were eating bark and boiling their shoes. They had no coats, a lot of them. They'd built their huts in the snow and they were dying in them. And somebody had just ordered three thousand of them to get into sleighs and ride across two miles of ice in the dark, in the coldest night of the coldest winter there had ever been, to attack a line of forts.

Some of them were going to decide they'd rather not.

And a man who deserted that night wouldn't run back up into the hills, into the snow, toward the officers who'd hang him. He'd go the only way left. Across the ice, to us. Tonight, while there was still time to be worth something when he got there. And he'd come out on the ice exactly where I was lying, because it was the shortest way across, and because the ice was thickest there, and because a man running for his life over the sea goes the way the ice tells him to.

They came about an hour after sunrise. Five of them.

I saw them first as black specks against the white, a long way off, moving in a bunch, stopping, moving again. They stopped a great deal. As they came closer I saw why. Three of them had no shoes. They'd wrapped their feet in rags and old blanket tied on with string, and the rags had frozen, and they walked like old men. One had a coat, a real one, blue, with the facings torn off. The others had blankets round their shoulders like women's shawls. They were young. They were all young. The oldest might have been twenty-five.

When they were close enough I stood up, slowly, with my hands open, as I'd stood up in front of the Hessian at Kingsbridge. They stopped dead. One of them made a sound, not a word. For a moment I thought they'd run, back the way they'd come. But there was nowhere to run. They'd used up their running.

"You're coming in?" I said.

The one in the blue coat nodded. His lips were cracked through. He couldn't make them say anything.

"Then you'll want to tell somebody what's coming behind you," I said. "Tonight. Before it comes. That's what'll make them glad to see you." I looked at each of them. "Is it tonight?"

The one in the coat looked at the others. Then he found his voice. It came out cracked and very young.

"Tonight," he said. "All of us. Lord Stirling's taking the whole lot over. Three thousand men. Five hundred sleighs. They told us at evening parade. Tonight, and take the forts in the dark before they know." He swallowed. "We're not going. We're not dying on the ice for a fort."

I believed him. I'd have believed him without the words. I looked at his feet in their frozen rags, and his hands, cracked across the knuckles from cutting logs for huts, and the bones of his face. A man sent over to deceive us would have been fed first, and given shoes. You don't send a liar across the sea barefoot. You need him to get there.

"Come with me," I said. "Walk where I walk. Don't stop. I'll take you to a fire."

It was a mile back to the shore, and it took us most of an hour. The smallest of them, a red-haired boy with nothing at all on his feet but the rags, kept stopping. Not because he was tired. Every so often he'd look down at the ice between his feet as if he'd only just noticed what he was standing on.

Halfway across, the ice sang. The long low note came from somewhere up the Kill and ran at us and under us and away, and all five of them stopped dead, and the red-haired boy went down on his knees and put both hands flat on the ice, as if he could hold it still.

"Tide," I said. "Just the tide, lifting it."

He looked up at me. I don't think he believed me. I'd said it the way the driver had, I realized. For both of us. But I held out my hand, and after a moment he took it, and his hand was so cold it didn't feel like a hand at all. I walked him the rest of the way like that, the way you'd walk a child across a street. He didn't let go till we reached the shore.

The others called him Davy.

* * *

I didn't take them to Rudd.

I took them straight past the redoubt where he was, along the shore, half a mile, to the house above the watering place where the brigadier had his quarters, and I walked them up to the sentry at the door, five starving rebels in frozen rags and me in my horse blanket, and I said, very loudly, so that it would carry into the house through the window that had a light in it, that these five men had deserted from Lord Stirling's army that morning, and that they'd come to tell General Stirling that three thousand men in five hundred sleighs were coming across the ice to attack him that night.

The window opened. A man put his head out. Gray hair, nightcap, no wig, his breath smoking in the cold. Thomas Stirling, brigadier general, in his shirt.

He looked down at the five of them, standing in the snow with their rag feet. He looked at them for a long time. He didn't look at me at all. Then he said, "Bring them in to the fire. Give them something hot. Send for my brigade major," and shut the window.

And by noon every man on Staten Island was inside the redoubts, with the gates shut and the guns loaded and the ditches swept, and sentries doubled all along the heights, and a boat had gone off as far as the ice would let it toward the city to beg for help. Rudd among them. He passed me in the afternoon, carrying cartridges, and looked at me, and I looked at him, and neither of us said a word about it. I've always rather liked him for that.

* * *

They came after dark. Exactly as the boy in the blue coat had said.

I watched from the wall of the biggest redoubt. You could see them coming for the last mile, black on the white under the stars, the sleighs in long dark lines, like ink running across paper. And you could hear them. You could hear them the whole way. The runners singing, and the bells, and the horses, and the men. Three thousand men, coming at what they thought was a sleeping fort in the dark, and you could hear them from the far shore, every one.

They landed and found every gate shut and every gun manned. Lord Stirling looked at the forts from the snow below, and saw what he'd have had to do to take them, and wouldn't. He was a brave man, but he wasn't a murderer. So his three thousand men spent the night in the open, on the hills above the watering place, in the snow, without tents, without fires, most of them, in the coldest night of that whole winter. We watched them from the walls. Nobody fired. There wasn't any point.

They stayed all the next day, looking at us, and another night after it. By then five hundred of them had frostbite. Some of them never walked right again. And on the morning after that they loaded the sleighs with whatever they, and the Jersey farmers who'd followed them over, could steal from the Loyalist farms on the island, which was a good deal, and went back across the ice to Jersey. And the boats that New York sent out to help us all came back, because they couldn't get through.

Almost nobody died. That's the thing I want you to write down, Ms. Barnett. Three thousand men came across the ice to fight, and the fight never happened, and almost nobody on either side died of it. Not a single one of the five boys who came across barefoot that morning. Not a single one of Rudd's men. Not, as far as I ever heard, a single one of Lord Stirling's, though some of them lost their toes.

It's the cleanest win I ever had. There have been two others like it, in all that time, where nobody died. Only two. I'll tell you about them some day.

He stopped and sat back against the porch wall, and closed his eyes for a moment, in the heat, with his face turned up.

It was late in the afternoon. The tractor had gone from the field and the hay lay in long, combed rows all the way to the trees, and the shadows of the rows were getting long. The green coat was still on the chair beside me. At some point while he was talking about the ice, I'd taken it off the chair and put it across my knees. I didn't remember doing that either.

"What happened to them?" I said. "The five."

He opened his eyes.

"They were fed, and given shoes, and questioned for a week," he said. "Then two of them enlisted with Skinner's men and fought for the King for the rest of the war. One went to sea. The boy in the blue coat went to work for a baker in Hanover Square and married the baker's daughter." He smiled. "And one walked back across the ice in February, when it was still hard enough, and rejoined his regiment in Jersey, and told them he'd been lost in a snowstorm for a month. And they believed him. Or they were so short of men by then that they decided to."

"You know all that?"

"I kept track," he said. "I kept track of all five." He picked up his water glass, found it empty, and set it down again. "I've done a great many things in a great many lives that I'm not proud of, Ms. Barnett. You'll hear about some of them, if you keep driving up here. But I've never once regretted those five boys on the ice." He looked out at the hay. "I'd like you to have one like that. Before the rest of it."

I wrote it down. *Session 31. The five. Nobody died.* I underlined *nobody*, which I don't usually do either.

Then I folded the coat over my arm and put the recorders in my bag, and he walked me as far as the top of the steps, slowly, with his hand on the rail, and stood there in the long light while I went down. When I got to the car, I looked back. He was still standing there, looking out over the field toward the east, toward the sea, a hundred and fifty miles away, and I knew without asking that he was hearing the ice.
