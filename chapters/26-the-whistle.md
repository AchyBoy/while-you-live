# Sic Semper

## Chapter 26: The Whistle

On the fourteenth Thursday the field was frozen hard, and he was already in his chair with the notepad on his knee when I came in. There was no tea. The kettle was cold on the stove; I touched it on my way past, out of habit. The top page of the notepad was covered in small writing, which it never was before we started.

"Did you sleep?" I said.

"Some." He did not look up. "I've been awake with this part since four. I'd like to get it said, if you don't mind, before I think about it any more."

I did not mind. I sat down and took out the recorders, and he started talking while I was still untangling the cord.

"I found him in two days," he said. "It wasn't clever. I just knew the country, and I knew who to watch."

* * *

*April 18 to 23, 1865*

I did not look for Booth. Booth was a man with a broken leg lying still in the woods, and a man lying still in the woods cannot be found by looking, not in that country, not by anyone. I looked for Thomas Jones.

Jones lived above the river, near Pope's Creek, on a bluff where he could see the whole width of the Potomac and the Virginia shore beyond it. He was a quiet, thin, careful man in his forties who fished for a living and had spent the whole war doing something else. Everyone in the county knew what. Nobody said it. He had been in prison for it once, early on, and come home, and gone on doing it.

If Booth was being kept anywhere, he was being kept for Jones. And a man being kept alive in the woods has to be fed.

So I watched Jones. I took one of my mother's horses and rode down toward the river on the back roads in my blue coat, the way a Union man on leave might ride out to look at the country, and I found a place on a wooded rise across a field from the lane that led up to his house, and I lay in the leaves there with a piece of bread and my father's old spyglass, and I watched.

The country was full of soldiers. Cavalry went down the river road three and four times a day. Twice a patrol passed so close below me that I could hear the men talking about the reward. A hundred thousand dollars, one of them said, and what he would do with his share, and another one laughed at him. They rode on toward the river. They never once looked up at the rise.

Jones went about his business as if nothing in the world had happened. He fished. He mended a fence. He went to the landing and talked to the men who were watching the river for the army, pleasantly, for a long time. He was, I thought, lying there, the calmest man in Maryland.

And on the second afternoon, a little before dark, he walked out of his lane with a basket over his arm, and turned off the road, and went into the pines.

I followed him as far as I could without being seen, which was not far. The pines on that side of the road were thick, young, close-grown, the kind of thicket where you cannot see ten feet in front of you. I lost him in it almost at once. I stood at the edge of it, very still, and listened.

And from somewhere inside, not far off, I heard a whistle. Three notes. A little rising tune, like a bird that is not quite any bird.

A moment later, from deeper in the pines, somebody whistled it back.

* * *

I stood at the edge of those pines for a long time.

He was in there. A hundred yards from me, perhaps less. Lying on the ground on a blanket with his leg in splints, and Herold beside him, and Jones kneeling to open the basket. I could have walked in. I could have walked in and stood over him. I have thought about what I would have said, many times. I have never once been able to think of anything worth the walk.

And I could have walked out. That was the other thing. I could have walked back to the road and waited for the next patrol and pointed into those pines and said *there,* and he would have been in irons by dark.

I want you to understand what that would have meant, because I understood it, standing there, all at once.

It would have meant Jones. Jones would have been taken, and Samuel Cox, the planter at Rich Hill, whose land that was and whose people had hidden them in those pines, and every man and woman in that part of the county who had carried a letter or a meal or a message that week. And the soldiers would not have stopped there, because they would not have needed to. They would have gone back up the road Booth came down, house by house. The doctor. The tavern. The farm by the swamp where two riders had stopped at the gate at dusk, and where a girl in the hayloft had seen them, and told no one, and a mother had had a lamp lit in the kitchen.

They hanged people, that summer, for a great deal less.

I stood at the edge of the pines and chose. I chose my mother, and Lottie, and a county that had hidden me and nursed me and asked me nothing, over the President of the United States, who was dead, and over the justice that was owed him, which was coming anyway. Booth could not stay in those pines forever. He could not walk. He would have to cross the river, and in Virginia he would be one crippled man among strangers, with every soldier in the Union army looking for him. He was going to be caught. I did not need to be the one who caught him here, on this shore, in the middle of everyone I loved.

But I would be the one who knew where he went.

I turned around and went back to my rise and lay down in the leaves again, and watched Jones's lane, and waited for the night he took them to the river.

* * *

It was Friday. The twenty-first. A week to the night.

A little after nine, I saw Jones come out of the pines leading a horse with a man on it, and another man walking beside, and they went down across the fields toward the bluff, slowly, without a light. I followed them as far as the top of the bluff, on my belly, the last of it.

From there I watched him put them in a boat. A small flat boat, pulled up in the reeds below. I watched Herold help Booth down into it, and Booth sit, and Jones lean down and say something to them both and point out across the water, and push them off. And I watched the boat go out into the dark on the river and turn south and west, toward Virginia, and grow smaller, and go.

Jones stood on the shore a long time after they were gone. Then he walked back up the bluff past where I lay, close enough to touch, and went home.

He never knew I was there. He told the whole story himself, years later, in a book. I have read it. I'm not in it.

* * *

They did not reach Virginia that night. I know that now; the whole country knows it. The tide and the dark took them up a creek on the Maryland side instead, miles off, and they had to hide another day and go again the next night. I did not know that then. I only knew that the boat had gone out on the river on Friday night, and that by Saturday morning, if they had any luck at all, they were in Virginia.

On Saturday and Sunday I watched the army search Maryland.

They were everywhere, and they were nowhere. They were searching the Zekiah Swamp, which Booth had left a week before. They were searching barns in Bryantown. They had questioned Dr. Mudd twice, and were arresting other men all over the county and taking them to Washington. And every hour they searched Maryland was an hour he had in Virginia.

I waited until I was sure he was across. I did not want them on Jones's shore.

On Sunday afternoon I rode into Port Tobacco, in my blue coat.

There was an officer there from Washington with a party of detectives, running the search in that part of the county from the hotel. A major, a provost marshal named O'Beirne. I had heard his name on the roads all week. I went and stood in the hotel yard among the soldiers and the horses, a lame Union private on leave, and waited until one of his detectives came out, and I told him.

I told him that I had been fishing on the Potomac below Pope's Creek on Friday night, and that I had seen a small boat put off from the Maryland shore in the dark, a little after nine, with two men in it. That one of them had been lifted into it by the other, as if he could not walk. That the boat had gone out toward the Virginia side, south and west, and had not come back.

I did not say whose shore. I did not say whose boat, or whose pines, or whose lane. I did not say a single name.

The detective looked at me a long time. He wrote it down. He asked me my regiment, and I gave him one, a Pennsylvania regiment I knew had been in the fighting around Petersburg, and he wrote that down too. He went inside.

I learned afterward, as everyone did, that on Monday morning Major O'Beirne telegraphed the War Department that he believed the men they were looking for had already crossed into Virginia. And that the War Department believed him, and that afternoon put a troop of cavalry on a steamer and sent it down the river. My report had been wrong in the one detail, and right in the one that mattered.

I did not learn that on Sunday. On Sunday, the detective came back out into the hotel yard with another man, and the other man looked at my face, and at my cane, and at my leg, and then at my face again, and said, "What's your name, soldier?"

And I heard, under the question, from somewhere behind me in the yard, one of the soldiers say to another: *a lame one. The clerk said a lame one.*

He stopped.

I had been writing fast. I looked up.

"They arrested you."

"They arrested me." He smiled slightly. "For the first time in that life. Not the last time in all of them."

"For being lame."

"For being lame, and a Marylander, in a coat that did not quite fit, with a story that was too useful." He turned the pencil. "A clever man who is being careful does not walk into a room full of detectives with the one piece of information they need. I knew that. I did it anyway. I wanted them in Virginia. I wanted them out of my county."

I looked at my notes, and then at him.

"You're going to tell me how you got out," I said.

"Somebody got me out," he said. "I've never been entirely sure who."
