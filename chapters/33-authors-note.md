# Sic Semper

## Extra 33: Author's Note

This is a novel, and the people at the heart of it are made up. Ari, Kit Keene and his family, Ned Hurley, Damon, Iola and Freda Barnett, the client and his lawyers: none of them lived. But the history they walk through is as close to the record as I could make it, and the rule I held to was simple. Nothing they do changes anything that really happened. They can only be standing near it.

So, for the curious, here is where the line falls.

**What really happened**

Lincoln really did stand on the parapet at Fort Stevens in July 1864, the only sitting President ever to come under enemy fire in battle.

John Wilkes Booth really did plan to kidnap him and trade him for Confederate prisoners, and the attempt on March 17, 1865 really did fail because Lincoln changed his plans that afternoon and never came down the road.

On April 10, the morning after Appomattox, Lincoln really did ask a crowd at the White House for "Dixie," and told them the Attorney General had ruled it a lawful prize. On April 11 he really did speak from a window about giving the vote to Black men, "the very intelligent, and those who serve our cause as soldiers," and Booth, standing below, is recorded as saying it would be the last speech he ever made.

Almost everything on April 14 is from the record: the bay mare with the white star from Pumphrey's stable and the warning about her halter; Mrs. Surratt's drive to her tavern with the package that held field glasses; Booth's card left at the Kirkwood House for Andrew Johnson; the letter he handed to the actor John Mathews for the newspapers; the Grants leaving town that afternoon, and Julia Grant's memory of a horseman who rode up and stared into their carriage; the empty chair of the President's guard; the laugh line in the third act.

Lewis Powell really did get into Secretary Seward's house by claiming to bring medicine from his doctor, really did beat Frederick Seward with a revolver that misfired, and really did run into the street shouting that he was mad. Seward and his household all lived. George Atzerodt really did take a room above the Vice President's, and really did drink in the bar below, and never went upstairs.

Lincoln really was laid slantwise across a bed too short for him in the Petersen house, and died there at twenty-two minutes past seven the next morning. Thomas Jones really did hide Booth in the pines for days. Booth really was shot in a burning tobacco barn on the Garrett farm, and really did look at his hands at the end and say "Useless, useless."

The trial, Weichmann's and Lloyd's testimony, Powell at Mrs. Surratt's door with a pickaxe, "Before God, sir, I do not know this man," the plea for mercy that President Johnson said he never saw, and the hangings on July 7 with one day's notice: all of it is real. John Surratt really did go on to give a public lecture about the plot, in Rockville, Maryland, in December 1870.

Lincoln really did receive warning letters, a great many of them. A friend remembered him saying he had eighty of them, and his secretaries wrote that he kept them in an envelope marked *Assassination.* The Seikilos song is real too. Its words are carved on a tombstone that was found in Turkey in 1883, and it is the oldest complete song, words and music, that survives anywhere.

**What I invented**

Damon's letter to Lincoln does not exist. No warning naming Booth ever reached the President, as far as anyone knows. The forged note in the dress circle, the lie that sent Atzerodt home, the unsigned letter that freed Kit, and the moment on Tenth Street when two men noticed the doctor's sleeves at the same instant are all mine. So is every word spoken by anyone who did not live.

The record says only that Atzerodt lost his nerve and drank and left. That is still true. I just wondered who might have helped him.
