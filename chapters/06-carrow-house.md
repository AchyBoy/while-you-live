# Sic Semper

## Chapter 6: Carrow House

At noon he stopped and made soup. He would not let me help. It was a thick vegetable soup out of a big dented pot that had clearly made more pots of soup than anyone could count, and he served it with bread from the town bakery and apologized for the bread, which did not need an apology.

We ate at the kitchen table, and he asked me about myself.

He asked the way he did everything, without pushing. Where I had grown up. Whether I liked the city. How long I had been a reporter. What I used to report on. I had not meant to tell him much of anything, but the soup was good and the kitchen was warm, and it had been a long time since anyone had asked me a question and then actually waited for the answer.

So I told him about Carrow House.

I told it badly, the way people tell the worst year of their lives, which is to say all out of order and with too much in it. I told him about the call to the newsroom, and the first meeting in a diner where my source had ordered a grilled cheese and a cup of tomato soup and not touched either one. That became a joke between us, later: he always ordered the grilled cheese and the soup, and he never once ate them. I told him about the laundromat. I told him about that summer, which was the hottest I could remember, and how the air conditioning in the newsroom had broken in July and nobody fixed it until September.

I told him about my source's building, a brick walk-up over a dry cleaner's, with a landlord named Petrakis who complained about everything: the tenants, the pipes, the parking, the city. A woman on the second floor called Petrakis about noise so often that he had stopped answering her. The dry cleaner's steam came up through the floor in the mornings, so the whole building smelled of hot cotton.

And I told him about the Sunday the story ran, and how my source had called me that morning from the phone at the dry cleaner's downstairs, because he never trusted his own, to say he had bought ten copies, and then felt foolish, because who was he going to give them to. After that came the Monday, and the lawsuit. I had told my source to stay away from Carrow that week, to take some sick days until it blew over, and he had said he would. We were supposed to meet on the Wednesday, at the diner, at noon. He didn't come.

"Gone how?"

"Gone. The phone was off. He wasn't at the diner, and he wasn't at home. Petrakis let the police in on the Friday, and the apartment was empty. Well, not empty. His things were there, some of them. But he wasn't. And his cat was shut in the bedroom, half starved, poor thing. Petrakis took it to the shelter and then complained to me for ten minutes about the smell. I remember he wanted to know who was going to pay for the carpet." I shook my head. "And that was it. That was the last anybody saw of him. By the end of the year the paper had printed the correction, and I was out."

"I'm sorry," he said.

"It was a long time ago."

"It doesn't sound like it."

I did not answer that. He took my bowl and filled it again without asking, and set it in front of me, and began to talk about something else entirely: the bakery in town, which had been run by the same family for three generations and was going to close in the spring because none of the grandchildren wanted it.

* * *

After lunch we went back to the living room. I asked him about Ned, the cousin at Point Lookout, and he went back to 1864.

He talked again about the letter from Point Lookout, and how his mother had carried it in her apron pocket all that fall. He talked about what it did to a family to have a son in a place like that, where you knew he was hungry and could do nothing, where every letter might be the last one. He said the men in those camps wrote home as if they would be back by Christmas, even the ones who knew better. Everyone was hoping for the best.

"Ned wrote that he thought often of home," he said. "He said to tell my mother not to worry. You don't write that to people unless you mean to come back to them."

He was looking at the window. My pencil had stopped.

"Then he meant to come home," he said.

I looked up from my notebook. "Ned?"

"No." He turned from the window. "Your man. With the cat."

I did not understand him at first, and then I did.

"A man who is running away takes his cat," he said, gently. "Or he asks someone to look after it. He doesn't shut it in a bedroom and leave. He shut it in there the way you do when you're going out for an hour and don't want it under your feet. He meant to come back that evening."

I laughed. It came out louder than I meant.

"Or he panicked," I said. "People forget things when they're running for their lives."

"He may have panicked later."

"What does that mean?"

"He could have gone out intending to return, and something happened after."

"That doesn't mean something happened to him. He could have changed his mind. He could have decided to leave town."

"Yes," he said. "He could have."

I looked at him. He had agreed with me, but it had not made me feel any better.

"The police knew about the cat," I said. "Petrakis told the whole building. It was in the report."

"What did they make of it?"

"I don't remember. They asked Petrakis when he'd last seen him. They asked me whether my source had said anything about leaving town." I stopped. "I told them he hadn't."

"Had he?"

"No. That's why I told them."

He nodded. I could tell he was waiting for me to go on.

"They thought he'd run because of the lawsuit," I said. "So did the paper. By the end of that week, I'd heard it so many times I was answering their questions as though I knew it too."

"But you didn't."

"I didn't know where he was." I laid my hand over the open notebook. "I thought if he was hiding, he might call me when things settled down."

"And did you expect him to?"

"For a while." I gave a small, embarrassed laugh. "I kept the phone beside my bed. I was furious with him, which made it easier to think he was alive somewhere."

He said nothing.

"I've told that story a hundred times," I said. "The cat was always in it. Petrakis wouldn't let anyone forget the cat. But I never stopped to think about what he'd done before he left the apartment."

"The carpet," he said.

"What about it?"

"Petrakis wanted to know who would pay for it. Was the cat's litter box in the bedroom?"

"I don't know."

"If it was somewhere else, your man shut the cat away from it."

"He might not have been thinking about that."

"He might not." He glanced at my notebook. "Petrakis was thinking about it by Friday. You could ask him what he found."

"I haven't spoken to him since. I think he sold the building."

"Then you could start with the police report."

I had looked at that report once, years ago, for anything that might tell me where my source had gone. I could not remember whether it said a word about the cat beyond where they had found it.

"He was supposed to meet me at noon," I said.

"Perhaps that was where he was going."

I looked toward the window. For years I had remembered the empty booth at the diner, the waitress coming to refill my coffee, the clock over the counter edging past twelve. Now I tried to picture him in his apartment that morning instead, shutting the bedroom door and leaving the cat behind it.

"That doesn't tell us what happened to him."

"No."

"He could have decided not to come."

"Yes."

For a while neither of us spoke. Then I picked up my pencil.

"What did Ned write after that?"

He went on about Ned. I wrote it all down, and did not look at him again for some time.

* * *

On the drive home I tried to remember the name of the cat.

I had been in that apartment. Once, early on, before I knew better than to meet a source at home. The cat had come and sat on my notebook. It had been gray, with white feet. He had told me its name, and I had written half a page of notes that day, and I could see the page in my mind, and I could not see the name.

Nor could I remember whether I had ever asked what happened to it after the shelter.

It did not matter. It had been six years. It was a cat.

At the red light at the bottom of the hill, I took out my notebook and wrote, in the margin of that day's page: *cat, bedroom, door shut. Meant to come back?* And then I left it there, and did not cross it out.
