# Sic Semper

## Chapter 32: The Carpathia

The photocopy sat on the kitchen table all that evening, in its folder, and neither of them touched it.

Freda had seen it when she came in from school. She had asked, and Iola had told her, all of it: the envelope, the gray box nobody had asked for in twenty-two years, the handwriting, *lame in the left leg.* Freda had listened with her chin on her fist, the way she had listened as a little girl to stories at bedtime, and at the end she had said, "So he's not lying."

"I don't know."

"Mom."

"I don't know what he is," Iola said. "That's all I know. That's the most I've ever known about him."

Freda thought about that. "That's kind of a lot," she said, and went back to her chemistry, and her phone lay face up beside the book and buzzed twice, and she read both and laughed at one.

* * *

The payment came at eight o'clock.

Iola heard her phone go and looked at it and did not understand the number at first. Then she did. It was twenty thousand dollars. The note on the transfer said: *With our client's thanks, at the close of the first part.*

The first part.

At a quarter to nine there was a knock at the door.

It was a courier, a young man in a reflective jacket with a motorcycle idling at the curb, holding out an envelope and a tablet for her to sign. She signed. He was gone before she had the door shut.

The envelope was heavy cream paper, the same as the first letter, months ago, on the night of the electric bill. *Aldine, Mercer and Voss.* Inside was a single card, smaller than a postcard. There was no letter with it, no typed note from the lawyers. Only the card, and on the card one line, written by hand, in ink.

It was the same old-fashioned hand that had written *Please ask him what he thinks of 1865* at the bottom of the first letter. She would have known it anywhere. She went and got the first letter out of her file to be sure, and laid them side by side on the kitchen table under the light.

It was the same hand. But it was not the same. The letters were the same shapes, the same careful, schooled, slightly slanting shapes, but they wavered now. The long strokes had a tremor in them, a little shiver, like a line drawn on a moving train. The pen had stopped, here and there, and started again. Whoever had written this had written it slowly, and it had cost him something.

*Please ask him about the Carpathia.*

"What's the Carpathia?" Freda said, from across the table.

Iola was already looking it up.

"A ship," she said. "It was a ship. In 1912." She read, and her voice went quieter. "It was the ship that came for the Titanic. It picked up the survivors from the lifeboats, in the morning. Seven hundred and five of them."

"The Titanic?" Freda put her pencil down. "Like, the Titanic?"

"Like the Titanic."

"Did he ever say anything about the Titanic?"

"No." Iola looked at the card. "He never said anything about it. Not once. Not in seventeen weeks." She thought back, carefully, through every page of every transcript, all of it, the way she had been trained to. "He's never said anything about 1912. He's never talked about anything after 1865, except to say he's lived before."

Freda looked at the card, and at her mother's face.

"Then how does the client know to ask?"

Iola did not answer. She sat at the kitchen table with the two cards side by side under the light, the steady one and the shaking one, and the folder with the photocopy in it at her elbow, and she did not have an answer to that at all.

* * *

On Thursday it snowed a little, for the first time that winter. The Thursday after Christmas had been the only one they had missed in eighteen weeks. The gravel road was white at the edges, and the field beyond the house was white, and the old blue pickup had a clean cap of snow on its roof.

He had the kettle on. He had a fire going. He looked, she thought, tired, more tired than she had seen him, and she wondered for the first time how old he really was in this life, and whether anyone looked after him when she was not there.

She had the folder in her bag. She had decided, on the drive up, that she would show him. That she would lay the photocopy on the table in front of him, the waiter's letter, *I am one of those you spoke of,* and watch his face while he read it, and that whatever his face did would tell her the thing she had not been able to decide in seventeen weeks.

She set out the recorders. She said the date and the time. And then, because it was how every Thursday began, and because it was the client's question and she was being paid to ask it, she took the card out of her notebook first.

"The client sent a question," she said. "Monday night. By hand."

"Read it to me."

She read it.

"'Please ask him about the Carpathia.'"

* * *

He did not move.

That was the first thing. He had been reaching for his tea, and his hand stopped, just short of the cup, and stayed there. The pencil in his other hand stopped on the notepad. His face did not change at all, and that was the second thing, because his face was always changing, always listening, always a little amused, and now it was entirely still, like a pond in the moment after the stone and before the rings.

She waited. She counted without meaning to, the way she had counted on the tape in the autumn. Eleven seconds, that first day, after *1865.*

She got to forty.

Then, very slowly, he drew his hand back from the cup and put it flat on his knee. He looked past her, over her shoulder, at the mantel. At the empty wooden frame with its polished glass, waiting.

"Say it again," he said.

"'Please ask him about the Carpathia.'" She held up the card so he could see it. "It's in the same hand as the first letter. The one at the bottom that said 1865. But it's shakier. Like he's..." She stopped. She did not know what she had been going to say.

He looked at the card for a long time. At the handwriting. At the tremor in the long strokes.

"Ms. Barnett," he said. "Did I ever say that word to you? *Carpathia.* In any session. Even once, in passing?"

"No. I checked. Every transcript."

"I know you did." He was still looking at the card. "I have never said it to anyone. Not in this life. I have never written it down. I have never told a soul where I was in April of 1912." He said it very quietly, almost wonderingly, as if he were checking it against his own memory as he spoke. "Not one living person."

The fire ticked in the stove. Outside, the snow had started again, very fine, drifting past the window without seeming to fall.

"Then how does he know?" Iola said.

He did not answer her. He sat in his chair by the woodstove, an old man in a cardigan buttoned wrong by one button, and looked at the empty frame on the mantel, and for the first time since she had known him, he looked like a man who did not know something.

The folder with the photocopy in it sat in her bag on the floor by her chair, six feet from the notepad on his knee. She did not take it out. She found that she could not. Not today. Not on top of this.

On the notepad, without looking down, his pencil had begun to move again. Going over something already drawn. Around, and around, and around.

"Tell your client," he said at last, and stopped, and began again. "Tell your client yes."

"Yes, you'll tell him?"

"Yes," he said. "I was there." He turned from the mantel and looked at her, and something had come back into his face, something sharp and awake that she had not seen in it since the dress circle at Ford's. "Next Thursday, Ms. Barnett. And bring a warmer coat."

"Why?"

"Because it is very cold," he said, "in the North Atlantic, in April."
