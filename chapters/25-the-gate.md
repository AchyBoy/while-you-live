# Sic Semper

## Chapter 25: The Gate

On the thirteenth visit there was the hardest frost yet, white to the fence line, and the woodstove was already going hard when I came in. He had put a second blanket over the back of my chair. He did not mention it, and neither did I.

"Getting out of Washington," I said, when the recorders were on. "You said it was a story by itself."

"It was a very short story, in the end," he said. "The army took me."

* * *

*April 16 and 17, 1865*

By Sunday morning half the Union army seemed to be going into Southern Maryland. That was where everyone believed Booth had gone, and everyone was right. Cavalry went down the roads in columns, and infantry after them, and wagons after the infantry, carrying feed and rations and tents for the men who would be searching the swamps. The roads south were closed to everyone. Everyone but the army.

I stood at the edge of a wagon yard near the Navy Yard, in my faded blue coat and my forage cap, with my cane, and watched the teamsters loading up for Bryantown.

It is a strange thing, but true, that nobody on this earth is less questioned than a wounded soldier who looks tired. I limped up to the last wagon in the line and asked the teamster, in the flattest voice I could make, whether he was going down past Bryantown, because I'd been given leave to go home and my people were down that way, and I'd walked as far as I could on this leg. He looked at the leg. He looked at the coat. He jerked his head at the back of the wagon, and I climbed up among the sacks of oats, and we went out over the bridge that afternoon in a long column of wagons with cavalry on both sides of us, and nobody looked into the back of that wagon once.

I had left Washington in July under a load of potatoes. I came back into Charles County in April under a load of oats. I thought about that, lying there. I did not think it was funny yet.

* * *

The teamster put me down at a crossroads a mile short of Bryantown, and I walked the rest of the way home across the fields.

Bryantown was full of soldiers. I could see their tents from the ridge, and their horses in the fields, and smoke from their fires. They had come in on Saturday, the day after, a detachment of cavalry, and taken over the village. The tavern was their headquarters. Every farm for miles was being searched.

Ours had been searched on Sunday morning. I learned that on the porch, from my mother, before she had even let go of me. Soldiers had come, an officer and six men, and gone through the house and the barn and the empty cabins, politely enough, and asked whether any strangers had come by, and gone away.

"They didn't ask about you," she said. "Not your name. Nobody's asked your name." She held my face in both her hands again, the way she had in the ditch. "Kit, what are you wearing?"

"A coat," I said. "I'll tell you."

I never did.

She did not ask me anything else. She had her own grief that week, and it was not the President. The schoolmaster's letter about Ned had come to her first, before she sent it on to me, and she had read it on the porch and folded it up and put it in her apron pocket with the others. I could see the corner of it. She had not taken it out since.

* * *

It was Lottie who told me about the gate.

She found me in the barn that evening, where I had gone to be alone, and she sat down on a hay bale across from me with her arms around her knees, the way she had sat when she brought me newspapers in the summer. She was fifteen now. She had grown up in the winter, the way girls do, all at once. She looked at the blue coat and did not ask about it either.

"Somebody came to the gate," she said. "Saturday night."

I kept my face still.

"I was in the hayloft," she said. "Reading. I wasn't supposed to be. It was nearly dark. And two men came up the lane on horses, slowly, from the Bryantown road. They didn't come to the house. They stopped at the gate." She looked at me. "One of them got down to open it. And he fell."

"Fell."

"Fell down. When his foot touched the ground. Like his leg had gone out from under him. And the other one got down and helped him up, and he stood there holding onto the gate. Holding onto it like it was all that was holding him up." She was quiet a moment. "And they looked at the house for a long time. Mama had the lamp lit in the kitchen. And then the one who fell said something to the other one, and the other one got him back up on his horse, and they went away. Not back to the road. Down across the field. Toward the swamp."

"What time?"

"After dark. Seven, maybe." She had thought about it; I could see it in her face. She had been thinking about it for two days. "I didn't tell Mama. I didn't tell the soldiers either. When they came on Sunday and asked if we'd seen any strangers." She looked at me steadily. "I don't know why I didn't."

"I do," I said.

She waited.

"You didn't tell them because you'd already guessed who it was," I said. "Didn't you."

Lottie's face crumpled. She had been holding it together for two days, alone, fifteen years old, with a secret like that. She put her face down on her knees.

"He was so nice to me," she said into her knees. "He said I was a reader. He said I had ink on my finger." And she cried, and I sat beside her on the hay bale in the dark barn and held her, and did not say anything, because there was nothing true I could say that would help.

* * *

When she had gone in, I sat alone in the barn and put it together.

A man who fell when his foot touched the ground. I had watched Booth come down onto the stage of Ford's Theatre on one knee, and get up, and run into the wings, limping. Something had happened to that leg. Two men on horses, riding slowly. On Saturday evening, in the last light, coming up our lane from the direction of Bryantown and the doctor's house on the other side of it.

He had come to our gate. He knew the lane. I had drawn it for him.

And then he had stood at the gate holding onto it, and looked at my mother's lamp in the kitchen window, and not come in.

I have wondered about that ever since. I would like to believe it was the one decent thing he did that week: that he stood at the gate of a family that had been kind to him and understood what it would cost them to take him in, with the army an hour behind, and turned away to spare them. I would like to believe it. I have known men do that. It is possible.

It is more likely that he stood at the gate and saw, from there, what I had seen from the ridge that afternoon: the fires of the cavalry in the fields around Bryantown, a mile away, and understood that the lane he had studied in December now led straight into the arms of the Union army. And turned his horse toward the swamp, because the swamp was the one place in Charles County the cavalry could not ride.

I don't know which. I never will. Most of what I know about John Wilkes Booth is like that.

But I knew where he was going.

The swamp was not a hiding place. The Zekiah was miles long, and wet, and a man with a broken leg could not live in it. It was a road. It ran south, in the dark, all the way to the river country, to the big farms along the Potomac where the landings were and the men who had rowed people across all through the war. And the man who ran those crossings, everyone in the county knew, was a quiet fisherman down by the river named Thomas Jones. He had put more Confederate couriers across that river than anyone alive, and the Union had never managed to stop him for long.

Booth could not cross yet. Every gunboat in the Potomac flotilla was on that river now, up and down, day and night. He would have to be hidden, close to the water, and fed, and kept quiet, and wait for the right night. Somebody would have to hide him who knew the river country and was not afraid.

I sat in the barn and listened to my mother moving in the kitchen, and I knew, as well as I have ever known anything, that John Wilkes Booth was lying in the woods somewhere between our swamp and the Potomac, waiting for Thomas Jones to tell him the river was dark enough.

And I knew those woods.

He stopped.

"Lottie," I said.

"Yes."

"She was fifteen. And she kept that."

"She kept it all her life. I asked her, years later, whether she'd ever told anyone. She said no. She said she'd been waiting for someone to ask her, and nobody ever had." He looked at the window, at the frost going white on the field. "She was the best person I knew in that life. I've never been able to decide whether I did right by her."

I wrote that down. Then I sat looking at it.

"You're going to go and find him," I said. "In the woods."

"I'm going to go and find him," he said. "That's next."
