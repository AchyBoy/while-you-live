# Sic Semper

## Chapter 23: The Tape

He stopped, and did not start again.

My pen was still above the notebook. The last thing on the page was *the sleeves,* and under it a line that ran off into nothing where my hand had stopped. The woodstove had burned down to a red glow. The window had gone blue. I did not know when that had happened.

He was sitting with his hands flat on his knees, the way he had put them there two hours before, looking at the floor between his feet. He was not crying. I had half expected him to, and would have known what to do if he had. People cried in interviews all the time. You waited, and you did not look away, and you did not touch the recorder.

He looked like a man who had carried something up a very long flight of stairs and set it down at the top, and did not yet know whether he could stand up again without it.

I reached over and turned off the recorders. First the phone, then the old one. The two red lights went out.

Never once, in my whole career, had I turned off a recorder before the subject said they were done.

He looked up at the sound of the click, and saw what I had done, and for a moment something moved in his face that I did not have a word for.

"Thank you," he said.

* * *

I stayed another half hour. I did not ask him anything. I made the tea, for the first time, in his kitchen, finding the cups by opening the wrong cupboards first, and brought it to him, and he drank it, and after a while some color came back into his face.

At the door, in the dark, with my bag over my shoulder and my keys in my hand, I said, "Are you going to be all right?"

It was not a question I had ever asked a source.

"I'm always all right," he said. "That's the trouble." He tried to smile, and very nearly did. "Drive carefully. It's late for that road."

* * *

It was nearly eight when I got home.

The house was lit, every window. I sat in the car for a minute in the driveway, looking at it, before I went in.

Freda was asleep at the kitchen table, with her head on her arms on top of a chemistry textbook and her pencil still in her hand. Her phone lay face down beside the book. There was a plate by her elbow with a sandwich on it, cut into triangles, with plastic wrap over it, and a sticky note on the plastic wrap that said *MOM* in capital letters.

I stood in the doorway and looked at her for a long time.

Then I took the blanket off the back of the couch and put it over Freda's shoulders, carefully, so as not to wake her. She stirred and said something that was not a word, and did not wake. I sat down across from her and ate the sandwich, in the bright kitchen, with every light in the house on, and did not turn any of them off.

* * *

That night I did not sleep.

At eleven I woke Freda enough to walk her up to bed, one arm around her, the way I used to when she fell asleep in the car, and she said "you're back" and "how was the guy" and was asleep again before I could answer. At midnight I sat down at the kitchen table with my headphones and my laptop and began typing up the transcript, because the lawyers wanted it by Monday and because I did not know what else to do with myself.

It was very strange, typing it. In the room, I had been inside it. At the kitchen table, with my fingers on the keys, I was outside it again, the way I had always been outside every story, and I could hear it for what it was.

It was too good.

That was the thing I kept coming back to, at two in the morning, and at three. Not the facts. The facts were the easy part; I had checked a hundred facts for him by now, and anyone could learn facts. It was the rest of it. The seven minutes on the bad leg. The boy on the ground in the alley asking why. The doctor at the top of the steps with his sleeves rolled down. A man standing beside him in a thin white jacket who could not get out the one word that mattered.

I had interviewed liars. Good ones, some of them. Liars tell a story from the outside. They know how it ends, so they build toward it. They give you the big moments large and clear, the shot, the leap, the famous words, because those are the parts they have read about.

He had not heard the famous words. He had been in the lobby, with a crowd screaming, and he had not heard them. He had seen the man's face, and it was the happiest he had ever seen it.

A liar would have heard the words.

At three, I stopped typing and looked it up. I searched every way I could think of for a doctor rolling down his sleeves on the steps of the Petersen House. For anyone who had noticed it, or written about it. There was nothing. There were a hundred accounts of that morning and none of them mentioned it. It was exactly the kind of detail nobody could check. Like the color of the thread.

I thought: either he is the best liar I have ever met in my life, or

I did not finish the sentence. I sat at the kitchen table in the lit house and looked at the words on the screen, and I did not finish it.

* * *

At four I finished the transcript and read it through once, the way I always had, for typos. When I got to the part about the sleeves I stopped, and read it twice.

*I saw it, and Damon saw it, at the same moment. I know that, because we said it at the same moment, very low, both of us: "His sleeves."*

I thought about the client, whoever he was, reading this on Monday. Somebody who read carefully. Somebody who had asked about the color of a thread, and the National Hotel, and whether the old man had ever spoken to the waiter again. I wondered what he would make of the sleeves.

I attached the file to an email to Aldine, Mercer and Voss, and wrote *Session 11* in the subject line, and sent it.

Then I sat in the kitchen with every light on until the windows went gray, and it was Friday, and somewhere upstairs Freda's alarm began to go off, and went on going off, the way it did every morning, until I went up the stairs to wake her.
