# Sic Semper

## Chapter 24: The Blue Coat

The payment for Session 11 landed on Monday afternoon.

Iola looked at it on her phone in the parking lot of the grocery store, and then looked again, and then sat in the car with the engine off and the groceries getting warm in the back. It was six thousand dollars. Twice the rate. The note on the transfer, instead of the session number, said: *Our client asks you to take all the time the gentleman needs.*

There was no question with it. There was no email at all.

She called the number at Aldine, Mercer and Voss that afternoon, the one she had called in the beginning. The same woman answered on the second ring. Iola said there must have been a mistake in the payment. The woman said there was no mistake. Iola asked whether the client had a question for the next session. The woman said the client did not, and that he had asked her to say, if Ms. Barnett called, that he had read the transcript with great attention, and that he was grateful.

"Grateful for what?" Iola said.

"He didn't say, Ms. Barnett."

Iola hung up. She sat in the car a while longer.

In eleven sessions the client had never once said anything about the story itself. He had asked his odd, narrow questions, one at a time, like a man picking a lock. He had never said whether he believed it, or liked it, or what it was for. And now he had read the part where an old man and a waiter said *his sleeves* in the same breath on a street in 1865, and he had doubled her money, and he was grateful, and he did not have a question anymore.

She put the car in gear and drove home, and put the groceries away, and did not tell Freda about the money, because she did not know how to explain it.

* * *

She told him on Thursday. She read him the note from her phone.

He listened without moving. When she got to *take all the time the gentleman needs,* he looked at the mantel, at the empty frame with its polished glass, for longer than he had ever looked at it before. His face did not change. But he was quiet for so long that she checked the recorders.

"Well," he said finally. "That's kind of him."

"Is it?"

"I don't know yet." He turned back to her, and some of the usual dryness came back into his voice. "I find I don't know a great many things about your client. I'm not used to that." He picked up his pencil. "The fifteenth of April. Everybody in Washington was looking for someone. I was one of the someones."

* * *

*April 15 to 18, 1865*

I did not go home that morning. I walked.

The city had changed in the hours we had been standing on Tenth Street. The flags from the victory were still up, but somebody had already begun hanging black crepe over them, and by noon the whole of the Avenue was black and white, the bunting of Monday and the mourning of Saturday on the same buildings. The saloons were closed by order. The theaters were closed. Soldiers were everywhere, at every corner, stopping men and asking their names.

And they were arresting people. Anyone. Everyone. A man who had said something careless about the President in a barbershop. A man who looked like Booth. Every actor in Ford's company, and the stagehands, and the Ford brothers who owned the theater. Anyone who had ever been seen with Booth. Hundreds of them, in those first days, until the jails were full. The ones they thought mattered most were put in irons below decks on ironclad ships in the river.

I knew, walking through it, that I was one of the people they wanted. I did not yet know how much.

I found out that afternoon.

The family I lodged with by the Navy Yard met me at the door. Their faces told me before they said a word. Two men had come that morning, detectives, in plain clothes. They had not asked for me by name. They did not have my name. They had asked whether a young man boarded there who walked with a cane. A young Marylander, lame in one leg. They had a description, the detectives said, from the desk clerk at a hotel, of a lame young man who had come in at ten o'clock on Friday night asking for John Wilkes Booth.

The family had told them no. They had lied for me, to men from the War Department, the morning after the President was murdered, because my mother's people had hidden their sons in the war. They told me so, standing in the hall. They were shaking.

"You have to go," the woman of the house said. "Not for your sake. For ours."

She was right.

* * *

I sat on the edge of the bed in my narrow room and went through it, one door at a time, the way I always did. I had perhaps an hour.

What did they have? Not my name. I had kept it out of every mouth that could have given it to them: out of Weichmann's hearing, off Booth's papers. They had a clerk's description. A lame young man, a Maryland voice, a cane, asking for Booth. That was all.

But it was enough. A lame young man was a thing you could see from across a street. I could not hide the leg. I could not walk ten steps without it announcing me. Every soldier on every corner of Washington was looking for Booth and for a lame young Marylander, and I was the second of those, and I could not stop being him.

Unless I was someone else who limped.

Washington in April of 1865 was full of lame young men. That is the thing I saw, sitting there. The hospitals were overflowing. There were thousands of wounded soldiers in that city, on crutches and in slings and on canes, in the streets every day, going to and from the hospitals and the pension offices and the railroad depots. Union soldiers. Nobody looked at them twice. Nobody could. There were too many of them.

A lame young man in a Confederate farm boy's coat, asking after Booth, was a suspect.

A lame young man in Union blue was furniture.

I went down to the rag shops on Seventh Street, where the soldiers sold what they did not need and the dealers sold it on to the next soldier, and for two dollars I bought an army coat that had belonged to somebody bigger than me, faded, with a darned place at the elbow. I looked at the darn for a long moment in the shop. It had been mended with the right thread. I bought a forage cap to go with it. I left my own coat in the shop in part payment, and the man did not ask me why, and I did not tell him.

And I walked out onto Seventh Street in the uniform of the army that had shot me, with my cane, into a city full of soldiers looking for a lame young man, and not one of them looked at me.

* * *

I did not leave the city that day. I could not. The bridges were closed, all of them, and the roads out were stopped by cavalry, and every boat on the river was being searched. And there was something I had to know first.

I had to know where Booth was going.

I knew that he had crossed the Navy Yard Bridge. Everyone knew it by the next day; the sentry, a sergeant, told the officers who questioned him that a man on a bay horse had come across late that night and given his name, and said he was going home, into Maryland, and that he had let him through. And another man, a little after, on another horse. He had let him through too.

A man going south, who says he is going home. I had written that down, on the back of an invoice, in March. They let him go.

So he was in Maryland. On my road. And there was one house on that road that he knew as well as any house in the world, because I had drawn the lane to it for him, at Christmas, on the back of my mother's tobacco receipts, in her kitchen.

I sat in the blue coat in a saloon doorway on Seventh Street, with the city going past me in its black crepe, and understood that John Wilkes Booth, with half the Union army behind him, might at that moment be riding up the lane to my mother's door.

* * *

He stopped.

"You went home," Iola said. It was not a question.

"I went home." He turned the pencil in his fingers. "Getting out of Washington that week is a story by itself. I'll tell you next time. I'm going to tell you how I found him, Ms. Barnett. Booth. I knew where he was going before the army did. That was the one thing that week I got right." He looked at her. "It didn't make any difference to him, in the end." He put the pencil down. "Next week."

She wrote *Seventh Street, rag shops, blue coat,* and then, underneath, because she could not help it, *the right thread.*
