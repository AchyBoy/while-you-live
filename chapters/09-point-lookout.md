# Sic Semper

## Chapter 9: Point Lookout

He walked me out to the car at four o'clock, slowly, with one hand on the porch rail going down the steps. The light was already going. Somebody down the road was burning leaves, and the smoke hung low over the fields and made everything smell like the end of the year.

"How is your daughter?" he asked.

It was the kind of thing people asked. I nearly said *fine,* the way you do. But it had been a long day of listening to him, and he had listened to me at lunch the week before, and I found that I wanted to answer properly.

So I told him. I told him that Freda was good. That she was taking chemistry this year with a teacher named Mr. Ostrowski, who wore a bow tie every single day and had once set his own sleeve on fire in front of the class, and that Freda, who had said all through ninth grade that she hated science, now came home and explained things to me about moles and molecules whether I wanted to hear them or not. Last week it had been a long complaint about the scale at her bench, which read everything about two percent heavy, so that her whole lab came out wrong and she had to write an extra paragraph at the end explaining why. That she had grown two inches since spring and none of her jeans fit. That she had started doing her homework at the kitchen table instead of in her room, which was new, and which I was not complaining about, because it meant I got to see her. That every light in the house was on whenever I came home, like Freda was expecting a party.

"And her phone buzzed all through dinner on Tuesday," I said. "She turned it face down on the table without looking at it. She's been doing that. I thought, well, at least she's not texting at the table. I've been trying to get her to stop that for three years."

He had stopped at the bottom of the steps. He was looking at me in the way he had, the mechanic listening to the engine.

"She knows what it is," he said.

"What?"

"When the phone buzzes. She doesn't need to look, because she already knows what it will say. And she doesn't want to read it." He said it gently. "Somebody is saying things to your daughter that she doesn't want to hear, and she doesn't want to be alone with them. That's why she works at the kitchen table now, where you are. And it's why every light in the house is on when you come home."

I stood very still by the car.

The thing about the lights had been a joke, one line in a long list, the way you say things about your children. I had not thought about it twice. He had picked it out of everything else I said and put it next to the phone.

"You don't know her," I said.

"No," he agreed. "But you do. And you'd noticed already, or you wouldn't have told me about the phone. You'd have told me about the chemistry teacher and stopped there."

I did not answer that. I got into the car. He stayed by the steps while I turned around on the gravel, and I drove away down the road through the smoke.

* * *

I did not say anything to Freda that night.

All the way home, two hours, with the radio off, I had thought about it. I had thought about sitting her down and asking straight out, *Is someone bothering you?* It was easy to picture exactly how that would go. Freda would say *no, Mom,* in the voice that meant the conversation was over, and go to her room, and shut the door, and take her homework with her. And then she would not be at the kitchen table anymore. And whoever it was would have her all to themselves.

So I came in, and said hello, and hung up my coat, and did not look at the phone lying face down beside the chemistry book. Freda had made pasta. There was some left in the pot for me, with a plate on top to keep it warm, which was not a thing Freda had ever done before. I ate it standing at the counter and said it was very good, which it was.

Then I got out my own laptop and sat down at the kitchen table across from my daughter, and went to work.

* * *

Point Lookout was real. I had known it would be.

It was all there, the way everything was always there, a few clicks down: the long peninsula between the Potomac and the Chesapeake, and at the very bottom of it, where the two waters met, a flat, sandy point that was now a state park, with campsites and a fishing pier and a lighthouse. People went there on weekends to catch rockfish. There were photographs of families on the beach.

In 1863 the Union had put a prison camp there. It was built to hold ten thousand men. At times it held nearly twice that. There were no barracks, only tents, on open sand, behind a high board fence, with water on three sides. Men wrote about the heat in summer and the wind off the bay in winter, about the bad water, about the rations. The exchange of prisoners had broken down in 1863, and after that, the men who went in mostly stayed until the war was over, or until they died.

About a mile up the road from the park, beside the highway, there was a tall granite obelisk. The United States government had put it up in 1911. It said that three thousand three hundred and eighty-four Confederate prisoners had died at Point Lookout and been buried there, and that their graves had been moved, more than once, and could no longer be told apart. They were all together now, under the grass by the road. Their names were on bronze plaques around the base.

Other websites gave other numbers, some of them much higher. None of them agreed with each other.

I sat back.

None of it proved anything. That was the first thing I wrote in my notebook, because it was the thing I needed to remember. Everything he had told me about Point Lookout was on the first page of results. A man with ten thousand books and a lot of time could have read all of it in an afternoon. He could have read it forty years ago. He could have built a whole life, a whole Kit Keene, on top of an afternoon's reading, and believed it by now.

But there were records. There were always records. That was the other thing I knew.

The prisoner-of-war records were at the National Archives, and somebody had scanned a great many of them, and a genealogy company had put them online behind a paywall, at twenty-nine ninety-nine a month. A month ago I would not have spent thirty dollars to find out anything at all. For a while I looked at the button. Then I paid it.

I searched for *Carroll,* for the boy Ned had written about, *Jem Carroll died of the flux on Tuesday.* There were more Carrolls at Point Lookout than I could check in a night, from Virginia and North Carolina and Maryland and Georgia, Jameses and Johns and a Jeremiah and several with only an initial. Any of them could be Jem. None of them had to be.

Then I searched for *Keene,* and there were Keenes, too, but no Christopher, and then I stopped and made myself think it through. Kit had never been at Point Lookout. Kit had been shot outside Washington and carried home in a wagon under a load of potatoes. If there had ever been a Christopher Keene, he would not be in these records at all. The one who would be was the cousin.

And I did not know the cousin's last name. Ned had called Kit's mother *Aunt Margaret.* He could have been a Keene, if he was the son of her husband's brother. He could just as easily have been anything else.

I wrote it down: *Ned's last name?* And underneath it, smaller: *Would a real one know? Ask him. Watch how fast he answers.*

* * *

"Is that him?"

Freda had come around the table and was reading over my shoulder. I had not heard her get up.

"Is that who?"

"The guy. Your guy. The Confederate." Freda leaned closer and read the screen. "Point Lookout. Is that where he was?"

"His cousin. He says."

"Oh." Freda read a little more, frowning. "That's so many people." She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Did the cousin die?"

"I don't know yet. He hasn't told me."

"You should ask him." Freda straightened up. "Actually, don't ask him. Let him tell you. You always say that, people tell you more if you wait."

I looked up at her.

"I do say that," I said.

"You say it all the time." Freda went back around the table and sat down and picked up her pencil. The phone lay beside her chemistry book, face down, and it buzzed once, and she did not look at it. "Tell me if the cousin dies, okay?"

"Okay."

We worked on, across the table from each other, until nearly eleven. Freda did her chemistry and I did nothing useful at all. I read about Point Lookout. I read about the exchange of prisoners, and why it had stopped, and who had stopped it. I read the same page about the obelisk twice. The phone buzzed three more times. Freda did not turn it over, and I did not look at it, and neither of us said anything about it, and every light in the house stayed on.

When Freda finally went up to bed, she stopped in the kitchen doorway.

"Night, Mom."

"Night, baby."

"Don't call me baby."

"Night, Freda."

Freda almost smiled, and went up. I sat for a while longer, in the bright kitchen, with the laptop open in front of me and the house quiet. Then I turned off the lights, one by one, all of them, and went up too.
