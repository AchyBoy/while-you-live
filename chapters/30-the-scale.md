# Sic Semper

## Chapter 30: The Scale

It was a strange lunch. It was the first one in sixteen weeks that was not in the middle of anything. 1865 was over. Neither of them knew quite what they were supposed to talk about now, and so, for a while, they did not talk at all. He made grilled cheese, because the soup had run out, and apologized for it, and she said it was the best thing she had eaten all week, which was true.

Then, halfway through the second half of her sandwich, he said, "Your man didn't take his files."

She put the sandwich down.

"Bookbinder," he said. "You told me two things. Weeks apart. You told me that the police found his family photographs still on the living room wall. And two weeks ago you told me that he kept two boxes of papers in the closet by the door, and that the police found no papers at all." He wiped his fingers on a napkin, carefully, one at a time. "You said you always figured he took them with him."

"He must have."

"A man who runs," he said, "and has time to pack, takes his photographs first and his papers if he can carry them. He doesn't leave his mother's face on the wall and his cat shut in a bedroom and remember to take two banker's boxes." He looked at her. "Your man left the photographs on the wall and the cat in the bedroom, and the files were gone. That isn't a man packing to run. That's two different people. One of them lived there, and meant to come home. The other one came in after him, and knew exactly what to take."

"Not after him," Iola said. "He let them in."

He looked at her.

"I called Petrakis. Weeks ago." She told him about Pearl, and the door, and the pressing machine. "He only shut her in when somebody came to the door. That was the rule. Petrakis made it."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he smiled, a little.

"Then it's worse than I said," he said gently. "He knew them."

Iola sat at his kitchen table with half a sandwich in front of her and six years going over in her head like pages in a wind. The report she had read a hundred times. *Framed family photographs on living room wall. Desk.* The closet by the door. The cat.

"The police would have seen that," she said. "Anyone would."

"Anyone who put the two things side by side. They were weeks apart in your telling. I expect they were weeks apart in the police's too." He picked up the plates. "I'm sorry. I've wanted to say that for some time. I didn't know how you'd take it."

"How am I supposed to take it?"

"The way you take everything," he said. "By checking."

* * *

He walked her out to the car at three. The hawk was still over the field, or another one like it.

She had the door open and one foot in when he said, "Oh. And tell Freda something for me."

She looked up.

"Tell her to ask Mr. Ostrowski to weigh something on her scale."

Iola stood there with her hand on the car door.

"What?"

"On her scale. At her bench, in the lab. The one that reads everything two percent heavy." He said it simply, as if it were obvious, standing there with his hands in his cardigan pockets. "You told me about it weeks ago. She complained to you that her whole lab came out wrong because of it, and she had to write an extra paragraph explaining why."

"I don't," Iola said, "I don't understand."

"Every number in her write-up is off by the same two percent. Wrong, in exactly the way nobody else's in that room could be wrong, because nobody else was weighing on that scale. And she wrote a paragraph saying so." He tilted his head. "Now, if somebody else's write-up has the same numbers in it, word for word, the same wrong numbers, then that somebody copied them from Freda. Because their own scale worked. They'd have got the right answers, if they'd done the lab."

She stared at him.

"She won't tell you who," he said. "You told me that. She won't say a word against them, and she goes quiet. That's a girl protecting somebody she cares about, even now, even while they let her take the blame. I'd guess a friend. You'd know which one better than I would." He smiled a little. "She doesn't have to say a word against anyone. She only has to ask her teacher to put something on her scale. The scale will say it for her."

Iola opened her mouth, and closed it again.

"How long have you known?"

"Since last week," he said. "When you told me the other write-up had the same weird numbers in it. Before that it was only a guess, and I don't hand your daughter a guess."

"Last week." Her voice went up. "Why didn't you say?"

"Because last week I had just told you about the Arsenal," he said, "and I sent you home early, and I was not fit to think about anything else. I should have said it at the door anyway." He looked at her. "I'm sorry. I'm saying it now."

She did not know what to say. She got into the car. He shut the door for her, and stepped back, and lifted his hand, the way he always did, as she turned the car around on the gravel.

She drove the whole two hours home with the radio off, saying it over to herself so she would get it exactly right. *Ask Mr. Ostrowski to weigh something on your scale.*

* * *

Freda was at the kitchen table. Every light was on. The phone was face down beside the chemistry book.

Iola hung up her coat. She put her bag on the chair. She did not sit down. She had thought, all the way home, about how to say it, and in the end she just said it.

"Ask Mr. Ostrowski to weigh something on your scale."

Freda looked up.

"What?"

"Your scale. At your bench. The one that's two percent off." Iola sat down across from her. "If the other write-up has your numbers in it, it has your wrong numbers. Their scale worked. They'd have gotten it right if they'd done it themselves. You don't have to say anything about anybody. Just ask him to weigh something."

Freda did not move. Iola watched her face do the thing it did when she was working out a problem, the little frown between her eyebrows that she had had since she was four years old. She watched it get there. She watched Freda's eyes go to the phone, and away, and back.

Then Freda put her pencil down very carefully.

"How did he know about the scale?"

Iola had not expected that. "I told him."

"You told him about my scale."

"I told him about you," Iola said. "I tell him about you every week." And then, because it was true, and because she was tired of not saying true things in her own kitchen: "You're my favorite thing to talk about. You always have been."

Freda looked at her for a long moment. Her face did something complicated, and then something simpler.

"That's so embarrassing," she said. And then she put her head down on her arms on top of the chemistry book, and her shoulders started to shake, and it took Iola a moment to understand that she was crying, and a moment more to get around the table.

* * *

It took four days.

On Monday Freda asked Mr. Ostrowski. On Tuesday he put a hundred-gram weight on her scale in front of her, and it said a hundred and two, and he looked at her write-up, and at the other one, and at the extra paragraph at the end of hers, and he took off his glasses and cleaned them for a long time. On Wednesday there was a meeting in the vice principal's office that Freda was not in and did not tell Iola about. On Thursday, Maddie's mother called Iola, and was very quiet on the phone, and apologized for something she did not quite name, and Iola was kinder to her than she had planned to be.

On Thursday night, Iola came home from the old man's house, and hung up her coat, and put her bag on the chair.

Freda was at the kitchen table, doing her chemistry. The phone was beside the book. It was face up. While Iola watched, it lit up with a message, and Freda glanced at it, and snorted at whatever it said, and typed something back with one thumb, and put it down again. Face up.

Only the kitchen light was on. The rest of the house was dark.

Iola stood in the doorway and looked at her daughter for a long time.

She had taken the job for the money. She would have said so to anyone. Three thousand dollars a session, for an old man's stories, from a client who knew too much. She had meant to do her ten sessions and cash the checks and pay off the car and be done with it.

The ten sessions had been over for seven weeks. She had not stopped. She had told herself it was the double payments, or the story, or the not being able to leave a thing half finished, which had always been her worst habit.

Standing in the doorway, she knew that she would go back next Thursday, and the Thursday after that, for as long as he would have her. And that it was not for the money anymore, and not for the story, and not for the client, whoever he was.

It was for the phone, face up, on her kitchen table.
