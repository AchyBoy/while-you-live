# Sic Semper

## Chapter 20: The Kirkwood

I did not bring the box the next week. I thought about it, standing in the closet with the light on, and left it where it was.

But on the Monday I looked up Petrakis. It took me twenty minutes. He had sold the building four years ago, and the dry cleaner's was a smoothie place now, and he lived with his daughter in a split-level house in Silver Spring. The daughter answered and said he didn't talk to reporters, and then Petrakis took the phone from her and said he would talk to whoever he liked.

He remembered me. He remembered the carpet, and he told me again who had never paid for it, and I let him.

Then I asked about the cat.

"Pearl," he said at once. "Gray, white feet. Nice cat. Stupid, but nice."

Pearl. I wrote it down, and something in my chest let go that I had not known was holding on.

"The police found her shut in the bedroom," I said. "Did he do that often?"

"Only when somebody came up." He said it as if anyone would know. "Every time that door opened, out she went, down the stairs and into the cleaner's, and then I'm the one on my knees under the pressing machine. Twice I did that. After the second time I told him, somebody comes to your door, you put the cat away first. And he did. He was a good tenant, except the rent."

I did not say anything for a moment.

"So if she was shut in," I said, "somebody had come up."

"Somebody always comes up," Petrakis said. "Why? You know where he went?"

"No," I said. "I don't."

I did not tell him about Pearl that Thursday. It had been his idea to ask. I wanted, this once, to be the one who knew what the answer meant.

He was waiting on the porch when I drove up, which he had never done before, in his coat, with the umbrella furled though it was not raining. He did not make small talk. He did not make tea. When I had set out the recorders and said the date and the time, he began before I had finished writing it down, as if he had been saying it to himself all week and was only now letting me hear.

* * *

*April 14, 1865, a quarter to ten*

The Kirkwood House stood at the corner of Twelfth Street and the Avenue. It was not grand, the way the National was. It was a plain, busy, respectable hotel, the kind congressmen stayed in when they did not want to be seen spending money, and it was four blocks from Ford's. I did those four blocks faster than I had walked anywhere since Fort Stevens. My leg did not like it. I did not ask it.

The lobby was bright and nearly empty. A clerk at the desk. Two men in chairs with newspapers. And through an open door on the left, the bar, with the noise of a Friday night coming out of it.

I went to the desk. I made myself slow down before I got there, and lean on the cane a little, and smile. A lame young man with a pleasant face, asking after a friend.

"Has Mr. Booth been in?" I said. "The actor. John Wilkes Booth."

The clerk's face did what everyone's face did at that name. "Mr. Booth? Yes, sir, he was in." He was pleased to know it. "Left his card for Mr. Browning. That's the Vice President's secretary." He lowered his voice, the way people do when they are telling you something about the great. "Wanted to know was Mr. Johnson at home."

So it was true.

I stood at that desk and felt it go through me. Damon had not lied. Booth had been here, asking for Johnson. Everything in the message had held.

I would have asked when in the next breath. I never got to the next breath.

Through the open door of the bar, over the clerk's shoulder, I saw Atzerodt.

* * *

He was at the far end of the bar, alone, in a dark coat, with a glass in front of him that was not his first. His hat was pushed back. His face had the loose, shining look of a man who has been drinking for courage for an hour and has found it does not come from there. As I watched, he leaned across the bar and said something to the bartender, low, and the bartender looked at him oddly and shook his head and moved away to someone else.

I found out later what he had asked. Everyone did. It came out at the trial. He had asked where the Vice President's room was, and what sort of man he was.

And upstairs, I knew, one floor above the Vice President's rooms, was a room Atzerodt had taken that morning in his own name. I did not know then what he had left in it. They found it the next day: a loaded revolver under the pillow, and a long knife between the sheets and the mattress, and Booth's bankbook in the pocket of a coat.

I did not need to know that, standing in the lobby. I only needed to look at him.

I have told you what I thought about Atzerodt the first time I met him: that he would say yes to anything, cheerfully, as long as it was far away, and that if it ever came close, he would find he had somewhere else to be. Looking at him through that door, I saw that I had been right, and also that I had not been right enough. He had not found somewhere else to be. He was sitting forty feet below the Vice President, with a room upstairs and a weapon in it, and he was drinking. A frightened man who drinks can go either way. He can drink himself out of it. Or he can drink until the fear goes quiet for ten minutes, and in those ten minutes he can climb a flight of stairs.

Booth was not in the bar or the lobby. He had asked whether Johnson was in and left a card, the way a man checks a door before he comes back to open it. So either he was coming back, or he was upstairs already. Either way, the man with the room and the knife was sitting in front of me.

So I did the only thing that made sense. I went into the bar.

* * *

Atzerodt saw me in the mirror behind the bar before I reached him, and I saw his face do three things, one after another. Fear, first, because anyone coming toward him that night was something to fear. Then he knew me, and there was relief: Keene, the lame boy, the one who knew the roads. And then, right behind the relief, a terrible hope.

I did not sit down. I leaned on the bar beside him with my cane between us, and I did not look at him. I looked at the bottles, the way two men do who are not together.

"He says leave it," I said.

I said it quietly, the way I had heard Booth say things all winter, as if it were already settled and I were only passing it along.

Atzerodt turned his head and stared at me.

"Leave it," I said again. "It's off. He said to find you and tell you. Go get your horse and go home. Go anywhere. Don't go upstairs, don't go back to your room. Just go."

That was the lie I told him. I had worked it out on the four blocks from Ford's, in case I needed it, the way you work out a move before you know if you'll need to make it. It was not a clever lie. It did not need to be. It only needed to be the thing he most wanted to hear, said by someone he believed was close to Booth. Nobody questions the thing he most wants to hear. I have used that in a great many lives. I am not proud of how often.

He did not question it. I watched it go into him like warmth. His shoulders came down. He let out a breath that he must have been holding since eight o'clock.

"He is not angry?" he said. His accent was always thicker when he drank. "I told him. At the house, tonight. I told him, I did not come for killing. I came for the other thing, the taking. Not this. I told him."

"He's not angry," I said. "Go."

He put money on the bar, too much of it. He picked up his hat. He went out of the bar and through the lobby and out the front doors onto the Avenue without once looking at the staircase, and I stood at the bar and watched him in the mirror until he was gone.

He said afterward, in the statements he gave after they arrested him, that he had refused Booth. That he had never meant to go through with it. I believe that was true, as far as it went. He had refused the moment somebody gave him leave to.

* * *

I stood there for a moment, with my hand on the bar.

The Vice President was asleep upstairs. I learned later that he had gone to bed early that night. He did not know, and he never knew, that a drunk carriage painter with a knife in his room had sat forty feet below him, working up to it, and that a lame boy had sent him home. It is not in any book. It is not in the trial record. The record says only that Atzerodt lost his nerve, and drank, and left. That is true. That is exactly what happened.

It was the only thing I did that night that worked.

And standing there, with the noise of the bar going on around me, I thought: *where is Booth?*

He had sent me here to help with Johnson. The message said he had come to see to it himself. He was not in the bar, and Atzerodt had not seen him, or he would have said. He was not upstairs either. The house was quiet, and a clerk does not sit calmly at his desk under a floor where the Vice President is being killed. If Booth had meant to come, he would have been here before me. He had a fast horse, and I had a cane.

I went back to the desk.

"Mr. Booth," I said to the clerk. "When he left his card. What time was that?"

The clerk thought about it. "This afternoon, sir," he said. "Three o'clock, maybe. Half past. Mr. Browning had it by five." He looked at me, curious now. "Were you to meet him?"

This afternoon.

I have told you that knowing a thing is not the same as knowing it. That was a night I learned it again. It all turned over in me at once, the whole half minute in the dress circle, every sound step of it, like a card turned face up on a table.

Booth had not come to the Kirkwood tonight. Booth had never been coming. The card was six hours old, and it was true, and it had been chosen because it was true, because I would check, and it would hold. Somebody had wanted me out of that dress circle. Out of the seat by the only door. And had found the one message that would move me, and made it true enough to check.

He was not Booth's man. Booth's man would never have sent Booth's backup away from the door. There was only one reason to empty that seat.

He thought I was guarding it.

And he was standing by it now, in his white jacket, against the back wall, alone. And Booth, who had not been at the Kirkwood, was somewhere I had just walked away from.

I looked at the clock over the desk. It was eight minutes past ten.

I went out through the doors onto the Avenue and turned east, toward Tenth Street, and I ran. I had not run since July. I had a cane and a leg with a Union bullet hole through it, and I ran.

He stopped. His hands were shaking. I had never seen them shake.

"I'm going to stop there," he said, "for a minute."

He got up and went into the kitchen, and I heard the tap run, and run, and after a while stop. I sat in the living room with the woodstove ticking and the two red lights on the recorders, and I did not follow him, and I did not turn them off.
