# Sic Semper

## Chapter 15: The Last Speech

We ate lunch without talking much. I could tell he was somewhere else, and I let him be there. When we went back into the living room, he stood at the window for a while with his hands in his cardigan pockets, looking out at the field, before he sat down.

"The next night," he said, "he gave the speech he had promised them."

* * *

*April 11, 1865*

Booth came for me at the lumber yard at six o'clock. He was sober and freshly shaved and very cheerful, and that frightened me more than the brandy had.

"Come and hear the great man," he said. "He's going to tell us what he means to do with us."

Powell was with him, a step behind, the way he always was, too big for his coat, saying nothing. And Herold came along too, trotting to keep up, talking. We walked up to the President's house through streets still hung with flags from the night before, and bunting, and candles in every window. The whole city was lit. People had put candles in every pane of glass they owned, so that the houses seemed to be made of light.

The crowd on the lawn was bigger than the one that morning. It filled the grounds and ran out into the avenue, thousands of people, soldiers and clerks and families, and a great many Black men and women, standing together in a group near the front, dressed in their best, as if for church. Booth steered us through the crowd until we were quite close, under the tall window over the north door, near the front. He wanted to see.

I saw Damon before the speech began.

He was standing with the others near the front, a little to one side, very straight, in a dark coat that was not a waiter's coat. He had come on his own time. He was watching the window the way you watch a door you have been waiting a long time to see open.

He had not seen us yet. I made sure to stand where Booth's shoulder was between us.

* * *

The window opened, and the President appeared at it, and the crowd roared so that you felt it in your chest.

He had a sheaf of papers in his hand. A man stood behind him and a little to one side with a candle, holding it up so that he could read. And every time he finished a page, he let it fall, and a small boy darted out from behind him and caught the pages as they came down, and gathered them up off the floor. His son, somebody near me said. Tad.

He read it. He did not orate. I had heard great speakers, in other lives, men who could make a crowd weep or riot, and I had heard Booth, who could do both before supper. This was not that. It was a tired man in a window reading a careful, difficult paper about Louisiana: how the state had written a new constitution, and whether it should be let back into the Union, and on what terms. Most of the crowd had come for a victory speech. They got a lawyer's argument. I felt them grow restless around me, and shift, and murmur.

Then he came to the part about the vote.

Some people were unhappy, he said, that the new government of Louisiana had not given the vote to the colored man. And then he said, in that same dry, reading voice, that he himself would prefer that it were given now to the very intelligent, and to those who serve our cause as soldiers.

It was one sentence in the middle of a long speech. He did not raise his voice for it. Half the crowd, I think, did not even hear it for what it was.

The group near the front heard it. I watched it go through them. Nobody cheered. It was too big a thing to cheer. A woman put her hand over her mouth. An old man took off his hat and held it against his chest, as if a flag were going by.

And Damon closed his eyes.

That was all he did. He stood very straight in his good coat in the candlelight and closed his eyes, the way a man does when he has been carrying something heavy a long way and has finally been told he can set it down. Not here yet. Not today. But the door was open. For the first time in his life, a President of the United States had stood at a window and said it out loud.

Beside me, Booth said, "That means citizenship."

He said a good deal more than that, in words I will not use in your house. And then he said, not loudly, but in the trained voice that could carry to the back of any theater in America without seeming to try:

"That is the last speech he will ever make."

* * *

I have told you that I was clever in that life. I want you to understand that it did not take cleverness to understand that sentence. A child would have understood it. But I will tell you how it fell into place for me, standing there, because it all fell at once, and it was the worst moment of that life.

The pistol on the table the night before, the kind that fires once. I had carried one in another life. I knew how short its reach is: a few steps, no more. A man who carries one means to walk right up to someone, close enough to touch. The brandy, and the quiet, and the talk about Brutus. *The men who kill a tyrant are honored for a thousand years.* The kidnapping dead, and Booth not grieving for it at all, only waiting, the way an actor waits in the wings. And now this: the last speech he will ever make.

He was not going to take the President anywhere. There was nowhere left to take him. He was going to kill him.

I turned my head and looked at Powell. Powell was looking at Booth. He was not shocked. He was not anything. He was waiting to be told.

And past Powell, through a gap in the crowd that had opened when people shifted to leave, I saw Damon.

His eyes were open again. He was looking straight at us. At Booth, who had just said it, and at Powell beside him. And at me, standing at Booth's elbow, in the coat Booth had bought me, as close to him as a brother.

He had heard it. Booth's voice was made to carry, and it had. I saw on his face that he had heard every word, and I saw him look at me and put it together with everything else he had seen of me since the farm.

Then the crowd closed again, and he was gone.

* * *

I walked back down the avenue with them afterward. I laughed when Herold made a joke. I said good night to Booth at the door of the National and shook his hand.

I did all of that on purpose.

I lay awake all that night in my room by the Navy Yard, and I went through it the way I would go through any problem, one door at a time.

I could go to the police. And say what? That an actor, drunk and angry, had said something ugly about the President in a crowd? Half of Washington had heard somebody say something ugly about the President that week. The Provost Marshal had drawers full of such reports. They would ask me how I knew John Wilkes Booth so well. And the answer to that was a kidnapping plot, and the answer to that was a rope. For me, and for Arnold and O'Laughlen in Baltimore, and for a widow on H Street who had given my mother blankets, and for the people in Charles County who had hidden me, and for my mother. And they would still not have Booth, because Booth had done nothing yet, and he was the most famous young actor in America, and he would laugh at them.

I could run. Go home, and sit on the porch in Bryantown, and wait, and read about it in the newspaper.

Or I could stay exactly where I was. At Booth's elbow. The man he trusted, the man who knew the roads. If he meant to do it, he would need the others, and he would need a time and a place, and he would tell me, because I was one of his. And then I would know when, and where, and who. And I could stop it. Not with the police. Myself. Quietly. The way I had taken back my drawings: so that nobody would ever know anything had happened at all.

It was the cleverest of the three. I lay there until the window went gray and could not find the flaw in it.

There was one. There were two, in fact. The first was that I was not the only clever man watching Booth.

The second was that the other one had just seen me standing at his elbow.

"You stayed," I said. "On purpose. To spy on him."

"To be inside when it happened. Yes."

"That's insane."

"It was the only door that led anywhere," he said. "The police door led to the rope and didn't stop him. The home door led to the newspaper. Only that one went through the room where he would decide." He looked at me. "Tell me which you'd have chosen. Honestly."

I opened my mouth, and closed it again.

Once, six years ago, I had chosen it myself. A source inside Carrow House, who could have walked away and didn't, because inside was the only place you could see what they were doing. I had told him to stay, for those eight months. I had told him it was the only way.

I did not say any of that. I wrote down *April 11,* and underlined it, and after a moment I wrote, underneath, *the other one,* and looked at it, and did not ask.
