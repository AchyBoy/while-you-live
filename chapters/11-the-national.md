# Sic Semper

## Chapter 11: The National

The client's question came on Tuesday, like the first one. It was one line.

*Please ask the gentleman whether he ever went to the National Hotel.*

Iola looked it up before she did anything else. The National had been the grandest hotel in Washington before the war, on Pennsylvania Avenue at Sixth Street, halfway between the White House and the Capitol. Congressmen lived there. So did John Wilkes Booth, whenever he was in the city, in room 228. The building had been torn down in 1942.

So it was not an odd question at all. It was the most natural question in the world. Booth lived there, and the old man said he had been one of Booth's men.

She read it again anyway. It had taken the client a week of silence to ask it.

She had also called the National Archives. You could not simply walk in and ask for microfilm, it turned out. You needed a researcher card, and to get one you had to sit through an orientation, and the next orientation with a free seat was a week from Monday. She put it on the calendar under *Archives. Hurley.* and felt, absurdly, as if the building were stalling her on purpose.

* * *

"The National," he said. "Yes. Many times."

He did not look at the mantel this time. He looked at her, and for a moment she had the feeling that he was deciding something. Then he smiled and settled back.

"Tell your client yes," he said. "And I'll tell you about it. I think it's what he wants to hear."

* * *

*March 1865*

The National Hotel was the kind of place where a farm boy with a cane was invisible, and I was glad of it.

It took up most of a block. It had a great lobby with marble floors and brass spittoons and a gallery around the top where ladies could look down on the men without being looked at, and a barbershop and a telegraph office and a bar that never closed. Half the Congress seemed to live there. So did a good many people who wished the Congress would go home to their states and stay there. It had always been a Southern sort of hotel. Before the war the planters had stayed there when they came to Washington, and some of their friends still did, and in the bar you could hear things said about the President, in low voices, that would have got a man arrested in most of the city.

Booth held court there like a prince. He knew every clerk and every porter by name. He tipped them all too much. When he came down the stairs, people turned to look, and he let them, and pretended not to notice. He had a way of standing in that lobby with one hand on the newel post that I think he had learned on a stage.

I went there to bring him the bridge. Every week or so I would come up Pennsylvania Avenue from the Navy Yard, with my lumber merchant's invoices folded in my coat, and ask for Mr. Booth at the desk, and he would come down and take me into the dining room and order me a dinner I could not have paid for in a month. And while I ate, I told him what the guards had done that week, in a low voice, and he listened and smiled and said *good, good,* and ordered another bottle.

It was on my third or fourth visit that I noticed the bill of fare.

It was a card on the table, the day's dishes written out by hand. Most hotels printed them. The National's were written fresh every morning, and whoever wrote them had the most beautiful hand I had seen in that life: even and flowing and perfectly clear, with long, looping capitals, the kind of writing that was taught in the best schools and hardly anyone ever mastered. I remember that I picked it up and turned it to the light, the way you pick up a good piece of workmanship.

"Isn't it something," Booth said. "The headwaiter says it's one of the new men. He won't tell me which. He says if the guests find out, they'll all want letters written, and he'll never see the man again." He laughed. "I told him I'd pay double for a love letter in that hand. I could use one."

I put the card down. I did not think about it again for some time.

I should have.

* * *

I saw Damon a few minutes later.

He came through the door from the kitchen with a tray on his shoulder, in the white jacket that all the National's waiters wore, and he walked the length of the dining room and set down four plates at a table by the window without a sound, and turned, and saw me.

It was a small thing. Nobody else in that room would have noticed it. His step did not change and his face did not change. But his eyes went to me, and stopped, and I watched them take me in the way they had taken in Lottie's newspaper that afternoon in the kitchen yard: quickly, left to right and down. My cane. My good coat, which Booth had paid for. The ink on my fingers from the lumber merchant's invoices. And the man across the table from me, laughing, with his hand on a bottle of wine.

Then he looked away, and went back through the kitchen door, and was gone.

"Somebody you know?" Booth said. He noticed everything that had to do with faces.

"A man who used to work on our farm."

"Ah." Booth lost interest at once. "Well, he's done better for himself than most of them. The National doesn't take just anybody."

Damon came back to our table twenty minutes later, to clear the plates. Booth was talking, as he always was, and he did not look up. The waiters at the National were not people to him. They were a kind of weather that brought food.

I looked up. I said, quietly, "Damon."

He stopped, with my plate in his hand. For a moment I thought he would not answer me at all. Then he did.

"M-Mister. K-Keene."

It took him a long time. The first sound stuck, and he waited for it with his jaw set, patiently, the way you wait for a stiff door, and then pushed it through. I had not known. On the farm I had never heard him say more than a handful of words at a time. I had thought that was caution, or the habit of that place. Some of it had been. Some of it, I understood now, sitting there, was this.

"Is Miss L-Lottie well?" he said.

It was the last thing I expected him to ask. I think it was the only thing he could have asked that I would have answered without thinking.

"She's well," I said. "She reads too much."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth that on another man would have been the start of a smile. "She alw-ways did."

He did not ask about my mother. He picked up the rest of the plates, and went away with them, and he did not come back to our table again that night. Another waiter brought the coffee.

* * *

I saw him every time I went to the National after that. I made a point of it. I told myself it was only habit, that I always want to see.

He was always busy and always silent, a tall man in a white jacket at the edge of the room, never quite looking at anyone. Guests talked in front of him as if he were a chair. And I saw, because I was watching, that he was listening to every word of it.

He listened most of all to Booth.

I could hardly blame him. Booth was not a quiet man, in the dining room of the National, with a bottle in him. He said things about the President, in front of me and anyone else who happened to be near, that I will not repeat to you. He said them in the beautiful voice he had been trained to use in the largest theaters in America, and he laughed afterward, and the men at the tables around us laughed too, or pretended not to hear. Once, when he said something about what should be done with the President's new friends, the freedmen, Damon was pouring water at the next table. I watched the water stop. Only for a second. Then it went on pouring, and the glass filled, and Damon moved to the next one.

I thought I understood that. A man who had been freed four months before, by a vote of a few hundred in a state that did not want to free him, standing in a room full of men who wished he were not free, and listening to the most famous of them talk. Of course he listened. In his place I would have listened too. I would have remembered every face in that room.

What I did not think about, not once, was what he made of me. The Keene boy, who had come back from the war different, whom he had watched all that summer and fall and not liked what he saw. Sitting now at John Wilkes Booth's own table, in a coat Booth had bought him, talking low, with papers in his pocket.

He had known me for four months on a farm. He had never known me to lie. And he had never, I think, had any reason to believe a single good thing about me.

I did not think about any of that. I was busy watching the bridge.

* * *

He stopped there, and was quiet for long enough that Iola checked the red light on the recorder.

"The menus," she said. "The handwriting."

"Yes?"

"You said you didn't think about it again for some time." She looked at her notes. "When did you?"

"Later," he said. "Later than I should have. You're quicker than I was." He looked down at his notepad, and his pencil moved a little, going over something already drawn. "I was quite a clever young man in that life, Ms. Barnett, and I've told you already how often clever young men are wrong."

"You haven't, actually."

"Haven't I?" He seemed to find that funny. "Then I'll tell you now. Very often. Nearly always about the thing right in front of them."

On the drive home she tried to think of a way to check the handwriting on a bill of fare at a hotel that had been torn down eighty years ago, and could not think of one, and was annoyed with herself for trying.
