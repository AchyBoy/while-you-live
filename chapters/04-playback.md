# Sic Semper

## Chapter 4: Playback

She texted Freda from the end of the gravel road, *Leaving now,* and got back a thumbs up, which in her daughter's language meant *received, and please don't make it a conversation.*

The drive south was slower than the drive north. There was an accident on the highway, and then there was traffic from the accident, and then there was the long stretch of gray afternoon where she found herself driving with the radio off, which she never did, going back over what he had said.

She did not believe him. She wanted to be clear with herself about that.

She had spent six years being very careful about what she believed, because the last time she had believed someone, it had cost her everything she had. She believed in things she could check. So she did what she always did with a story that was too good. She separated it into parts.

The part she could check: Lincoln at Fort Stevens. A man shot beside him. A Confederate army that came to the edge of Washington in July 1864 and then went away.

The part she could not check: that an old man in a cardigan had been lying in a field with a rifle, a few hundred yards away, in somebody else's body.

The first part was history. The second part was either a lie, or a delusion, or something she did not have a word for. And it was not her job to decide which. Her job was to write down what he said, check what could be checked, and cash the checks.

That was what she told herself for the first hour.

For the second hour, she thought about how he had said *I was always glad of that.*

* * *

The house was lit up when she parked out front, every window, the way Freda left it when she was home alone and pretending she didn't mind.

Freda was at the kitchen table with her laptop and a stack of chemistry worksheets, a pencil behind one ear and another in her hand. She looked up when the door opened, and for a second her face did something that looked like relief, and then she remembered she was sixteen and put it away.

"You're late."

"There was an accident on the highway. Not me. Somebody else's."

"I know. I looked at the traffic." Freda turned a page. "So is he crazy?"

Iola hung up her coat. She put the folder on the counter and her bag on the chair and the two recorders side by side on the table, the way she had always set them out after an interview, like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"He's very nice," she said. "He made me tea. He has about ten thousand books."

"That's not a no."

"He says he's lived before."

Freda put her pencil down.

"Like a past life? Like he was Cleopatra?"

"Like a lot of them. He says he remembers them. He says he was a Confederate soldier at the end of the Civil War."

"That's why they want to know about 1865." Freda looked at her with real interest now, which happened so seldom these days that Iola felt it like sunlight. "That's so weird. Do you believe him?"

"No."

"Do you think he believes it?"

Iola thought about that. It was a better question than most editors had ever asked her.

"Yes," she said. "I think he does."

Freda considered this. Then she said, "Well, that's kind of sad," and went back to her chemistry, and Iola loved her so much in that moment that she had to go and look into the refrigerator for a while.

There were two mugs in the sink. Two plates, rinsed, with crumbs on them.

"Did you have someone over?"

"Maddie came by after school. We did the lab write-up." Freda did not look up. "She left like an hour ago."

"That's fine. I was only asking."

"I know you were only asking."

Iola made herself a sandwich she did not want and ate it standing up, and let it go. She had learned that with Freda, the way you learned it with a nervous source: if you pushed, the door shut. If you waited, sometimes, it opened on its own.

It had not opened in a while.

* * *

At ten o'clock her phone buzzed with a notification from her bank.

She looked at it twice. Then she opened the app and looked at it a third time, because she had been checking that balance every morning for six years, and she knew to the dollar what it had said that morning.

The first payment had already landed. Three thousand dollars, from Aldine, Mercer and Voss, with the note *Session 1* and nothing else. Not in thirty days. Not in a check that would take a week to clear. The same afternoon.

She paid the electric bill. Then she sat at the kitchen table with the house quiet around her and Freda's music faintly through the wall, and she put on her headphones and played the recording back from the beginning.

She always did this. It was one of the few habits from the old job she had never broken, even on the gutter guards. You heard things on the tape that you did not hear in the room. In the room you were busy: thinking of the next question, watching the face, making sure the red light was on. On the tape, you were only listening.

She heard her own voice, too bright, saying the date. She heard the woodstove, and the cups, and his laugh when she said *whichever one I trusted.* She heard, all through the first part, a small dry sound she had not noticed at the time, like a mouse in a wall: his pencil, going around and around on the notepad.

Then she heard herself read out the client's question.

*"Please ask him what he thinks of 1865."*

And then nothing.

She checked the time on the playback. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds of the stove ticking and a truck going by on the far road, and, very faintly, the pencil, going around. In the room it had felt like a pause. On the tape it was a silence, the long kind, the kind people leave when a question has gone somewhere deeper than they expected.

*"What I think of it,"* he said at last.

She listened to the whole thing. The rain and the mud. The sun coming out when Lincoln stood to speak. The heat in the field. The man with the missing tooth. The tall man on the wall. The bullet. The army leaving in the dark. His voice did not change much as he told it, and it did not perform. It was the voice of a man describing a place he had lived, not a story he had learned. It went slower in some places than others, and stopped sometimes to go back and correct a small detail, the way people do when they are remembering and not reciting.

Liars did not usually correct themselves. It made them look unsure. The good ones, the ones who had rehearsed, told it smooth.

That proved nothing either, she reminded herself. Some people were very good.

* * *

At eleven, she opened her laptop and started checking.

Lincoln at Fort Stevens was easy. There were books about it, and articles, and what was left of the fort itself, now a small grassy park in Brightwood, twenty minutes from her own front door. She had lived in Washington her whole adult life and never once been. Lincoln had come out to the fort on July 12, 1864, and stood on the parapet under fire, and an army surgeon standing near him had been shot. Everything Tom Hale had said about it was in the record.

That was the problem. Everything he had said about it was in the record. Anyone could have read it.

She found the surgeon's name in an old account: Cornelius Crawford, assistant surgeon, 102nd Pennsylvania. He had been shot in the leg. He had lived, and years later he had given his own account of that afternoon, and drawn a diagram of where everyone had stood on the wall.

*He lived, I think. I was always glad of that.*

She sat back.

It was a small thing. It was a strange thing to say, that was all. If you had read about Fort Stevens in a book, the surgeon was a footnote. The book told you his name and that he was hit, and the book moved on to Lincoln, because the book was about Lincoln. You had to go looking to find out whether the surgeon lived. And if you had gone looking, you would know. You would not say *I think.*

You would only say *I think* if you had never looked it up at all. If the last thing you knew about that man was seeing him fall.

She wrote that down in her notebook, and then she drew a line through it, because it was the kind of thought that had gotten her into trouble before.

Then, after a moment, she wrote it down again underneath.

The inauguration was harder, but not much. It had rained on the morning of March 4, 1865, and the streets had been deep in mud. And a newspaper correspondent standing near the platform had written that as Lincoln rose to speak, the sun broke through the clouds, and that Lincoln himself had noticed it, and told a friend afterward that he was just superstitious enough to take it as a good omen.

Also in the record. Also proof of nothing.

She closed the laptop at one in the morning and sat for a while in the dark kitchen.

On the refrigerator, under a magnet shaped like a strawberry, was the calendar where she kept the whole family's life in pencil: Freda's dentist, the gutter deadline, the car insurance. She took the pencil from the string and wrote in next Thursday's square, in small neat letters, *Hale, 10:00.*

Then, because she was honest with herself on paper, she wrote beside it a small question mark, and looked at it, and left it there.
