# Sic Semper

## Chapter 4: Playback

I texted Freda from the end of the gravel road, *Leaving now,* and got back a thumbs up, which in my daughter's language meant *received, and please don't make it a conversation.*

The drive south was slower than the drive north. There was an accident on the highway, and then there was traffic from the accident, and then there was the long stretch of gray afternoon where I found myself driving with the radio off, which I never did, going back over what he had said.

I did not believe him. I wanted to be clear with myself about that.

I had spent six years being very careful about what I believed, because the last time I had believed someone, it had cost me everything I had. I believed in things I could check. So I did what I always did with a story that was too good. I separated it into parts.

The part I could check: Lincoln at Fort Stevens. A man shot beside him. A Confederate army that came to the edge of Washington in July 1864 and then went away.

The part I could not check: that an old man in a cardigan had been lying in a field with a rifle, a few hundred yards away, in somebody else's body.

The first part was history. The second part was either a lie, or a delusion, or something I did not have a word for. And it was not my job to decide which. My job was to write down what he said, check what could be checked, and cash the checks.

That was what I told myself for the first hour.

For the second hour, I thought about how he had said *I was always glad of that.*

* * *

The house was lit up when I parked out front, every window, the way Freda always left it when she was home alone.

Freda was at the kitchen table with her laptop and a stack of chemistry worksheets, a pencil behind one ear and another in her hand. She looked up when the door opened, and for a second her face did something that looked like relief, and then she seemed to remember she was sixteen, and put it away.

"You're late."

"There was an accident on the highway. Not me. Somebody else's."

"I know. I looked at the traffic." Freda turned a page. "So is he crazy?"

I hung up my coat. I put the folder on the counter and my bag on the chair and the two recorders side by side on the table, the way I had always set them out after an interview, like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"He's very nice," I said. "He made me tea. He has about ten thousand books."

"That's not a no."

"He says he's lived before."

Freda put her pencil down.

"Like a past life? Like he was Cleopatra?"

"Like a lot of them. He says he remembers them. He says he was a Confederate soldier at the end of the Civil War."

"That's why they want to know about 1865." Freda looked at me with real interest now, which happened so seldom these days that I felt it like sunlight. "That's so weird. Do you believe him?"

"No."

"Do you think he believes it?"

I thought about that. It was a better question than most editors had ever asked me.

"Yes," I said. "I think he does."

Freda considered this. Then she said, "Well, that's kind of sad," and went back to her chemistry. It was the longest conversation we had had in weeks, and Freda had started it. I turned and opened the refrigerator, so that my face would not give me away.

There were two mugs in the sink. Two plates, rinsed, with crumbs on them.

"Did you have someone over?"

"Maddie came by after school. She wanted to look at my lab." Freda did not look up. "She left like an hour ago."

"That's fine. I was only asking."

"I know you were only asking."

I made myself a sandwich I did not want and ate it standing up, and let it go. I had learned that with Freda, the way you learned it with a nervous source: if you pushed, the door shut. If you waited, sometimes, it opened on its own.

It had not opened in a while.

* * *

At ten o'clock, clearing the table, I checked my bank balance, the way I did every night.

There was money in it that had not been there that morning. Three thousand dollars, from Aldine, Mercer and Voss, with the note *Session 1* and nothing else. It had come in at four o'clock that afternoon, a couple of hours after I left his house. People who hired writers paid in thirty days, if you were lucky, or sent a check that took a week to clear. This client had paid the same afternoon.

I read it three times. I had checked that balance every day for six years, and I knew to the dollar what it had said that morning.

I paid the electric bill. Then I sat at the kitchen table with the house quiet around me and Freda's music faintly through the wall, and I put on my headphones and played the recording back from the beginning.

I always did this. It was one of the few habits from the old job I had never broken, even on the gutter guards. You heard things on the tape that you did not hear in the room. In the room you were busy: thinking of the next question, watching the face, making sure the red light was on. On the tape, you were only listening.

I heard my own voice, too bright, saying the date. I heard the woodstove, and the cups, and his laugh when I said *whichever one I trusted.* And all through the first part there was a small dry sound I had not noticed at the time, like a mouse in a wall: his pencil, going around and around on the notepad.

Then I heard myself read out the client's question.

*"Please ask him what he thinks of 1865."*

And then nothing.

I checked the time on the playback. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds of the stove ticking and a truck going by on the far road, and, very faintly, the pencil, going around. In the room it had felt like a pause. On the tape it was a silence, the long kind, the kind people leave when a question has gone somewhere deeper than they expected.

*"What I think of it,"* he said at last.

I listened to the whole thing. The rain and the mud. The sun coming out when Lincoln stood to speak. The heat in the field. The bullet. The boy with the missing tooth. The tall man on the wall. The army leaving in the dark. His voice did not change much as he told it, and it did not perform. It was the voice of a man describing a place he had lived, not a story he had learned. It went slower in some places than others, and stopped sometimes to go back and correct a small detail, the way people do when they are remembering and not reciting.

Once I stopped the tape and played a line back. *It was the hottest day I had felt in a hundred years.* It was the kind of thing anybody says about a hot day. He was in his seventies. I wrote it down anyway, with a question mark, and let the tape run on.

Liars did not usually correct themselves. It made them look unsure. The good ones, the ones who had rehearsed, told it smooth.

That proved nothing either, I reminded myself. Some people were very good.

* * *

At eleven, I opened my laptop and started checking.

Lincoln at Fort Stevens was easy. There were books about it, and articles, and what was left of the fort itself, now a small grassy park in Brightwood, twenty minutes from my own front door. I had lived in Washington my whole adult life and never once been. Lincoln had come out to the fort on July 12, 1864, and stood on the parapet under fire, and an army surgeon standing near him had been shot. Everything Tom Hale had said about it was in the record.

That was the problem. Everything he had said about it was in the record. Anyone could have read it.

I found the surgeon's name in an old account: Cornelius Crawford, assistant surgeon, 102nd Pennsylvania. He had been shot in the leg. He had lived, and years later he had given his own account of that afternoon.

*He lived, I think. I was always glad of that.*

I sat back.

It was a small thing. It was a strange thing to say, that was all. If you had read about Fort Stevens in a book, the surgeon was a footnote. The book told you his name and that he was hit, and the book moved on to Lincoln, because the book was about Lincoln. You had to go looking to find out whether the surgeon lived. And if you had gone looking, you would know. You would not say *I think.*

You would only say *I think* if you had never looked it up at all. If the last thing you knew about that man was seeing him fall.

Except that he had known the man was a surgeon, and from Pennsylvania. So he had heard something, somewhere, a soldier's rumor or a line in a paper. He had heard who the man was, and never heard how it ended.

I wrote that down in my notebook, and then I drew a line through it, because it was the kind of thought that had gotten me into trouble before.

Then, after a moment, I wrote it down again underneath.

The inauguration was harder, but not much. It had rained on the morning of March 4, 1865, and the streets had been deep in mud. And a newspaper correspondent standing near the platform had written that as Lincoln rose to speak, the sun broke through the clouds, and that Lincoln himself had noticed it, and told a friend afterward that he was just superstitious enough to take it as a good omen.

Also in the record.

I closed the laptop at one in the morning and sat for a while in the dark kitchen.

On the refrigerator, under a magnet shaped like a strawberry, was the calendar where I kept the whole family's life in pencil: Freda's dentist, the gutter deadline, the car insurance. I took the pencil from the string and wrote in next Thursday's square, in small neat letters, *Hale, 10:00.*

Then, because on paper I never lied to myself, I wrote beside it a small question mark, and looked at it, and left it there.
