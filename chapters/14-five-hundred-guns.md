# Sic Semper

## Chapter 14: Five Hundred Guns

She had thought about it all week. On the eighth Thursday, before she had even taken off her coat, she said, "The drawings."

He was setting down the tray. He stopped with it halfway to the table.

"The roads you drew for Booth," she said. "On the tobacco receipts. In December, in your mother's house. You told me you drew him the way south: the tavern, the swamp, the farms where a man could hide, the landing places on the river. In your own hand." She sat down. "Booth kept everything. The police found Arnold's letter in his trunk. It was in the papers within a week. It's in every book." She looked at him. "So where were your drawings?"

He set the tray down very carefully. Then he smiled, the real one, the one that took ten years off his face.

"You've been waiting all week to ask me that."

"Since Tuesday."

"Good," he said, and sat down. "That's the right question. That's April the tenth."

* * *

*April 1865*

The guns woke me.

It was not yet light. The first one shook the window in its frame and brought me up out of bed with my heart going before I knew where I was, and for a moment I was on the field in front of Fort Stevens again, with the sun on my face and somebody shooting at me. Then the second gun, and the third. And I lay back down in the dark and listened, and counted, and understood.

They were coming from the Navy Yard, a few streets away, and from the forts around the city, and they were not coming at random. Each gun fired, and then the next, at the same slow, even pace, like a clock. Nobody fires like that at an enemy. That is how you fire when there is no enemy left, when you want the whole city to hear it and count.

I counted to a hundred and got up and dressed. The guns went on. By the time I was out in the street, it was full of people in their nightclothes and coats, and a man was shouting that Lee had surrendered, Lee had surrendered to Grant in Virginia yesterday, and it was over.

Five hundred guns, the newspapers said afterward. The Secretary of War had ordered it. Up by the President's house, around Lafayette Square, the windows broke.

* * *

I will not pretend to you that I felt only one thing that morning.

I stood in the street by the Navy Yard, with people I did not know shaking my hand and weeping and passing bottles, and I felt the war end in me like a fever breaking. It was over. Nobody else would lie in a ditch in Maryland with a belt around his leg. Nobody else would write home in pencil from a sand spit and die of the flux a week before the boat. I had wanted that more than I can tell you.

And I felt, under it, cold and clear, the other thing. That the plan was dead. There was no one left to trade prisoners to, and no Confederacy to hold a President for ransom, and no reason on earth for any of it anymore. Booth's great scheme was a pile of paper. And some of the paper was mine.

I stood there in the crowd with the guns going and thought about my drawings.

I had drawn Booth the whole road south, on the backs of my mother's tobacco receipts. The Keene farm was on them. Our lane was on them. Every friendly house between Washington and the river was on them, in my hand, on paper with my mother's name written at the top. And I knew Booth. He kept everything. He kept letters from women he had forgotten and bills he would never pay, all together, in a big leather trunk in room 228 at the National. He was the most careless man with paper I have ever known. He thought that nothing could touch him. Actors often do.

If anything ever went wrong now, anything at all, the first thing anyone would open was that trunk.

I did not know yet what could go wrong. The plan was dead. But I have lived a long time, Ms. Barnett, and I have never once seen a man like Booth simply put a thing down because the reason for it was gone.

* * *

Later that morning the men from the Navy Yard came out of the gates with their band, hundreds of them, workmen in their aprons and shirtsleeves, and started up the Avenue toward the President's house, and half the Navy Yard neighborhood went with them. I went too. I did not decide to. The crowd simply took me, and by the time we reached the President's house there were thousands of us, packed across the lawn and into the street, calling for him.

A window over the door opened, and he appeared at it.

It was the first time I had been near enough to see his face. At Fort Stevens he had been a tall hat on a wall, and at the inauguration a small dark figure on a platform across a sea of mud.

He did not make a speech. He said he would make one later, if they came back, and that he did not want to spoil it by saying anything now. And then he asked the band to play "Dixie." The crowd didn't understand at first. You could feel them not understand. Then he told them, in a high, dry, country voice that carried to the back of that crowd without seeming to try, that he had always thought "Dixie" one of the best tunes he had ever heard, and that the other side had tried to take it for themselves, but that he had put the question to the Attorney General, and had been told it was now our lawful prize. That we had fairly captured it.

And the crowd laughed. Thousands of them. And the band played "Dixie," to the President of the United States, on the morning the war ended, and I stood in the middle of all those Union people and heard it and could not breathe for a moment.

I thought: that is a man who has already forgiven us. And I had spent the winter planning to steal him.

I went to the National that night. The whole city was out. There were bonfires in the streets and rockets going up over the river, and bands on every corner, and I walked through all of it and hardly saw it.

* * *

Booth was in his room. He had been drinking all day. He was not loud, the way he usually was. That was the first thing that frightened me.

He was sitting in the chair by the window with the curtain pulled back, watching the rockets go up over the city, and he did not turn around when I came in. On the table beside him there was a bottle of brandy, most of the way down, and a pistol. It was a small pistol, a single-shot pocket pistol, the kind a gentleman carries, and it was lying there on the table next to the brandy as if it had been set down in the middle of a thought.

I looked at it and understood what kind of pistol it was. The kidnapping had been armed; there were carbines hidden in a ceiling at Surrattsville for the road south. But you cannot hold a carriage with a pistol that fires once, or keep a prisoner quiet with it. It is good for one thing only, at arm's length.

"Kit," he said, without turning. "Have you come to celebrate?"

I told him I had come to see how he was.

"I'm very well," he said. "I'm better than I have ever been." He laughed. It was a bad laugh. "You heard him? This morning? Asking for 'Dixie'? Our song. He's taken our song, Kit, and he's giving it back to us like a present. Like a man gives a bone to a dog."

"He was being kind," I said.

Booth turned around in the chair then and looked at me. I have had a long time to learn men's faces, and I can read most of them. His, that night, I could not read at all. That frightened me more than the pistol.

"Was he," Booth said.

I sat with him until he fell asleep in the chair, a little after midnight, with the rockets still going up and the bottle empty. It took a long time. He talked, and I listened, and none of it was about the kidnapping. It was about Rome, and Caesar, and Brutus, and how the men who kill a tyrant are honored for a thousand years. I told myself it was the brandy. I told myself it was an actor talking about a play he had been in. I have told myself a great many things.

When he was asleep, I opened the trunk.

It was not locked. Of course it was not locked. It was full, the way I had known it would be: shirts, costumes, a wig, a bundle of playbills, a box of cartridges, letters tied in bundles with string. I found my receipts in the second bundle, folded in half, between a bill from a tailor and a letter from a woman in Boston. Eleven of them. I counted them twice, because I had made eleven. The road south, the farm, the lane, the landing places, in my hand, on my mother's paper.

There was another letter in that bundle, from a village outside Baltimore, in a hand I knew. It was signed *Sam.* It was Arnold's, telling Booth to wait, to be careful, to see how Richmond felt first. I held it in my hand for a long moment. I could have taken that too.

I didn't. It was not mine to take. That is what I told myself. The truth is that if I had taken everything, Booth would have noticed the next morning, and known who had been in his room. If I took only my own, eleven slips of paper out of hundreds, he would never know they were gone.

So I left Sam Arnold's letter in Booth's trunk, and I put my eleven receipts inside my shirt, and I closed the lid, and I let myself out.

In the corridor, at the top of the back stairs, a waiter in a white jacket was standing with a tray of empty glasses, as if he had been waiting there for somebody to come out of a room.

He did not say anything. Neither did I. I went past him and down the stairs with my eleven receipts against my chest, and I felt his eyes on my back the whole way down.

* * *

"You burned them," Iola said.

"In the stove at the lumber yard, the next morning. All eleven." He looked at his hands. "It's why you won't find the Keene farm in any book. It's one of the reasons you won't find me."

"And Arnold's letter."

"Was in every newspaper in the country within the week. It helped put him in prison for four years." He met her eyes. "You asked me if I was clever. I was clever that night. I made sure nothing would ever lead back to my mother's house. I did it quickly and quietly and well. And I left a man I had drunk with, who had argued for the sensible road, to take what came."

"You couldn't have taken it. Booth would have known."

"That was my reason," he agreed. "It was a good reason. I've never been sure it was the real one."

She wrote it down. Then she wrote underneath it, smaller: *Pistol. April 10. Check.*
